# 05-07 — The Hidden Workforce: Data Labelers and Content Moderators

**Module:** 5 — Work, Labor, and Economic Power
**Estimated time:** 35 minutes
**Content note:** This lesson refers to the psychological effects of reviewing violent and sexual material. No such material is described.

**In this lesson:** Systems described as automated depend on large numbers of people who label data, rate answers and screen out harmful content. This lesson looks at who they are, what the work gives them and costs them, and who is responsible for their conditions.

## The Workers Who Made ChatGPT Safer

In January 2023 the magazine *TIME* reported that OpenAI had contracted Sama, an outsourcing company with offices in Nairobi, Kenya, to label tens of thousands of passages of text describing violence, hate speech and sexual abuse, including the abuse of children. The labels were used to train systems that filter such content out of ChatGPT's responses. That filtering is part of why ordinary users rarely see it.

According to the reporting, the workers were paid roughly $1.32 to $2 an hour and described lasting psychological harm from reading the material. Sama ended the contract early. It said it paid fair wages for the region and provided support for workers' wellbeing. OpenAI said it took workers' wellbeing seriously.

Separately, former Facebook content moderators in Kenya, also employed through Sama, sued Meta, alleging trauma, poor conditions and interference with their attempts to unionize. In 2024 a Kenyan appeals court ruled that Meta could be sued in Kenya. Kenyan data workers have since formed unions, including the African Content Moderators Union.

## The Human Labor Inside AI

AI depends on people at several points.

| Work | What it involves | Who does it |
|---|---|---|
| Data annotation | Labeling images, text and audio, such as drawing boxes around objects or transcribing speech | Crowdworkers around the world, including in Kenya, the Philippines, India, Venezuela and the U.S. |
| Rating responses | Comparing a model's answers and writing ideal ones, which trains models to be helpful and safe | Increasingly, skilled contractors, including people with doctorates, doctors and lawyers |
| Red-teaming | Trying to get a model to produce harmful output, so the problem can be fixed | Contractors and specialists |
| Content moderation | Reviewing user posts and model outputs for violations of policy | Often outsourced workers, exposed to traumatic material |
| Data creation | Writing prompts, essays and code examples for training | Freelancers |

The researchers Mary Gray and Siddharth Suri call this **ghost work**: labor hidden from view so that a product appears to run by itself. The second row has a technical name, **reinforcement learning from human feedback**, the method by which human ratings teach a model which answers people prefer.

## What the Work Offers

This work is not only exploitation, and the people who do it describe real benefits.

It brings income to places with few good jobs. In Kenya, the Philippines and elsewhere, data work pays in dollars, can be done from home, and requires a computer and an internet connection more than formal credentials. For many young graduates in countries with high youth unemployment, it is one of the few ways into the digital economy. Some data-work companies have presented themselves as offering "impact sourcing," jobs that lift people out of poverty.

It is also flexible, like gig work in Lesson 05-06, and it can build skills that lead to better jobs.

And the work matters. Content moderators protect millions of users, including children, from seeing violent and abusive material. The people who rate model answers are a large part of why AI assistants are useful at all.

## What It Costs Them

The documented problems are serious.

Pay is low and unpredictable. Much of the work is paid per task. Tasks can disappear without notice, and pay is sometimes disputed or withheld. A 2018 study of Amazon Mechanical Turk, a major platform for such work, found median earnings of around $2 an hour.

Moderators can be psychologically harmed. Workers exposed to violent and abusive material have reported symptoms similar to post-traumatic stress disorder. In 2020 Facebook paid $52 million to settle a U.S. class action brought by its content moderators.

The work is insecure. There are rarely benefits or contracts, and accounts can be suspended without explanation.

Workers are monitored, often with the same tools Lesson 05-05 described.

They are often bound by nondisclosure agreements that prevent them from discussing their work, or even naming the companies it serves.

And the work moves to wherever labor is cheapest. One major annotation platform abruptly pulled out of Kenya and several other countries in 2024, leaving workers without income.

By 2025 and 2026 a split had emerged. Leading AI companies increasingly paid experts, such as doctors, lawyers, mathematicians and programmers, well to produce high-quality training data. Meanwhile basic annotation and moderation remained low-paid and precarious. The data workforce now has two tiers.

## Who Is Responsible?

AI developers often say that working conditions are the responsibility of the contractors and subcontractors who employ the workers. Critics apply the reasoning used for supply chains in clothing and electronics.

Under that reasoning, companies have a duty of **supply-chain due diligence**: to know who does the work they depend on and under what conditions, and to address harms. That means standards such as a living wage, psychological support, limits on exposure to traumatic material and freedom to organize. It means being transparent about data-labor practices, for example in the documentation that describes a model. The EU's Corporate Sustainability Due Diligence Directive of 2024 may apply to some large companies' supply chains, though its scope was narrowed in 2025 and 2026. And the Fairwork project at the Oxford Internet Institute rates data-work platforms on fair pay, conditions, contracts, management and representation, creating public pressure.

This is the structural injustice Lesson 01-06 described. Nobody set out to create these conditions. They arise from many companies each making reasonable decisions within the rules. Iris Marion Young's view is that everyone who benefits, including the companies at the top of the chain and arguably the people who use the products, shares some responsibility for changing them.

## Where People Disagree

Is paying $2 an hour exploitative, in a country where that is more than many local jobs pay? One view says no: the work is voluntary and improves on the alternatives, and paying much more would move the work elsewhere. Another says yes: the companies are among the richest in the world, the work can cause lasting harm, and the comparison should be with what the work is worth, not with local poverty.

If AI safety depends on people being exposed to harmful material, what do AI companies owe them? At minimum, many argue, psychological support, limits on exposure and fair pay. Some argue for more, such as long-term health care.

And should the documentation that describes a model disclose how the human labor behind it was sourced and paid? It would make conditions visible. Companies say it would reveal commercially sensitive information.

## Key Terms

- **Ghost work:** Hidden human labor that makes "automated" systems work.
- **Reinforcement learning from human feedback:** Training a model using people's ratings of its answers.
- **Content moderation:** Reviewing content for violations of a platform's policies.
- **Supply-chain due diligence:** Identifying and addressing harms to people in one's supply chain.
