# 05-07 — The Hidden Workforce: Data Labelers and Content Moderators

**Module:** 5 — Work, Labor, and Economic Power
**Estimated time:** 35 minutes
**Content note:** References the psychological effects of reviewing violent and sexual content. No such content is described.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

1. Describe the human labor behind AI: data annotation, RLHF rating, red-teaming and content moderation.
2. Analyze the working conditions and harms documented for these workers.
3. Evaluate responsibility across AI supply chains.

## Opening case: the workers who made ChatGPT safe

In January 2023, *TIME* reported that OpenAI had contracted Sama, an outsourcing firm in Nairobi, Kenya, to label tens of thousands of text snippets describing violence, hate speech and sexual abuse, including child sexual abuse. The labels were used to train classifiers to filter toxic content from ChatGPT.

According to the reporting, workers were paid roughly **$1.32–$2 per hour** and described lasting psychological harm. Sama ended the contract early. Sama said it paid fair wages for the region and provided wellness support. OpenAI said it took workers' well-being seriously.

Separately, former Facebook content moderators in Kenya, also employed through Sama, brought lawsuits against Meta alleging trauma, poor conditions and union-busting. In 2024 a Kenyan appeals court ruled that Meta could be sued in Kenya ⚠ (check the status). Kenyan data workers formed a union and the African Content Moderators Union.

## Core ideas

### The human labor inside AI

| Work | Description | Who does it |
|---|---|---|
| **Data annotation** | Labeling images, text and audio (bounding boxes, categories, transcriptions) | Crowdworkers globally (Kenya, the Philippines, India, Venezuela, the U.S.); platforms like Scale AI/Remotasks, Appen, Amazon Mechanical Turk |
| **RLHF rating** | Comparing model outputs; writing ideal responses | Often skilled contractors, increasingly with domain expertise (PhDs, doctors, lawyers) |
| **Red-teaming** | Trying to elicit harmful outputs | Contractors and specialists |
| **Content moderation** | Reviewing user content and model outputs for policy violations | Outsourced workers; exposure to traumatic material |
| **Data creation** | Writing prompts, essays, code examples for training | Freelancers |
| **Evaluation** | Assessing model quality | Contractors |

Mary Gray and Siddharth Suri call this "**ghost work**": labor deliberately hidden to make automation seem autonomous.

### Documented conditions

- **Low and unpredictable pay:** Piece rates; tasks disappearing without notice; payment disputes. Studies of Mechanical Turk found median earnings around $2 per hour (Hara et al., 2018).
- **Psychological harm:** PTSD-like symptoms among moderators exposed to violent and abusive content. In 2020, Facebook settled a U.S. class action by moderators for $52 million.
- **Precarity:** No benefits, no job security, accounts suspended without explanation.
- **Surveillance:** Keystroke and screen monitoring of annotators (05-04).
- **Secrecy:** NDAs prevent workers from discussing their work or the companies they serve.
- **Shifting geography:** Work moves to wherever labor is cheapest. Remotasks abruptly exited Kenya and other countries in 2024.

### The shift to expert data work

By 2025–2026, frontier labs increasingly paid domain experts (doctors, lawyers, mathematicians, coders) to produce high-quality training data, sometimes at substantial hourly rates. This created a **two-tier data workforce**: well-paid experts and low-paid, precarious annotators and moderators.

### Supply chain responsibility

AI developers often argue that contractors and subcontractors are responsible for working conditions. Critics apply **supply-chain ethics**, as in garments and electronics:

- **Due diligence:** Developers should know who does the work and under what conditions.
- **Standards:** Living wages, psychological support, limits on exposure to traumatic content, freedom of association.
- **Transparency:** Disclose data-labor practices (e.g., in model documentation).
- **Law:** The EU Corporate Sustainability Due Diligence Directive (2024) may apply to large companies' supply chains, including AI data work ⚠ (its scope was narrowed by the 2025–2026 omnibus simplification).
- **Fairwork's** ratings of AI data platforms (Oxford Internet Institute) score platforms on fair pay, conditions, contracts, management and representation.

## Applying the frameworks

- **Justice (structural):** Global inequality makes extremely cheap labor available. Young's structural injustice (01-06) assigns responsibility to all who benefit.
- **Care ethics:** Moderators perform protective care work (shielding users from harm) without care for themselves.
- **Decency:** Would a decent company accept the benefits of this labor without ensuring decent conditions?
- **Explicability:** "Automated" systems that hide human labor mislead users about how AI works.

## Discussion questions

1. If AI safety depends on workers being exposed to harmful content, what obligations do AI companies have to those workers?
2. Is paying $2 per hour in a country where that exceeds the local average wage exploitative? Argue both sides.
3. Should model cards or system cards disclose data-labor practices?
4. You use AI tools daily. What responsibility, if any, do you have for the conditions of the workers who made them?

## Activity: supply chain map (15 minutes)

Map the human labor in one AI product you use. List at least five categories of workers (from chip fabrication to annotation to moderation), where they might be located, and one known or likely risk for each. Mark which ones you'd never have thought about before this lesson.

## Key terms

- **Ghost work:** Hidden human labor powering "automated" systems.
- **RLHF:** Reinforcement learning from human feedback.
- **Content moderation:** Reviewing content for policy compliance.
- **Supply-chain due diligence:** Identifying and addressing harms in one's supply chain.
