# 01-04 — Duties, Rights, and Human Dignity

**Module:** 1 — Ethical Foundations and AI in Society

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

1. Explain deontological ethics, including Kant's two main formulations of the categorical imperative.
2. Distinguish negative and positive rights and apply them to AI cases.
3. Analyze consent, and its limits, as a basis for legitimate data use.

## Opening case: "the image was fake, so no one was harmed"

When the Grok image controversy broke in January 2026, some users defended the sexualized edits on consequentialist grounds: the images were synthetic, no one was physically touched, and the "victims" might never see them.

Most people find this defense not merely weak but morally beside the point. The women and girls depicted didn't consent. Their images were used as raw material for someone else's gratification and, often, to humiliate them publicly. Something is wrong here even if no measurable harm results. This lesson gives that intuition a structure.

## Core ideas

### Deontology: duties before outcomes

Deontological ethics (from Greek *deon*, "duty") holds that some actions are required or forbidden regardless of their consequences. What matters is the nature of the act and the will behind it.

### Kant's categorical imperative

Immanuel Kant (1724–1804) offered several formulations. Two matter most for this course:

1. **Universalizability:** Act only on a maxim (a principle of action) that you could will to become a universal law. *Test:* Could everyone use facial recognition to identify strangers on the street? Then no one could ever be anonymous, a world many would reject.
2. **Humanity:** Treat humanity, in yourself and others, always as an end and never *merely* as a means. *Test:* Using someone's photo to generate sexual imagery without consent treats them merely as a means. They are an object for another's purpose, not a person whose will counts.

The word "merely" matters. We use each other as means all the time: a cashier serves us, and an employer profits from our work. That is permissible when the other person could reasonably consent and their agency is respected.

### Rights

Rights are claims that protect individuals against certain treatment, even for the sake of greater total good.

| Kind | Meaning | AI examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Negative rights** | Rights not to be interfered with | Not to be surveilled without cause; not to be depicted sexually without consent; not to be arrested without probable cause |
| **Positive rights** | Rights to receive something | To an explanation of an adverse decision; to human review; to accessible services |
| **Procedural rights** | Rights about how decisions are made | Notice, hearing, appeal, an impartial decision-maker |

Many AI governance instruments are rights-based. The EU Charter of Fundamental Rights underlies the EU AI Act. GDPR Article 22 limits solely automated decisions with legal or similarly significant effects. The U.S. Constitution's Fourth Amendment and due process clauses shape policing and public-benefit cases.

### Consent and its limits

Consent can transform an action: the same touch is a medical exam or an assault depending on consent. Tech companies rely heavily on consent through terms of service, cookie banners and "I agree" buttons. Consent in AI contexts is weak for several reasons:

- **Uninformed:** Few people read the terms, and those who do can't foresee future uses, such as training a model on their photos.
- **Uncoerced only in name:** Refusing may mean losing access to essential services, a job or a social life.
- **Third parties can't consent:** Bystanders recorded by smart glasses (Lesson 02-08), people in group photos and people discussed in your chat history didn't agree to anything.
- **Inference outruns consent:** You may consent to sharing purchases but not to the model inferring pregnancy or health status (Lesson 02-09).

Contextual integrity (Lesson 02-01) offers an alternative to consent-based privacy.

### Dignity

Human dignity is the idea that persons have inherent worth not reducible to their usefulness. It anchors the German Basic Law, the Universal Declaration of Human Rights and many AI principles. In practice, dignity arguments object to:

- treating people as data points to be sorted without explanation,
- making people perform for an algorithm (for example, emotion-scored video interviews, Lesson 03-04), and
- using likeness, voice or body without permission.

### Weaknesses of deontology

- **Conflicting duties:** Truthfulness vs. preventing harm, or privacy vs. safety. Kant offers little guidance on resolving conflicts.
- **Rigidity:** "Never lie" even to a murderer at the door strikes most people as wrong.
- **Whose rights?** Rights claims can be stretched. Is there a "right to train AI on public data"? A "right to compute"?
- **Thresholds:** Most people accept that a catastrophe would justify overriding some rights. Where is the threshold?

## Applying the frameworks

Compare the two frameworks on the live facial recognition case from 01-03:

| | Consequentialist view | Deontological view |
|---|---|---|
| Core question | Do benefits outweigh harms? | Does scanning everyone respect each person as an end? |
| Key evidence | Arrest and false-alert numbers | Whether people consented; whether there is individualized suspicion |
| Likely verdict | Often permissive | Often restrictive without a warrant-like threshold |

Neither column is "the answer." Good arguments use both.

## Discussion questions

1. Apply the universalizability test to this maxim: "I may train my model on any image publicly visible on the internet." What follows?
2. Is clicking "I agree" ever meaningful consent for AI training? What would meaningful consent look like?
3. Do people have a right to know when they are interacting with an AI? A right to a human decision-maker? Defend one answer, and then argue against it.
4. A deepfake depicts someone saying words they never said, but nobody believes it's real (it's satire). Is anyone wronged?

For each, say whether current law in your jurisdiction recognizes it (one sentence; "unsure" is acceptable).

## Key terms

- **Deontology:** Ethics of duties and constraints.
- **Categorical imperative:** Kant's unconditional moral law.
- **Negative / positive right:** A right against interference / a right to provision.
- **Procedural right:** A right concerning how a decision is made.
- **Informed consent:** Agreement given with understanding, voluntarily and with capacity.
