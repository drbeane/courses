# 01-04 — Duties, Rights, and Human Dignity

**Module:** 1 — Ethical Foundations and AI in Society
**Estimated time:** 35 minutes
**Content note:** Brief reference to non-consensual intimate imagery.

**In this lesson:** Some things seem wrong even when nobody can point to the damage. This lesson covers the ethics of duties and rights, which explains that reaction, and looks at why consent, the tech industry's favorite answer, is weaker than it appears.

## "The Image Was Fake, So No One Was Harmed"

When the Grok image controversy broke in January 2026, some users defended the sexualized edits of real women's photos with an argument about results. The images were synthetic. Nobody was physically touched. The women might never see them. Where, they asked, was the harm?

Most people find this defense worse than weak. It seems to miss the point. The women and girls in those images did not agree to any of it. Their photos were taken as raw material for someone else's gratification and, often, for their public humiliation. That is wrong even if no measurable harm ever follows, and even if the person never finds out.

The last lesson ended with a problem for consequentialism: some acts seem wrong whatever the total comes to. This lesson presents the tradition that starts from that thought.

## Duties Before Outcomes

**Deontology**, from the Greek word for duty, holds that some actions are required or forbidden in themselves, whatever their results. What matters is what kind of act it is and the intention behind it. On this view, the person who made a sexualized image of a stranger did wrong at the moment of making it. The wrong does not wait for consequences.

The most influential deontologist is the eighteenth-century philosopher Immanuel Kant. He argued that morality rests on one unconditional principle, which he called the **categorical imperative**, and he stated it in several ways. Two of them are useful throughout this course.

The first is a test of universality. Act only on a principle that you could accept everyone acting on. Suppose my principle is "I may use facial recognition to identify any stranger I see on the street." If everyone acted on it, no one could ever be anonymous in public again. That is a world most people, including me, would not choose. The principle fails its own test. I was making an exception of myself.

The second is about how we treat people. Treat humanity, in yourself and in others, always as an end and never merely as a means. To treat someone as an end is to recognize that they have purposes of their own that count. Using a person's photo to generate sexual imagery without her agreement treats her merely as a means: as an object for someone else's purpose, with no say of her own.

The word "merely" is doing real work in that sentence. We use each other as means all the time. A cashier serves us and an employer profits from our labor. That is acceptable when the other person could reasonably agree to the arrangement and their own choices are respected. The wrong lies in using someone in a way they have not agreed to and could not be expected to.

## Why It Persuades

Deontology explains several convictions that a pure tally of results cannot.

It explains why the "no one was harmed" defense fails. It explains why people think a promise should be kept even when breaking it would be slightly more convenient for everyone. And it explains why we do not think an innocent person may be sacrificed for the benefit of a crowd.

It also gives every individual a standing that does not depend on the arithmetic. In a consequentialist sum, one person's serious loss can always be outweighed by small gains to enough other people. Deontology says there are things you may not do to that one person at all. For people on the receiving end of powerful systems, that protection matters.

## Rights

The practical language of deontology is rights. A right is a claim that protects a person against certain treatment, even treatment that would raise the total good.

Three kinds come up constantly in AI cases. A **negative right** is a right not to be interfered with: not to be watched without cause, not to be depicted sexually without consent, not to be arrested without good reason. A **positive right** is a right to receive something: an explanation of a decision that went against you, a human review, a service you can actually use. A **procedural right** concerns how a decision is made: notice that it is happening, a chance to be heard, a way to appeal, and a decision-maker who is not biased.

Much of the law that governs AI is written in these terms. The EU's Charter of Fundamental Rights underlies the EU AI Act. Article 22 of the EU's data protection law, the GDPR, limits decisions made solely by automated means when they have legal or similarly serious effects on a person. In the United States, the Fourth Amendment and the constitutional guarantee of due process shape cases about policing and public benefits. When you analyze a system in A2, you will be asked which rights it touches.

## Consent, and Why It Is Weaker Than It Looks

Consent can change the moral character of an act completely. The same touch is a medical examination or an assault depending on whether the patient agreed. So it is natural that the technology industry leans on consent to justify what it does with people's data. You clicked "I agree."

For consent to do that moral work, though, it has to be **informed consent**: given with understanding, freely, by someone able to give it. In AI settings it usually falls short in four ways.

It is rarely informed. Few people read the terms. Those who do cannot foresee uses that did not exist when they agreed, such as training an image model on photos they posted ten years ago.

It is often free in name only. If refusing means losing access to a job application, a bank account or the place where your friends talk, "you can always say no" is not much of a choice.

Other people cannot consent for themselves through you. The bystander recorded by someone's smart glasses agreed to nothing. Neither did the friends in your group photo or the people you mention in your chats.

And inference goes further than consent. You may have agreed to share your purchases. You did not agree to a model working out from them that you are pregnant or ill.

Module 2 introduces an account of privacy, contextual integrity, that was developed in part because consent carries so little weight in practice.

## Dignity

Behind rights stands the idea of **human dignity**: that persons have a worth that is not reducible to how useful they are. It is written into the Universal Declaration of Human Rights and the German constitution, and it appears in many statements of AI principles.

In AI debates, an appeal to dignity usually objects to one of three things: sorting people as data points with no explanation, making people perform for an algorithm, as in video interviews scored for emotion, and using someone's likeness, voice or body without permission. The Grok case is the third. None of these objections depends on showing a measurable loss. The claim is that the person was not treated as a person.

## Where It Breaks

Deontology has serious weaknesses of its own.

Duties conflict. Truthfulness can pull against preventing harm, and privacy against safety. A list of duties does not tell you which one gives way, and Kant offers little help.

It can be rigid to the point of absurdity. Kant held that lying is wrong even to a murderer who asks where your friend is hiding. Nearly everyone thinks that conclusion is mistaken, and it suggests that results cannot be ignored altogether.

Rights can be stretched. Is there a right to train AI on anything published online? A right to computing power? When every interest is described as a right, the word stops settling anything.

And most people accept a threshold. If the stakes were high enough, a real catastrophe, many would agree that some rights could be overridden. But once you admit that, you are weighing results again, and you owe an account of where the threshold is.

There is also a cost that defenders of rights should state plainly. A right is a limit on what may be done for others' benefit. Requiring individual suspicion before the police may scan a face will leave some crimes unsolved. A rights argument should say so and explain why the protection is worth that price.

## Two Views of the Same Cameras

Return to the facial recognition cameras from Lesson 01-03. A consequentialist asks whether the benefits outweigh the harms, looks at arrest numbers and false alerts, and will often conclude that the cameras are justified. A deontologist asks a different question: does scanning every passer-by respect each of them as a person, when none has consented and there is no reason to suspect any one of them? The evidence that matters is different too. It is whether anyone agreed and whether there was suspicion of a particular individual. That view will often conclude that the cameras should not be used without something like a warrant.

Neither is simply the answer. The consequentialist is right that the victims of crime count. The deontologist is right that the passers-by are not just inputs. A good argument about the cameras uses both, and says which consideration it treats as weightier and why.

## Check Your Understanding

**1. What does it mean to treat a person "merely as a means"?**

:::answer Answer
To use them for someone else's purpose in a way they have not agreed to and could not reasonably be expected to agree to, giving no weight to their own choices.
:::

**2. Give an example of a negative right, a positive right and a procedural right in an AI setting.**

:::answer Answer
Negative: not to be surveilled without cause. Positive: to receive an explanation of an adverse decision. Procedural: to be able to appeal the decision. Other examples from the lesson are also correct.
:::

**3. Name two reasons consent is weak as a justification for using people's data in AI.**

:::answer Answer
Any two of these: people cannot foresee future uses, so it is not informed; refusing may mean losing something essential, so it is not free; third parties and bystanders never consented; inferences go beyond what was agreed to.
:::

**4. What is one serious objection to deontology?**

:::answer Answer
Any of these: duties can conflict with no clear way to rank them; strict rules give absurd results in extreme cases; rights claims can be stretched to cover almost anything; most people accept that a great enough catastrophe would justify overriding a right.
:::

## Key Terms

- **Deontology:** The ethics of duties and constraints, on which some acts are required or forbidden whatever their results.
- **Categorical imperative:** Kant's unconditional moral principle, stated as a test of universality and as a requirement to treat persons as ends.
- **Negative right:** A right not to be interfered with.
- **Positive right:** A right to be provided with something.
- **Procedural right:** A right concerning how a decision is made.
- **Informed consent:** Agreement given with understanding, freely, by someone able to give it.
- **Human dignity:** The inherent worth of persons, which does not depend on their usefulness.
