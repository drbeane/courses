## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

1. Describe how risk assessment instruments (RAIs) are used in pretrial release, sentencing and parole.
2. Analyze *State v. Loomis* and the due-process questions raised by proprietary algorithms.
3. Weigh the argument that algorithms reduce, rather than increase, bias relative to human judges.

## Opening case: *State v. Loomis* (Wisconsin, 2016)

Eric Loomis pleaded guilty to charges related to a drive-by shooting. At sentencing, the judge considered a COMPAS risk report that classified him as high risk and sentenced him to six years. Loomis argued that using COMPAS violated his due-process rights, because:

1. its methodology was a trade secret that he couldn't challenge,
2. it used group data, which violated his right to an individualized sentence, and
3. it considered gender.

The Wisconsin Supreme Court **upheld** the sentence. Courts may consider COMPAS, it held, but not as the determinative factor, and pre-sentence reports must include written warnings about its limitations, including that it is proprietary, based on group data, and not validated on a Wisconsin population. The U.S. Supreme Court declined to hear the case in 2017.

## Core ideas

### Where RAIs are used

| Stage | Decision | Common tools |
|---|---|---|
| **Pretrial** | Release, bail or detention | Public Safety Assessment (PSA), VPRAI, ORAS-PAT |
| **Sentencing** | Length and type of sentence | COMPAS, LSI-R |
| **Corrections** | Program placement; security level | Various |
| **Parole** | Release from prison | PATTERN (federal, under the First Step Act), COMPAS |

Most RAIs are not complex ML. Many are point-based checklists or logistic regressions using a handful of factors, such as age, prior convictions and prior failures to appear. The ethical issues don't depend on model complexity.

### The case for RAIs

- **Human judges are biased and inconsistent.** Studies find disparities by race, judge mood and even time of day. A transparent, consistent tool could reduce arbitrariness.
- **Decarceration:** Pretrial reform efforts (e.g., New Jersey's 2017 bail reform with the PSA) used RAIs to release more low-risk people, and New Jersey's pretrial jail population fell substantially.
- **Simulations:** Kleinberg et al. (2018), using NYC data, estimated that algorithmic release decisions could reduce crime by up to about 25% with no change in jailing rates, or cut jailing by up to about 40% with no increase in crime, while also reducing racial disparities.

### The case against

- **Labels reflect policing:** "Recidivism" is measured as re-arrest, so communities that are policed more look riskier (03-01, 03-08).
- **Error rates differ:** As in ProPublica's COMPAS analysis (03-02), Black defendants who don't re-offend are more often labeled high risk.
- **Group-based prediction vs. individual justice:** Punishing someone for what people like them did offends the principle that punishment should be for one's own acts.
- **Opacity and trade secrets:** Defendants can't examine proprietary tools.
- **Automation bias and selective override:** Judges may follow high-risk scores but override low-risk ones, producing *more* detention. Studies of Kentucky's RAI (Stevenson, 2018) found judges deviated from recommendations in ways that reduced the reform's benefits and widened some disparities.
- **Legitimacy:** Who chose to delegate liberty decisions to an algorithm, and through what democratic process?

### Validity

Most RAIs have only moderate predictive accuracy. The AUC for many common tools is around 0.65–0.72. Dressel & Farid (2018) found that people recruited online with no criminal justice expertise predicted recidivism about as accurately as COMPAS, and a simple two-variable model (age and number of priors) matched COMPAS's accuracy. This suggests the tool's apparent sophistication added little.

### The 2026 landscape ⚠

Generative AI is entering courts in new ways: drafting, summarization, legal research and transcript analysis. In its Spring 2026 issue, the ABA's Criminal Justice magazine surveyed these uses alongside continuing debates over RAIs. Some states have rules requiring disclosure of AI use in filings (see 04-01 on hallucinated citations).

## Applying the frameworks

- **Deontology:** Can it be just to detain a person based on what statistically similar people did?
- **Consequences:** RAIs might reduce jail populations, or entrench disparities. The answer depends on implementation.
- **Justice:** Procedural justice requires the ability to challenge evidence. Distributive justice asks who bears the false positives.
- **Virtue:** What does practical wisdom look like for a judge receiving an algorithmic score?

## Discussion questions

1. Should risk assessment tools be used in sentencing at all? In pretrial release? What's the ethical difference?
2. If an RAI is less biased than the average judge but still produces disparities, should it be used?
3. *Loomis* allowed the tool with warnings. Do warnings work, given automation bias?
4. Should defendants have a right to examine the source code and training data of any tool used against them?

4. transparency requirements, and
5. how judicial overrides will be tracked.

## Key terms

- **Risk assessment instrument (RAI):** A tool estimating the likelihood of future events (failure to appear, re-arrest).
- **Pretrial detention:** Holding someone before trial.
- **AUC:** Area under the ROC curve; 0.5 is chance and 1.0 is perfect.
- **Override:** A judge's decision contrary to the tool's recommendation.
- **Individualized sentencing:** The principle that sentences should reflect the individual case.
