# 03-06 — Insurance, Housing, and Tenant Screening

**Module:** 3 — Algorithmic Bias in Consequential Decisions
**Estimated time:** 35 minutes

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

1. Explain how algorithmic tenant screening works and why it can produce discriminatory outcomes.
2. Analyze the ethics of risk-based pricing in insurance when predictive features correlate with protected traits.
3. Distinguish "actuarially fair" from "socially fair" and explain why the distinction matters.

## Opening case: the score that ignored the voucher

Mary Louis, a Black woman in Massachusetts, applied for an apartment in 2021 with a housing voucher that would cover most of the rent and a 16-year record of paying rent on time. She was rejected based on a "SafeRent Score" from SafeRent Solutions' screening algorithm. The score weighed credit history and non-rental debts heavily, and didn't account for the voucher's guaranteed payments.

She and other plaintiffs sued under the Fair Housing Act, alleging the scores disproportionately disadvantaged Black and Hispanic voucher holders. In November 2024, SafeRent agreed to a **$2.3 million settlement** and to stop using its scoring model for applicants with housing vouchers in Massachusetts unless the model is validated by fair-housing experts. SafeRent did not admit wrongdoing. The DOJ and HUD had filed a statement of interest supporting the plaintiffs' theory that screening companies can be liable.

## Core ideas

### Tenant screening

Landlords increasingly rely on automated reports and scores combining:

- credit reports,
- eviction court records,
- criminal records, and
- identity verification.

**Documented problems:**

- **Eviction filings vs. judgments:** Many reports list *filings*, including dismissed cases or cases where the tenant won. Filings disproportionately affect Black women (Desmond's research on eviction).
- **Criminal records:** HUD's 2016 guidance warned that blanket criminal-record bans can violate the Fair Housing Act through disparate impact. ⚠ Check the status of HUD guidance after 2025 changes.
- **Mismatched records:** Name-matching errors attach other people's records to applicants, more often for common surnames, which are more frequent among Black and Hispanic Americans. The FTC and CFPB have brought cases against screening companies (e.g., TransUnion Rental Screening, $15M, 2023).
- **Opacity:** Applicants often receive only "denied," with no score or reasons, even though the Fair Credit Reporting Act gives them rights to dispute.

### Insurance and risk-based pricing

Insurance prices risk. More granular data enables more precise pricing, but precision itself raises ethical questions:

| Feature | Predictive? | Concern |
|---|---|---|
| Credit-based insurance score | Correlates with auto claims | Penalizes low-income people; correlates with race. Banned or restricted in California, Hawaii, Massachusetts and Michigan for auto insurance |
| ZIP code | Correlates with theft and accident rates | Historic redlining; race proxy |
| Telematics (driving behavior) | Directly measures driving | Surveillance; night-shift workers penalized for night driving |
| Occupation and education | Correlates with claims | Class proxy |
| "Price optimization" | Predicts willingness to pay or likelihood to shop around | Charges loyal customers more. Banned in many states as unfairly discriminatory |

**Colorado SB 21-169 (2021)** prohibits insurers from using external consumer data and algorithms that unfairly discriminate based on protected characteristics, and requires testing. Life-insurance regulations took effect in 2023, with auto and health following ⚠. It is one of the few laws requiring insurers to *test* their models for disparate impact.

### Actuarially fair vs. socially fair

- **Actuarial fairness:** Each person pays according to their expected cost. Price differences that reflect risk differences are "fair."
- **Solidarity:** Insurance exists to *pool* risk. Charging people for risks they can't control, especially when those risks reflect historical injustice, undermines that purpose.

**Example:** If people in historically redlined neighborhoods have higher auto-theft rates because of disinvestment, charging them more is actuarially accurate but makes them pay again for past discrimination.

Society has made choices on this before:

- The ACA (2010) bans pricing health insurance by pre-existing conditions.
- GINA (2008) bans genetic discrimination in health insurance and employment.
- The EU bans sex-based insurance pricing (*Test-Achats*, 2011).

AI makes these choices more pressing, because models can find many proxies for the attributes society has chosen to protect.

## Applying the frameworks

- **Justice (Rawls):** Behind the veil, would you choose a pricing system that charges more to people born in disadvantaged neighborhoods?
- **Consequences:** Granular pricing may lower costs for many while pricing others out of coverage entirely.
- **Rights:** Housing is widely recognized as a basic need. Opaque denial without recourse raises procedural rights concerns.
- **Decency:** Ignoring a voucher's guaranteed rent while penalizing medical debt fails to give reasonable weight to the applicant's actual circumstances.

## Discussion questions

1. Should tenant screening reports be prohibited from including eviction filings that didn't result in a judgment?
2. Is it fair for insurers to use credit scores in auto insurance if they genuinely predict claims?
3. Telematics rewards safe drivers with lower premiums. Is it a fair deal or coerced surveillance?
4. Which protected attributes, if any, should insurers be allowed to use? Age is commonly used in auto and life insurance. Why is age treated differently from race?

## Key terms

- **Tenant screening:** Automated background and risk assessment of rental applicants.
- **Credit-based insurance score:** An insurance risk score derived from credit history.
- **Price optimization:** Pricing based on predicted willingness to pay rather than risk.
- **Actuarial fairness:** Pricing according to expected cost.
- **Risk pooling / solidarity:** Sharing risk across a group.
