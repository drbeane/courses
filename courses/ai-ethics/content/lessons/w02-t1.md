# 02-01 — Privacy as Contextual Integrity

**Module:** 2 — Surveillance, Biometrics, and Privacy
**Estimated time:** 35 minutes

**In this lesson:** "It was public anyway" is the most common defense of collecting data about people. This lesson explains why that defense fails, introduces a better way to think about privacy, and sets out what surveillance actually costs.

## "But It Was Public"

Clearview AI built a database of billions of face images by copying them from the open web. Every one of those photos was, in a sense, public. It sat on a news site, a social media profile or a company's staff page, where the person who posted it knew strangers might see it.

Almost none of those people expected what came next. Their faces were converted into biometric templates, indexed, and made searchable by police in seconds. Clearview argued that collecting public images was legal and protected as free speech. Regulators in the United Kingdom, France, Italy, Greece and the Netherlands disagreed, and fined the company or ordered it to delete the data.

If "public" were the test, Clearview did nothing wrong. Most people feel strongly that it did. This lesson is about what that feeling is tracking.

## Why Public and Private Is Not Enough

The everyday idea of privacy sorts information into two bins. Private information is protected, and public information is fair game. AI breaks that picture in three ways.

The first is aggregation. Scattered facts that are each public can be combined into something none of them reveals alone: your daily routine, who you spend time with, what you believe.

The second is inference. Models derive new, sensitive information from data that looks harmless. Health conditions can be predicted from purchases, and sexual orientation from social connections. Lesson 02-09 covers how.

The third is scale and permanence. Being seen by a stranger on the street is not the same thing as being recognized by a system that can identify everyone, everywhere, and never forgets.

In each case nothing that was private became public. Something else changed, and the two-bin picture has no way to describe it.

## Privacy as Appropriate Flow

The philosopher Helen Nissenbaum offers a different account in *Privacy in Context* (2010). She argues that privacy is about whether information flows appropriately. Every area of life, such as medicine, friendship, work or shopping, has its own norms about how information moves within it. Privacy is violated when a flow breaks the norms of the context it came from. She calls this **contextual integrity**.

To analyze a flow, you describe it with five parameters. Who is the sender? Who is the recipient? Who is the subject, the person the information is about? What type of information is it? And under what **transmission principle** does it move, meaning the condition attached to it, such as confidentiality, consent, payment or legal requirement?

A simple example shows how it works. You describe your symptoms to your doctor. The sender is you, the recipient is the doctor, the subject is you, the type is health information, and the transmission principle is confidentiality. Now suppose your doctor passes that information to your employer. The information is exactly the same. But the recipient has changed and confidentiality has been dropped, so the flow breaks the norms of medicine. On this account, that change of context is the violation.

Now apply it to Clearview.

| Parameter | Original flow | Clearview's flow |
|---|---|---|
| Sender | You | Clearview's collection software |
| Recipient | Visitors to your profile | Police and, earlier, private clients |
| Information type | A photo for presenting yourself socially | A biometric identifier |
| Transmission principle | Social sharing under the platform's norms | Covert collection, then sale |

Every parameter changed except the subject. This is why "it was public" misses the point. You shared a photo with one audience for one purpose. Something different was done with it, by someone else, for someone else.

## Why It Persuades

Contextual integrity explains things the two-bin picture cannot. It explains why the same piece of information can be fine to share in one setting and a betrayal in another. It explains the reaction to Clearview without pretending the photos were secret. And it does not depend on consent, which Lesson 01-04 showed to be weak in practice.

It also has room for good flows. A fitness app that shows you your own step count, a hospital that shares your scan with a specialist, a blind person's device that reads a street sign aloud: each of these moves information, and each fits the norms of its context. The framework does not treat data as dangerous. It asks whether a particular flow matches what the people involved could reasonably expect. That makes it useful for design as well as for criticism. You will use the five parameters to draw data-flow diagrams in A2.

## What Surveillance Costs

When flows are inappropriate at scale, the harms go well beyond embarrassment.

The best documented is the **chilling effect**: people avoid lawful activities when they believe they are being watched. They stay away from protests, religious services, clinics and support groups. One study found a measurable drop in visits to Wikipedia articles on terrorism-related topics after the 2013 disclosures about U.S. government surveillance.

Surveillance also falls unevenly. Some communities are watched far more than others, and when the systems make errors, those communities absorb more of them.

Detailed profiles make manipulation possible, because persuasion can be aimed at a person's particular weaknesses.

The watcher gains power over the watched, since knowledge runs in one direction only.

People lose **practical obscurity**, the protection that came from information being technically available but hard to find. A court record in a filing cabinet and the same record in a search result are different things.

And every database is a security risk. Data that has been collected can be stolen.

## "Nothing to Hide"

The usual reply to all of this is that if you have done nothing wrong, you have nothing to fear. The legal scholar Daniel Solove has given the best-known answers, and they are worth knowing.

Privacy protects far more than wrongdoing. Medical conditions, relationships, and religious and political views are not wrong, and most people still do not want them known to everyone.

The individual does not get to decide what counts as wrong. Laws change and so do the priorities of those who enforce them. Data collected under one government is available to the next.

Mistakes happen. Angela Lipps had done nothing wrong, and Lesson 02-04 describes what happened to her.

Being watched changes how people behave, whether or not they are guilty of anything.

Finally, the argument treats privacy as a private cost that each person can choose to bear. That misses the way privacy now works. When one person sends their DNA to a genealogy site, their relatives are exposed too. When enough people's data trains a model, the model can draw conclusions about people who shared nothing. Privacy has become a collective good, something like clean air, that individual choices cannot protect. It also supports things a free society depends on, such as the ability to meet and organize without being recorded.

## Where It Breaks

Contextual integrity has real weaknesses.

Norms are often unclear or disputed. What are the norms for a group chat, or a conversation with a chatbot? When people disagree about the norms, the framework cannot settle the disagreement.

It leans toward the status quo. If privacy means following existing norms, the framework seems to favor however things have been done, including when the existing norm was unjust. Nissenbaum's answer is that a new flow can be defended if it serves the purposes of the context better than the old one. That is reasonable, but it brings back the kind of weighing the framework was meant to avoid.

New technologies arrive with no norms at all. There was no settled expectation about face search engines before one existed.

And it can undervalue the gains from new flows. Medical research, fraud detection and public health often need data to move in ways people did not originally expect. A rule that every flow must match prior expectations would block a great deal of good. The framework can accommodate this, but only by asking whether the benefits justify changing the norm, and reasonable people will answer differently.

## Clearview Again

With the framework in hand, look at how the approaches from Module 1 apply to the opening case.

An argument from rights says that turning a person's face into a searchable identifier without their knowledge treats them merely as a means. An argument from consequences sets the crimes solved against the chilling effects and the risk of misuse or a breach, while admitting that the second group is much harder to measure than the first. An argument from justice asks who is searched most often, and history suggests the answer is poor, minority and immigrant communities. And the widest lens asks what public life is like once nobody can be anonymous in public.

Contextual integrity does not replace those arguments. It explains what happened precisely enough for them to get started: a photo shared for one purpose became an identifier used for another.

Sender, recipient, subject, information type and transmission principle.

**2. According to contextual integrity, when is privacy violated?**

:::answer Answer

:::answer Answer

People avoiding lawful activity, such as protest, worship or seeking care, because they believe they are being watched.

## Key Terms

- **Contextual integrity:** Privacy understood as information flowing appropriately for the norms of its context.
- **Transmission principle:** The condition under which information flows, such as consent or confidentiality.
- **Practical obscurity:** The protection that comes from information being hard to find.
- **Chilling effect:** Avoidance of lawful activity caused by the belief that one is being watched.
