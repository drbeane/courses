# 02-04 — Facial Recognition in Policing: Wrongful Arrests

**Module:** 2 — Surveillance, Biometrics, and Privacy
**Estimated time:** 35 minutes
**Content note:** Wrongful arrest and detention.

**In this lesson:** Police use facial recognition to turn a photo of an unknown suspect into a name. This lesson looks at what the tool is good for, how a possible match has repeatedly become the arrest of an innocent person, and which rules would have stopped it.

## A Match From a Fake ID

*The account below comes from a civil complaint filed in September 2026, Lipps v. City of Fargo et al., as reported by Live 5 News. These are allegations. No court has ruled on them.*

In the spring of 2025, someone carried out a series of bank-fraud thefts around Fargo, North Dakota. Investigators had a photo of the person they were looking for, and an analyst with the West Fargo police, working with a state intelligence center, ran it through facial recognition software. The software returned a possible match: Angela Lipps, who lived in Elizabethton, Tennessee, about a thousand miles away.

According to the complaint, the Fargo detective who took the case had reasons to doubt the match from the start. Lipps lived in Tennessee and had never been to North Dakota. She did not fit the description of the suspect. Her finances showed no unexplained money. The complaint also says the photo that produced the match was not surveillance footage of the thief. It was the picture on the fake ID the thief had used, a detail the detective allegedly left out when he sought charges.

In July 2025, officers arrested Lipps at gunpoint in Tennessee while she was babysitting. She was extradited to North Dakota and held in the Cass County Jail on a $100,000 bond. She says that for long stretches she went without her dentures and her medication.

What freed her was ordinary evidence. Her attorney produced bank records showing she had been in Tennessee on the days of the thefts, and the charges were dropped on December 24, 2025. She says she left jail with little money and no winter coat.

Fargo's police department had no policy, training or supervision for facial recognition when this happened. The neighboring West Fargo department, whose analyst ran the search, did have a policy: only trained officers could use the tool, and nobody could be arrested on a match alone. Fargo's police chief acknowledged "investigative errors" in March 2026 and resigned later that month, saying new policies and training had been adopted. Lipps is suing for $10 million, claiming unlawful seizure, denial of due process, false arrest and imprisonment, malicious prosecution, and a failure by the city to set policy and train its officers.

## Why Police Want This Tool

It would be easy to read that story and conclude that police should never use facial recognition. Before weighing that, it is worth being clear about what the tool does for them, because the argument for it is a serious one.

Many crimes leave behind a face and nothing else. A camera records a robbery, an assault or a fraud at a bank counter, and the investigator has an image of a stranger. Without a name, the case often ends there. Facial recognition compares that image against a database of known photos, usually mugshots or driver's license pictures, and returns a list of people who look similar. An investigator can then check each one the usual way. Used like this, the software does not accuse anyone. It suggests where to look, in the way a tip line or a witness who says "that looks like my neighbor" does.

The technology is also much better than it was. In tests by the U.S. National Institute of Standards and Technology, the best algorithms searching a database of 12 million clear, front-facing portraits missed the right person in only about 0.1 percent of searches, and they have improved since. Lesson 02-03 covers these tests and their limits. Police agencies credit the tool with identifying suspects in violent crimes and, in child-exploitation cases, with identifying victims who could then be found and protected; these are the agencies' own accounts.

Two cautions belong next to that evidence. Most of it is reported by the agencies that use the tool, and there is little independent measurement of how many cases it solves that would otherwise have gone cold. And the accuracy figures come from good photos. The image from a crime scene is usually a poor one.

Still, the people who gain when the tool works are real: victims whose cases get solved, and innocent people who are cleared because the right person was found. Any rule that restricts the tool gives up some of that. The question for the rest of this lesson is what goes wrong, and whether it can be fixed at an acceptable price.

## The Same Failure, Again and Again

Lipps is not the first. At least six people in the United States have publicly described being arrested after a facial recognition search pointed to them for a crime they did not commit.

| Person | Place and year | What happened |
|---|---|---|
| Michael Oliver | Detroit, 2019 | Charged with larceny; the case was dismissed |
| Nijeer Parks | Woodbridge, New Jersey, 2019 | Jailed for 10 days; the match came from a photo on a fake ID |
| Robert Williams | Detroit, 2020 | Arrested in front of his family over stolen watches; his 2024 settlement changed Detroit's policy |
| Randal Reid | Georgia and Louisiana, 2022 | Jailed for about a week over thefts in a state he had never visited |
| Porcha Woodruff | Detroit, 2023 | Arrested while eight months pregnant |
| Angela Lipps | Tennessee and North Dakota, 2025 | Alleged; described above |

The American Civil Liberties Union counts more than a dozen such cases. Nobody knows the true number, because in most places a defendant is never told that facial recognition was used. Most of the people publicly known to have been wrongly arrested are Black, which fits the finding in Lesson 02-03 that many algorithms make more false matches on some groups than others. Lipps's case shows the problem is not confined to one group.

Read the cases side by side and the same things recur. The photo of the suspect was poor or was the wrong kind of image: a grainy still from a security camera, or a picture on a fake ID. It was compared against a very large database, which makes it likely that someone will look similar by chance. The police then did little to check the result independently. Facts that pointed away from the person, such as an alibi, a home in another state or a different build, were set aside. And nobody told the defense how the person had come to be a suspect.

Notice what is missing from that list. In none of these cases was the software's error the whole story. The software did what it is built to do and returned a person who resembled the photo. People turned that resemblance into an arrest.

## How a Lead Becomes an Arrest

Vendors and police policies describe a facial recognition result as an **investigative lead**: information that justifies looking further but does not justify an arrest. An arrest requires **probable cause**, the constitutional standard of a reasonable belief, grounded in facts, that this person committed the crime. Looking like someone is not probable cause. So how does a lead end up treated as if it were?

Part of the answer is **automation bias**, the tendency to trust what a machine produces more than the same claim from a person. A match from software feels objective and scientific. An anonymous caller saying "the thief looks like a woman in Tennessee" would be treated with caution. The same claim from a computer arrives with an air of authority.

Once an investigator believes the match, **confirmation bias** takes over: the habit of looking for evidence that supports what you already think and explaining away what does not. A suspect who lives a thousand miles away becomes a suspect who must have traveled.

The most damaging step is often the photo lineup. A common way to "confirm" a match is to show a witness a set of photos that includes the person the software picked. That looks like independent evidence, but it is not. The software chose that person because they resemble the suspect, so a witness asked to pick the face most like the one they remember will tend to pick them too. The lineup measures the same resemblance a second time and then presents it as a second source.

Every vendor says its product produces leads only. That statement is true and it is also the weakest kind of safeguard, because a disclaimer cannot enforce itself. Whether the vendor shares responsibility when its warning is ignored is taken up in Lesson 08-05.

## What the Safeguards Can and Cannot Do

Several rules have been adopted in response to these cases. Each one closes a particular gap, and each leaves something open.

The most direct is a **corroboration requirement**. Under the 2024 settlement in Robert Williams's case, Detroit police may not arrest someone on the basis of a facial recognition result alone, or on a photo lineup built from that result, without independent evidence. West Fargo's policy has the same core rule. It targets the exact step that failed in every case above. Its limit is that someone still has to judge whether the other evidence is really independent and really points to this person.

**Disclosure** rules require that a defendant be told facial recognition was used. A New Jersey appeals court held in 2023 (State v. Arteaga) that a defendant is entitled to information about the search that identified him. Disclosure lets a defense lawyer challenge the photo quality and the investigation that followed. It does nothing until after someone has been charged, and as of this writing it is not required in most states.

Other measures work earlier or later. Rules about photo quality ban searches using sketches, edited images or pictures of look-alikes, though compliance is hard to audit. Training and certification limit who can run a search, but trained people are still subject to both kinds of bias. Detroit also agreed to review its past cases back to 2017, which can find old errors but cannot prevent new ones.

Finally, some places have changed the law. A few states require a warrant or other approval before a search, and several cities, including San Francisco in 2019 and Boston in 2020, banned police use outright. Some bans have since been loosened, and none of them bind federal agencies. The result across the country is a patchwork: what protected a suspect in West Fargo did not exist a few miles away in Fargo.

## Where People Disagree

There are three broad positions on what the policy should be, and each has serious defenders.

The first is a **ban**. On this view the harm is built in. An innocent person jailed for a week has lost something no settlement gives back, the errors fall hardest on people with the least power to fight them, and searching millions of faces to find one treats everyone in the database as a suspect. Rules will be ignored in practice, as the vendor's disclaimer was. This is at bottom an argument about rights: some things should not be done to a person even if the overall results are good.

The second is a **strict framework**: allow the tool for serious crimes, with a warrant or supervisor approval, a corroboration rule, disclosure to the defense, and public audits. Its defenders point to the benefits described earlier and argue that a ban gives them up entirely. They also point out that the alternative to facial recognition is not a perfect process. It is eyewitness identification, which played a part in about 69 percent of the U.S. convictions later overturned by DNA evidence, according to the Innocence Project. The fair comparison is with the methods police would use instead.

The third is **permissive use under existing standards**. A match is one more lead, probable cause is already required for any arrest, and detectives are already trained to check their leads. On this view the wrongful arrests are failures of ordinary police work, not of the technology, and the answer is to enforce the standards that exist.

The Lipps case is a test of the third position. Fargo had exactly those ordinary standards and nothing more, and if the allegations are true they failed at every step. That does not settle the argument between the first two positions. It asks you to weigh a known, concentrated harm to a few people against a benefit that is spread widely but has been measured poorly. It also raises a question of justice that numbers do not answer: Lipps was freed because she had a lawyer who obtained her bank records. What happens to the person who has neither?

## Check Your Understanding

**1. What is the difference between an investigative lead and probable cause?**

:::answer Answer
A lead justifies further investigation. Probable cause is the constitutional standard for an arrest: a reasonable belief, based on facts, that this person committed the crime. A facial recognition match is a lead. Resemblance alone is not probable cause.
:::

**2. Why is a photo lineup that includes the software's pick weak evidence?**

:::answer Answer
The software chose that person because they look like the suspect. A witness asked to pick the most similar face will tend to choose them for the same reason. The lineup repeats the resemblance; it does not independently confirm it.
:::

**3. Name three things the known wrongful arrests have in common.**

:::answer Answer
Any three of these: a poor or inappropriate photo of the suspect; a search against a very large database; little or no independent corroboration; facts pointing away from the person were ignored; the defense was not told facial recognition was used.
:::

**4. Why is the true number of wrongful arrests linked to facial recognition unknown?**

:::answer Answer
In most places defendants are not told that facial recognition was used to identify them, so many people never learn how they became a suspect.
:::

## Key Terms

- **Investigative lead:** Information that justifies further investigation but not an arrest.
- **Probable cause:** The constitutional standard for an arrest: a reasonable belief, based on facts, that a person committed a crime.
- **Automation bias:** The tendency to trust a machine's output more than the same claim from a person.
- **Confirmation bias:** The tendency to seek evidence that supports what you already believe and to discount evidence that does not.
- **Corroboration requirement:** A rule that a facial recognition result must be backed by independent evidence before an arrest.
- **Exculpatory evidence:** Evidence that tends to show a person is innocent.
