# 01-05 — Decency, Character, and Care

**Module:** 1 — Ethical Foundations and AI in Society
**Estimated time:** 35 minutes

**In this lesson:** Rules and results do not cover everything. This lesson looks at three approaches that ask what kind of person, professional or organization acts well, and introduces the four lenses this course uses in every module.

## The Detective's Choice

According to the lawsuit in *Lipps v. City of Fargo*, the facial recognition result that pointed to Angela Lipps was only a lead. The detective allegedly knew that she lived in Tennessee, had never been to North Dakota, did not fit the description of the suspect and had no unexplained money. He pursued charges anyway. A few miles away, the West Fargo police department had a written policy that nobody could be arrested on a match alone.

The software did not decide to arrest her. People did. You can analyze what happened by its results, and they were terrible. You can analyze it by rights, and hers were allegedly violated. But another question seems just as natural: what kind of professional does this, and what kind does not? Two detectives with the same tool and the same lead could act very differently. The difference between them lies in their character and in the organizations that formed it, more than in any rule or calculation.

## Decency: A Standard People Can Meet

In *A Decent Life: Morality for the Rest of Us* (2019), the philosopher Todd May starts from a complaint about the two theories you have just studied. Taken strictly, both ask too much. Utilitarianism implies you should give away nearly everything you own, since someone else always needs it more. Kant's ethics forbids even the small lie that spares a friend's feelings. Most people cannot live that way and do not try, so the theories end up as ideals to admire from a distance.

May proposes a standard that ordinary people can actually meet, which he calls **decency**. As this course summarizes it, a decent person does not treat others merely as instruments. They give reasonable weight to other people's interests, especially when the cost to themselves is modest. They do not take advantage of someone's vulnerability. They accept that moral life involves trade-offs and do not demand sainthood of themselves or anyone else. And they pay attention to the effects of what they do beyond their own circle.

Applied to people who build and deploy AI, decency is a modest demand. It does not ask an engineer to solve the world's problems. It asks them not to cause harm that could have been avoided, to take seriously the interests of the people their system touches, and to refuse to take part in what they can see is wrong.

That modesty is the appeal and also the obvious question. Is decency enough for someone whose product affects millions of people? May's standard was written for ordinary life. You may reasonably think that people with unusual power owe more than the ordinary amount of care.

## Four Lenses

May describes moral concern as widening outward from oneself. This course turns that into four lenses, and each module will ask you to look through at least two.

The first is the **self**. What does this do to my own integrity, skills and well-being? An engineer asked to build software that monitors co-workers faces this question. So does a person who notices they are leaning heavily on a chatbot for company.

The second is **others and community**. Who is affected, and who among them is vulnerable? Think of applicants screened by a hiring tool, or a neighborhood with heavy camera coverage.

The third is **non-humans**. What about animals and ecosystems? A data center can drain a wetland, and AI is increasingly used to manage farm animals.

The fourth is the **world**. What happens at the scale of whole societies, the planet and people not yet born? Here the concerns are the concentration of power, the climate, and whether people can still trust what they read and see.

The lenses are not a theory. They are a way of checking that you have looked in every direction before you decide, including the directions where the good news is. A tool that reads text aloud for a blind user looks very different through the first two lenses than through the fourth.

## Character

May's approach belongs to an older family. **Virtue ethics**, which goes back to Aristotle, does not begin by asking which act is right. It asks what a good person would do, and which traits of character make up a good life. A virtue is a settled disposition, such as honesty, courage or fairness, that a person builds through practice until acting that way is second nature. The virtue that ties the others together is **practical wisdom**: the ability to see what a particular situation calls for.

The philosopher Shannon Vallor has asked which virtues people need in order to live well with powerful technology. Her list of "technomoral virtues" includes honesty, self-control, humility, justice, courage, empathy, care, civility, flexibility, perspective and magnanimity.

Character matters for AI professionals in three practical ways. No set of rules anticipates every case, and something has to guide a person when the rulebook runs out. Organizations shape character through what they reward: a team praised only for shipping fast learns to be impatient with safety review, whatever the company's stated values say. And some situations take courage. Raising a concern, refusing unsafe work or reporting wrongdoing can cost a person their standing or their job.

## Care

**Care ethics**, developed by Carol Gilligan, Nel Noddings and Joan Tronto among others, starts from a different observation. Moral life does not mainly consist of impartial judgments about strangers. It consists of relationships in which people depend on one another: children on parents, patients on nurses, the old on the young. On this view, acting well means being attentive to what a particular person needs, accepting responsibility within a relationship, and taking vulnerability and dependence seriously. Care ethics also draws attention to the work of caring, much of which goes unseen and unpaid.

It raises sharp questions about AI. A companion or therapy chatbot simulates care, but it does not care about anyone. Can it ethically meet a lonely person's need for someone who does? Do robots in elder care add to human attention, or replace it to cut costs? And who does the hidden care work that AI depends on, such as the content moderators who view violent and abusive material so that others never have to? Modules 4 and 5 take up these cases.

## What Each Adds, and Where Each Falls Short

Each of these approaches captures something that the tally of results and the list of duties leave out, and each has a real weakness.

Decency is achievable and intuitive, and it pushes concern outward. But it is vague at the edges. "Reasonable weight" to other people's interests is exactly the phrase people will disagree about.

Virtue ethics handles new situations well, and it puts the spotlight on professionals and on the culture of organizations, which is where many failures begin. But it does not give a procedure for deciding. Telling a detective to be wise is less useful than telling him he may not arrest on a match alone. The virtues can also conflict: honesty with kindness, courage with humility.

Care ethics puts vulnerability and relationships at the center. But a morality built on those close to us can neglect strangers, and it has less to say about harms that are built into large systems.

There is a more general objection as well. All three can be used to shift blame onto individuals. If the lesson of Fargo is "that detective lacked humility," the department that had no policy and gave no training gets off lightly. The best use of these approaches treats institutions as having character too.

## Fargo, Three Ways

Seen through decency, the Fargo arrest was a failure to give reasonable weight to the interests of a person with a great deal to lose. An innocent woman's liberty was put at risk to close a case.

Seen through virtue, the detective needed humility about what the tool could tell him and the courage to slow down. The department lacked practical wisdom: it had adopted a powerful tool with no rules for using it. West Fargo's one-sentence policy is what institutional humility looks like on paper.

Seen through care, the picture becomes particular. Lipps was looking after children when she was arrested. She says she went without her medication and her dentures in jail. Care ethics directs attention to the person, where the other frameworks tend to look at the case file.

None of this replaces what the earlier lessons said about results and rights. It adds the question those lessons could not ask: what would a good detective, in a well-run department, have done?

## Key Terms

- **Decency:** Todd May's modest, achievable moral standard: not using others merely as instruments and giving reasonable weight to their interests.
- **Virtue ethics:** Ethics centered on character and on what a good person would do.
- **Practical wisdom:** The ability to see what a particular situation calls for and act well in it.
- **Care ethics:** Ethics grounded in relationships, attentiveness and responsiveness to need.
