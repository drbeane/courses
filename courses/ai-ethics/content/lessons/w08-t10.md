# 08-10 — Portfolio Defense and Course Synthesis

**Module:** 8 — Governance, Accountability, and Responsible Deployment
**Estimated time:** 60 minutes plus the defense
**Type:** Capstone (A5 defense; A6 peer review)

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

1. Defend a deployment decision before peers and respond constructively to critique.
2. Conduct an evidence-focused peer review.
3. Synthesize the course's frameworks and cases into a personal professional ethic for AI work.

## Part 1 — Portfolio defense (A5)

**Format:** A 10-minute recorded presentation plus written answers to two reviewer questions.

**Suggested structure:**

| Minutes | Content |
|---|---|
| 0–1 | The system and the decision: what you recommend, in one sentence |
| 1–3 | Purpose, alternatives and affected people |
| 3–5 | Key evidence: evaluation results, including one failure and what changed |
| 5–7 | Top risks and safeguards, with owners and triggers |
| 7–8 | Rights, obligations and redress (dated) |
| 8–9 | Recommendation and conditions for reconsideration |
| 9–10 | What you're least sure about |

**Defense tips:**

- Lead with the decision. Don't bury it.
- Show evidence, not adjectives ("an impact ratio of 0.67 for Black applicants," not "some bias").
- Name your uncertainties before reviewers do.
- If you recommend deployment, say who is accountable for each residual risk.

## Part 2 — Peer review (A6)

Review one classmate's dossier using the A5 rubric. Your review (500–700 words) must:

1. **Summarize** the recommendation in two sentences, to show you understood it.
2. **Identify the two weakest evidentiary claims** and explain why.
3. **Propose one missing stakeholder or harm.**
4. **Assess whether the recommendation follows from the evidence.**
5. **Pose two questions** for the author to answer in writing.

**Standards for good reviews:**

- **Specific:** Cite sections and claims.
- **Evidence-focused:** Critique the reasoning, not the author's values.
- **Constructive:** Suggest how to strengthen the work.
- **Calibrated:** Being severe earns no credit. Being accurate does.

The author responds in 200 words or fewer.

## Part 3 — Course synthesis

### What we covered

| Module | Core lesson |
|---|---|
| 1 | Ethical reasoning uses several frameworks; law ≠ ethics; systems are sociotechnical |
| 2 | Privacy is about appropriate flows; facial recognition errors fall on real people; always-on devices affect non-users |
| 3 | Bias enters through labels and proxies; fairness metrics conflict; contestability matters |
| 4 | Generative AI fabricates; deepfakes harm real people, especially women and girls; verification is an ethical practice |
| 5 | Exposure isn't displacement; entry-level work is at risk; monitoring affects dignity; workers need voice |
| 6 | AI has a physical footprint; burdens are local; efficiency isn't enough; non-humans count |
| 7 | Openness trades concentration risk against diffusion risk; agents turn errors into events; evaluations can fail |
| 8 | Governance is plural and changing; accountability must be designed; deployment is a judgment |

### Cross-cutting threads, revisited

1. **Who bears the error?** In every module, the costs of AI errors fell disproportionately on people with less power.
2. **Law moved; ethics didn't.** Colorado, the EU Omnibus, U.S. preemption: legal requirements changed repeatedly during 2025–2026. The ethical questions stayed the same.
3. **Evidence discipline.** The best analyses separated measured from forecast, and allegation from fact.
4. **Power and concentration.** Who builds, who decides, who can refuse.
5. **The four lenses.** Self, others, non-humans, world.

### A personal professional ethic

**Final reflection (ungraded, but required for course completion; 400–600 words):**

1. Which case in this course changed your thinking most, and how?
2. Which framework do you find most useful in practice? Which do you find least persuasive?
3. Write three commitments you'll carry into your professional work with AI. Make them **specific and checkable**. For example: "I will not deploy a model affecting people without disaggregated evaluation," not "I will be ethical."
4. Describe one situation where you expect those commitments to be tested, and how you'll respond.
5. What question about AI and society do you think will matter most in five years that this course didn't address?

### Closing thought

Todd May's decency asks something modest: don't treat others merely as means, give their interests reasonable weight, and don't take advantage of their vulnerability. The cases in this course were rarely failures of genius. Most were failures of decency at scale: an arrest without corroboration, a tool released without foreseeable-misuse safeguards, a sandbox without isolation, a debt notice without recourse.

Graduates of this program will build, buy and govern these systems. The course's aim is that you'll be the person in the room who asks: *who bears the error, what's the evidence, and what would change our minds?*

## Key terms

- **Decision dossier:** The integrated A5 document supporting a deployment recommendation.
- **Peer review:** Evidence-focused critique by a classmate (A6).
- **Calibration:** Matching the confidence of claims to the strength of evidence.
