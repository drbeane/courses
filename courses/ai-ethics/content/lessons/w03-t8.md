# 03-08 — Predictive Policing and Feedback Loops

**Module:** 3 — Algorithmic Bias in Consequential Decisions
**Estimated time:** 35 minutes

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

1. Distinguish place-based from person-based predictive policing.
2. Explain runaway feedback loops and why crime data are not neutral measurements of crime.
3. Evaluate why several major predictive policing programs were discontinued.

## Opening case: the list and the knock on the door

From 2012 to 2019, the Chicago Police Department maintained the **Strategic Subject List** (SSL), sometimes called the "heat list." It used an algorithm to score people on their likelihood of being involved in a shooting, as victim or perpetrator. Officers sometimes visited people on the list to warn them.

- **RAND evaluation (2016):** Being on the list did not reduce a person's likelihood of being a shooting victim. People on the list were more likely to be arrested for a shooting, suggesting it was used as a suspect list.
- **Inspector General review (2020):** The program had problems with data quality, transparency and how scores were used.
- **The list itself:** Many people on it had never been arrested for a violent crime.

Chicago decommissioned the program in late 2019.

## Core ideas

### Two kinds of predictive policing

| | Place-based | Person-based |
|---|---|---|
| Predicts | Where and when crime is likely | Who is likely to be involved in crime |
| Examples | PredPol/Geolitica, HunchLab | Chicago SSL; LAPD's LASER (Operation LASER) |
| Typical output | Map boxes for patrol | Lists of names or scores |
| Main concern | Over-policing neighborhoods | Pre-crime targeting of individuals |

### Runaway feedback loops

Ensign et al. (2018) formalized a simple problem:

1. The model predicts crime in Area A based on past *recorded* crime.
2. Police patrol Area A more.
3. More patrols → more *observed and recorded* crime in Area A (especially discretionary offenses like drug possession).
4. That new data confirms the model's prediction → still more patrols.

Even if true crime rates are equal across areas, the model can lock onto one area. Lum and Isaac (2016) simulated PredPol on Oakland drug-arrest data. The algorithm sent police predominantly to Black and low-income neighborhoods, though public-health survey data suggested drug use was spread across the city.

### Crime data are not crime

Recorded crime reflects:

- **what's reported:** victim willingness to call police, which varies with community trust,
- **what's observed:** where police are, and
- **what's recorded:** officer discretion, department priorities and sometimes falsification (the "dirty data" problem).

Richardson, Schultz & Crawford (2019) documented that several jurisdictions using predictive policing had been under federal consent decrees or investigations for unlawful policing practices. Their data were shaped by those practices.

### Evidence and discontinuations

| Program | Outcome |
|---|---|
| LAPD Operation LASER and PredPol | Ended in 2019 and 2020 after an Inspector General audit found inconsistent implementation and weak evidence |
| Chicago SSL | Decommissioned in 2019 |
| PredPol (renamed Geolitica) | A 2023 analysis by The Markup and Wired of predictions in Plainfield, NJ found fewer than 0.5% matched reported crimes. The company shut down in 2023 and its assets went to SoundThinking. |
| Santa Cruz, CA | Became the first U.S. city to ban predictive policing (2020) |

Randomized evaluations of place-based predictive policing have found mixed or modest effects, and many deployments were never evaluated.

### What persists

Predictive policing as a brand has faded, but related tools are expanding:

- gunshot detection (ShotSpotter/SoundThinking),
- automated license plate readers (Flock Safety, used by thousands of agencies; questions about data sharing with federal immigration enforcement arose in 2025 ⚠),
- real-time crime centers that integrate cameras, facial recognition and data feeds, and
- "risk terrain modeling."

The feedback-loop logic applies to many of them.

## Applying the frameworks

- **Justice (structural):** Feedback loops reproduce historical patterns of over-policing without anyone intending discrimination.
- **Deontology:** Person-based prediction treats individuals as suspects for what they might do.
- **Consequences:** Ask whether the program reduced crime, increased trust or harmed communities, and look for independent evaluations.
- **Decency, community lens:** What is it like to live in a neighborhood the algorithm always flags?

## Discussion questions

1. Can predictive policing ever be designed to avoid feedback loops? What data would you need?
2. Is place-based prediction ethically different from deciding to patrol "high-crime areas" based on officer experience?
3. Chicago's SSL included people as potential victims. Is identifying likely victims an ethically different use? How should the response differ (police vs. social services)?
4. Should cities be required to approve surveillance technologies through public votes (as under some "Community Control Over Police Surveillance" ordinances)?

## Activity: feedback simulation (15 minutes)

Two neighborhoods have equal true crime (100 incidents per month). Police detect 20% of incidents in a patrolled neighborhood and 5% in an unpatrolled one. Month 1: patrols are split evenly, so each neighborhood is patrolled half the time and each records 12.5 incidents. The model then sends all patrols to whichever neighborhood recorded more crime the previous month. A data-entry error gives Neighborhood A 13 recorded incidents in Month 1.

Trace recorded incidents for Months 2–4. What does the model "learn"?

*(Key: A gets all patrols from Month 2, so A records 20 per month and B records 5 per month. The gap is permanent and self-confirming.)*

## Key terms

- **Place-based / person-based predictive policing:** Predicting locations / individuals.
- **Runaway feedback loop:** Predictions driving data that reinforce predictions.
- **Dirty data:** Data produced by flawed, biased or unlawful practices.
- **CCOPS ordinance:** Community Control Over Police Surveillance laws requiring public approval of surveillance technologies.
