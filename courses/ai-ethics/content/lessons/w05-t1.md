# 05-01 — Exposure Is Not Displacement: Reading Labor Evidence

**Module:** 5 — Work, Labor, and Economic Power
**Estimated time:** 40 minutes

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

1. Distinguish exposure, adoption, displacement and augmentation in AI labor research.
2. Classify labor claims as measured, estimated, forecast or speculative.
3. Critically evaluate headlines about AI and jobs.

## Opening case: two headlines, one month

In August 2026, readers could find both of these headlines:

- "**Stanford Finds No Broad AI Job Displacement**"
- "**AI Driving Decline in Entry-Level Hiring, Survey Finds**"

Both were accurate summaries of real research, and they're compatible. Aggregate employment can be stable while specific groups, here young workers in AI-exposed occupations, see real declines. Meanwhile, companies announcing layoffs sometimes credited AI. Others were accused of "AI-washing" cuts made for other reasons.

Evaluating AI's labor effects requires knowing what each kind of evidence can and can't show.

## Core ideas

### Four concepts

| Concept | Meaning | Typical evidence |
|---|---|---|
| **Exposure** | How much of an occupation's tasks AI *could* affect | Task-based indices (e.g., Eloundou et al., 2023: about 80% of U.S. workers have at least 10% of tasks exposed to LLMs) |
| **Adoption** | Whether firms and workers actually use AI | Surveys; usage data (e.g., Anthropic Economic Index; Census Business Trends survey) |
| **Displacement** | Jobs or hours lost because of AI | Employment and payroll data; firm studies |
| **Augmentation** | AI making workers more productive in existing roles | Field experiments; productivity studies |

**Exposure isn't destiny.** An exposed task may be automated, augmented or left alone, depending on cost, quality, regulation, liability and organizational choices.

### Evidence labels (used throughout the course)

| Label | Definition | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Measured** | Directly observed in data | "Employment of 22–25-year-olds in highly exposed occupations was 19% below the comparison trend in ADP data" |
| **Estimated** | Derived from data with modeling assumptions | "AI accounts for about one fifth of data-center electricity" |
| **Forecast** | Projection of future outcomes | "X million jobs will be displaced by 2030" |
| **Speculative** | Plausible scenario without strong evidentiary basis | "AGI will make most human labor obsolete within a decade" |

Many headline numbers ("300 million jobs") are **forecasts of exposure**, not measurements of job loss.

### What field evidence shows (selected, as of 2026)

- **Customer support:** Brynjolfsson, Li & Raymond (2023, *QJE* 2025) found a generative AI assistant raised productivity about 14% on average, and about 34% for novice workers, with little gain for experts. AI narrowed skill gaps.
- **Consulting:** A Harvard Business School and BCG field experiment (Dell'Acqua et al., 2023) found consultants with GPT-4 did better on tasks "inside the frontier," but worse on a task outside it, where they over-trusted the AI (the "jagged frontier").
- **Software:** Studies of coding assistants report speed gains on some tasks, while METR (2025) found experienced open-source developers were **slower** with AI tools on their own repositories, despite believing they were faster.
- **Freelance markets:** Studies found declines in demand and earnings for writing and image-related freelance work after ChatGPT and image generators launched (e.g., Hui, Reshef & Zhou, 2024).
- **Young workers:** The Stanford "Canaries" study (05-02).
- **A systematic review (2026):** One review of 94 studies found mixed effects, concentrated in specific tasks and groups rather than broad displacement ⚠ (verify the source quality).

### Reading layoff announcements

Firms may attribute layoffs to AI because doing so:

- signals innovation to investors,
- deflects blame from other causes (over-hiring, interest rates, tariffs), or
- reflects genuine automation.

Ask:

1. Which roles were cut?
2. Was AI actually deployed for those tasks?
3. Did hiring elsewhere increase?
4. What do the company's filings say?

### Why distribution matters more than totals

Even if total employment holds, AI can:

- shift work from some occupations, ages or regions to others,
- change job *quality* through deskilling, intensification and surveillance (05-04),
- change bargaining power, and
- shift income from labor to capital (05-03).

## Applying the frameworks

- **Consequentialism:** Aggregate gains may coexist with concentrated losses (01-03).
- **Justice:** Who bears transition costs, and what do they receive (01-06)?
- **Evidence discipline:** Policy should respond to measured harm while preparing for credible forecasts.

## Discussion questions

1. Why might exposure indices overstate or understate AI's real impact on a given occupation?
2. The customer-support study found AI helped novices most. Is that good news for workers, or does it make experienced workers' skills less valuable?
3. When a company says "we're replacing roles with AI," what would you want to verify?
4. Should policymakers act on forecasts or wait for measurements? What are the risks of each?

1. label it measured / estimated / forecast / speculative,
2. find the original source, and
3. note who produced it (academic, consultancy, vendor, government) and any interest they have.

This is practice for A3.

## Key terms

- **Exposure:** The share of tasks AI could affect.
- **Displacement / augmentation:** AI replacing labor / enhancing it.
- **Jagged frontier:** AI is uneven in what it does well, and the boundary is hard to see.
- **AI-washing:** Attributing decisions to AI for reputational or strategic reasons.
