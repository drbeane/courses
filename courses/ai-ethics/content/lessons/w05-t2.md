# 05-02 — Entry-Level Work and the Missing Rung

**Module:** 5 — Work, Labor, and Economic Power
**Estimated time:** 35 minutes

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

1. Summarize the evidence on AI and early-career employment, including the Stanford "Canaries" study.
2. Explain why fewer entry-level roles could harm skill formation and long-run labor markets.
3. Evaluate employer, university and policy responses.

## Opening case: the canaries

In August 2025, Erik Brynjolfsson, Bharat Chandar and Ruyu Chen of the Stanford Digital Economy Lab released "Canaries in the Coal Mine? Six Facts About the Recent Employment Effects of Artificial Intelligence." Using monthly payroll data from ADP, the largest U.S. payroll processor, covering millions of workers, they found:

1. **Young workers in exposed jobs:** Employment for workers aged **22–25** in the most AI-exposed occupations (such as software developers and customer-service agents) declined relative to less-exposed occupations after late 2022. The relative decline was about **13%** in the original analysis.
2. **No such decline for older workers** in the same occupations, whose employment was stable or growing.
3. **Automation, not augmentation:** Declines were concentrated in occupations where AI usage patterns suggest it **automates** tasks, not where it **augments** them.
4. **Hiring, not pay:** Adjustment occurred mostly through employment (hiring) rather than wages.

The authors' **August 2026 revision** reported that the gap had widened to about **19%** as of June 2026, and still found no broad, economy-wide displacement. The gap appears to come mainly from **reduced hiring of young workers**, not layoffs.

**Caveats:** The study is descriptive, not causal. Timing and structure are suggestive, but other factors (interest rates, post-pandemic tech over-hiring, offshoring) could contribute. The 19% is a relative comparison, not a count of jobs eliminated.

## Core ideas

### Why entry-level roles are exposed

Entry-level work often consists of tasks that are:

- well-defined and codified (documentation, first-draft writing, basic code, data entry, first-line support),
- abundant in training data, and
- easy to check.

These are precisely the tasks current AI does relatively well. Experienced workers' value often lies in **tacit knowledge**: judgment, relationships, context and handling exceptions. That knowledge is harder to automate.

### The "missing rung" problem

If firms stop hiring juniors because AI can do junior tasks:

- **Skill formation breaks:** Experts are made through years of junior work, so where do future seniors come from?
- **Apprenticeship erodes:** Junior roles are how organizations transmit tacit knowledge.
- **Inequality grows:** People without elite credentials or networks rely most on entry-level openings.
- **The pipeline thins:** Long-run shortages of experienced workers may follow.

This is an example of a **collective action problem**. Each firm saves money by not training juniors, but if all firms do this, the industry loses its future talent.

### Counterarguments and alternative readings

- **The tech hiring correction:** Software hiring boomed in 2020–2022 and then collapsed for reasons including higher interest rates. AI may be blamed for a cyclical correction.
- **New roles:** AI may create new entry-level roles (AI operations, evaluation, prompt and agent workflow roles) that aren't yet captured in occupational codes.
- **Faster ramp-up:** AI may help juniors become productive faster (05-01, the customer-support study), making them *more* valuable.
- **Short window:** Labor-market adjustments take years. Early data may not show the eventual equilibrium.

### Responses

| Actor | Possible responses |
|---|---|
| **Employers** | Maintain junior hiring as an investment; redesign junior roles around AI-assisted work with deliberate mentoring; avoid "experience required" for entry roles |
| **Universities** | Teach AI-augmented work; emphasize judgment, verification and domain depth; expand internships and apprenticeships; be honest with students about labor-market data |
| **Policymakers** | Apprenticeship subsidies; tax incentives for training; better data collection on AI adoption; transition support |
| **Workers / students** | Build skills AI complements; develop portfolios; learn to supervise and verify AI work |

## Applying the frameworks

- **Justice (intergenerational):** Today's productivity gains may be paid for by today's graduates.
- **Decency, self lens:** As a student, how should you prepare without despair or complacency?
- **Rawls:** Behind the veil, not knowing whether you'd be a senior engineer or a new graduate, what policy would you choose?
- **Consequences:** Short-term efficiency vs. long-term capability.

## Discussion questions

1. Should employers have any obligation to hire and train junior workers if AI can do the work more cheaply?
2. How should a CS or data science program change its curriculum in response to this evidence? What should it **not** change?
3. Which explanation for the entry-level decline do you find most convincing (AI, a tech correction or something else)? What evidence would distinguish them?
4. Is "learn to use AI" sufficient advice for new graduates? What else matters?

## Activity: role redesign (15 minutes)

Choose an entry-level role in your field, such as junior data analyst, junior developer or IT support. Redesign it for an AI-assisted workplace:

1. Which tasks does AI handle?
2. Which tasks does the junior do, and how do they learn?
3. What mentoring structure ensures skill growth?
4. How would you measure whether the redesigned role produces future seniors?

## Key terms

- **Tacit knowledge:** Know-how that's hard to articulate or codify.
- **Skill formation:** How workers acquire expertise over time.
- **Collective action problem:** Individually rational choices that are collectively harmful.
- **Relative employment decline:** Change compared with a reference group, not an absolute job count.
