# 05-02 — Entry-Level Work and the Missing Rung

**Module:** 5 — Work, Labor, and Economic Power
**Estimated time:** 35 minutes

**In this lesson:** The clearest early sign of AI's effect on jobs is not mass layoffs. It is fewer young people being hired into their first jobs. This lesson looks at that evidence, why entry-level work is exposed, why losing it could matter far beyond the people who miss out, and what employers, universities and governments can do.

## The Canaries

In August 2025, the economists Erik Brynjolfsson, Bharat Chandar and Ruyu Chen of Stanford's Digital Economy Lab published a study with a memorable title: "Canaries in the Coal Mine?" Miners once carried canaries underground because the birds sickened from bad air before people did. The authors asked whether some workers were serving the same purpose for AI.

They used monthly payroll records from ADP, the largest payroll processor in the United States, covering millions of workers. They found that employment of workers aged 22 to 25 in the occupations most exposed to AI, such as software development and customer service, had fallen relative to workers of the same age in less exposed jobs since late 2022. The relative decline was about 13 percent in the first version of the study. Older workers in the same occupations saw no such decline; their employment was stable or growing. The declines were concentrated in jobs where AI tends to automate tasks, not where it tends to assist with them. And the adjustment came mostly through hiring, not pay: companies hired fewer young people instead of paying them less.

In August 2026 the authors updated the study. The gap had widened to about 19 percent as of June 2026. They still found no broad displacement across the economy. The gap appeared to come mainly from less hiring of young workers, not from layoffs.

The study comes with important cautions. It describes a pattern; it does not prove that AI caused it. The timing and structure are suggestive, but other things could contribute, such as higher interest rates, a correction after technology companies over-hired during the pandemic, and offshoring. And the 19 percent is a comparison between groups, not a count of jobs eliminated.

## Why Entry-Level Work Is Exposed

Entry-level jobs tend to consist of tasks that are well defined and written down, such as documentation, first drafts, basic code, data entry and first-line customer support. Those tasks appear in enormous quantities in the data AI is trained on, and their results are easy to check. They are exactly the tasks current AI does relatively well.

Experienced workers' value often lies elsewhere, in **tacit knowledge**: judgment, relationships, context and the ability to handle the unusual case. That knowledge is hard to write down and hard to automate.

## The Case for Using AI in These Roles

From an employer's point of view, the logic is clear and not malicious. If an AI tool can draft the documentation, write the routine code or answer the common questions, a smaller team can do the same work, and the savings can go to lower prices, higher pay for remaining staff, or the business's survival. Customers may get faster service. And AI can make the juniors who *are* hired productive faster: Lesson 05-03 describes evidence that AI helps the least experienced workers most.

So the question is not whether firms should use AI for routine work. It is what happens to the way people become experienced if the routine work that used to train them disappears.

## The Missing Rung

If firms stop hiring juniors because AI can do junior tasks, several things follow.

Skill formation breaks. Experts are made through years of junior work. If nobody does that work, where do the next generation's senior engineers, lawyers and analysts come from?

Apprenticeship erodes. Junior roles are how organizations pass on tacit knowledge, through mentoring, correction and watching others.

Inequality grows. People without elite credentials or personal connections depend most on ordinary entry-level openings.

And the pipeline thins. A shortage of experienced workers may follow years later.

This is a **collective action problem**: a situation in which each party acting sensibly for itself produces a result that is bad for all. Each firm saves money by not training juniors. If every firm does the same, the whole industry loses its future talent, and every firm suffers.

## Other Explanations

There are serious reasons to doubt that AI is the main cause, or that the effect will last.

Software hiring boomed in 2020 to 2022 and then collapsed for reasons that included higher interest rates. AI may be getting blamed for a correction that would have happened anyway.

AI may be creating new entry-level jobs, in operating, evaluating and supervising AI systems, that existing job categories do not yet capture.

AI may make juniors more valuable, not less, by helping them become productive sooner.

And labor markets take years to adjust. Early data may not show where things settle.

## Responses

Different parties can act.

| Who | What they can do |
|---|---|
| Employers | Keep hiring juniors as an investment; redesign junior roles around AI-assisted work with deliberate mentoring; stop demanding experience for entry-level jobs |
| Universities | Teach how to work with AI; emphasize judgment, verification and depth in a field; expand internships and apprenticeships; be honest with students about labor-market data |
| Governments | Subsidize apprenticeships; offer tax incentives for training; collect better data on AI adoption; support people through transitions |
| Students and workers | Build skills that AI complements; develop portfolios of real work; learn to supervise and check AI's output |

## Where People Disagree

Do employers have any obligation to hire and train junior workers if AI can do the work more cheaply? One view says no: a firm's duty is to its customers, workers and owners, and training is a public problem for public policy. Another view says that firms that benefit from a trained workforce, and from the training other firms provided to their senior staff, owe something back.

Which explanation for the decline is most convincing: AI, the end of a hiring boom, or something else? What evidence would tell them apart?

And is "learn to use AI" sufficient advice for new graduates? It is necessary. Whether it is enough depends on whether employers create roles in which juniors can still learn.

## Check Your Understanding

**1. What did the Stanford "Canaries" study find in its August 2026 revision?**

:::answer Answer
Employment of 22-to-25-year-olds in the most AI-exposed occupations was about 19 percent below the trend for similar workers in less exposed jobs, mainly through less hiring, with no broad economy-wide displacement.
:::

**2. Why does the study not prove that AI caused the decline?**

:::answer Answer
It is descriptive. Other factors, such as interest rates, a post-pandemic hiring correction and offshoring, could contribute, and the figure is a relative comparison, not a count of jobs lost.
:::

**3. Why are entry-level tasks especially exposed to AI?**

:::answer Answer
They are well defined, abundant in training data and easy to check, which is what current AI does well. Experienced workers' value lies more in tacit knowledge.
:::

**4. Why is the missing rung a collective action problem?**

:::answer Answer
Each firm saves money by not training juniors, but if all firms do so, the industry runs short of experienced workers, which harms every firm.
:::

## Key Terms

- **Tacit knowledge:** Know-how that is hard to put into words or write down.
- **Skill formation:** The process by which workers acquire expertise over time.
- **Collective action problem:** A situation in which choices that make sense for each party are harmful for all.
- **Relative employment decline:** A change measured against a comparison group, not an absolute count of jobs.
