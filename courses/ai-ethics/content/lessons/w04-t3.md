# 04-03 — Deepfakes: Capabilities and Harms

**Module:** 4 — Synthetic Media, Information Integrity, and Human Agency
**Estimated time:** 35 minutes

**In this lesson:** Software can now make a convincing video or voice recording of a real person doing or saying something they never did. This lesson explains what the technology can do, who it is used against, what it is also good for, and the questions that decide where the line falls.

## The Video Call That Cost $25 Million

In early 2024, police in Hong Kong reported that a finance employee at the engineering firm Arup had transferred about HK$200 million, roughly US$25 million, to fraudsters. The employee had first received an email, apparently from the company's chief financial officer in the UK, asking for a confidential transaction. The employee was suspicious. Then came a video conference. On screen were the chief financial officer and several colleagues the employee recognized, looking and sounding as they always did. The employee made fifteen transfers.

Every other person on that call was a fake, generated from video and audio of the real people that was publicly available online. Arup confirmed the incident in May 2024.

The suspicious email had been the right instinct. The video call overrode it. For most of history, seeing and hearing someone was the strongest evidence that they were really there.

## What the Technology Can Do

A **deepfake** is synthetic media that realistically shows a real person doing or saying something they did not. By 2026 the main capabilities looked like this.

| Kind | What it can do | What it needs |
|---|---|---|
| Voice cloning | Speak realistically in a particular person's voice, in real time and in several languages | Seconds to minutes of recorded speech |
| Face swapping | Replace one face with another on live video, including on a video call | A few photos or a short video |
| Lip-syncing | Make a person in a real video appear to say new words | The video and the new audio |
| Fully synthetic video | Generate realistic scenes and people from a text description | A text prompt, sometimes with reference images |
| Photo editing | Make photorealistic changes to a real photo, including "undressing" a clothed person | A single photo |

Two developments made deepfakes a mass problem rather than a specialist one. The cost and skill required fell to almost nothing: real-time face and voice swapping now runs on ordinary consumer hardware. And the amount of material needed fell: a few seconds of someone's voice from a social media video can be enough to clone it.

Detection has not kept up. Studies have found that people's ability to tell high-quality synthetic faces and voices from real ones is close to chance. One 2022 study found that people not only failed to identify AI-generated faces but rated them as slightly more trustworthy than real ones. Automated detectors work less well on content from new generation methods they were not trained on. Lesson 04-06 covers detection and its alternatives.

## Who Is Targeted

The harms fall into several kinds.

| Harm | What it is | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Fraud | Impersonation for money or access | The Arup case; fake calls from a "CEO"; calls to grandparents in a cloned grandchild's voice; fooling banks' voice authentication |
| Sexual abuse | Non-consensual intimate imagery | "Nudify" apps; the Grok case (Lesson 04-04) |
| Reputational | False depictions that damage someone | In 2024 a school athletic director near Baltimore was charged over a fake audio recording of the principal making racist remarks |
| Political | Fabricated statements by candidates; misleading voters | A cloned-voice robocall in the 2024 New Hampshire primary (Lesson 04-05) |
| Epistemic | Undermining trust in all evidence | Real recordings dismissed as fakes |
| Harassment | Content meant to mock or humiliate | Cases in schools worldwide |
| Identity | Commercial use of a person's face or voice without permission | Fake celebrity endorsements in scam ads; disputes over cloned voices of voice actors |

The targets are not random. Sexual deepfakes overwhelmingly target women and girls: studies since 2019 have consistently found that most deepfake content online is non-consensual pornography, almost all of it depicting women. Voice-cloning scams disproportionately target older people. Public figures are targeted for political and reputational fakes, but ordinary people are increasingly targeted too, because so little material is needed. Employees with access to money are targets for fraud.

## What It Is Also Good For

The same technology has real and growing value, and a fair analysis has to count it.

It can give people back their voices. People losing the ability to speak, for example to ALS, can record their voice in advance or have it reconstructed from old recordings, and then speak in something close to their own voice through a device. Lesson 04-07 looks at this in detail.

It makes film and media accessible across languages. Dubbing can now match a speaker's own voice and lip movements in another language.

It can protect people. The 2020 documentary *Welcome to Chechnya*, about the persecution of gay people in Chechnya, used face replacement to disguise the people who told their stories, protecting them from reprisal while keeping their expressions visible.

It supports education and art: reconstructions of historical figures, clearly labeled, and satire and parody, which have a long tradition and strong legal protection in many countries.

## Where the Line Falls

Because the technology itself is neither good nor bad, the ethical questions are about particular uses. Four questions usually decide them.

**Did the person depicted consent?** Restoring someone's own voice at their request and cloning a stranger's voice to defraud their family use the same technique. Consent is the difference.

**Will viewers be deceived?** An obvious parody that nobody believes is different from a fake designed to be taken for real.

**What is the purpose, and what harm results?** Protecting a source in a documentary and humiliating a classmate are different acts.

**Is it labeled?** Clear labeling can turn a deceptive use into an honest one, though labels can be removed.

The hardest cases sit where these answers conflict: a satirical fake of a politician that is labeled but realistic enough to fool people who see it without the label, or a film that recreates a dead actor's performance with the family's consent but not the actor's.

:::answer Answer
Any two of these: restoring the voices of people who have lost speech; dubbing that matches speakers' voices across languages; disguising vulnerable sources in documentaries; labeled historical reconstructions; satire and parody.

## Key Terms

- **Deepfake:** Synthetic media that realistically depicts a real person doing or saying something they did not.
- **Voice cloning:** Synthesizing speech in a particular person's voice.
- **Non-consensual intimate imagery:** Sexual or intimate images of a person shared or created without their consent, whether real or synthetic.
- **Right of publicity:** The legal right to control commercial use of one's name, image and likeness.
