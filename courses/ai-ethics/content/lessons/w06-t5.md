# 06-05 — Efficiency, Rebound, and AI for Climate

**Module:** 6 — Environmental Costs and Global Access
**Estimated time:** 35 minutes

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

1. Explain the rebound effect (the Jevons paradox) and how it applies to AI efficiency gains.
2. Evaluate claims that AI will deliver net climate benefits.
3. Distinguish well-evidenced climate applications of AI from speculative ones.

## Opening case: cheaper, so more

In January 2025, the Chinese lab DeepSeek released R1, a reasoning model it said was trained at a fraction of the cost of comparable U.S. models. NVIDIA's stock fell about 17% in one day on fears that AI would need less hardware.

Microsoft CEO Satya Nadella posted: "Jevons paradox strikes again! As AI gets more efficient and accessible, we will see its use skyrocket." Within months, AI companies' capital spending plans *rose*.

Per-prompt energy at Google fell 33× in a year (06-02). Global data-center electricity is still projected to roughly double by 2030 (06-01). Both can be true at once.

## Core ideas

### The rebound effect

In *The Coal Question* (1865), William Stanley Jevons observed that more efficient steam engines **increased** total coal consumption, because cheaper energy services expanded use.

The **rebound effect** has degrees:

- **Partial rebound:** Efficiency saves some energy, but less than expected.
- **Full rebound:** Savings are completely offset.
- **Backfire (the Jevons paradox):** Total use increases.

For AI, efficiency gains (cheaper inference, smaller models) lower prices and enable new uses: AI in every app, agents running continuously, video generation. **Whether total energy rises depends on demand elasticity.** Early evidence suggests strong demand growth.

**Implication:** Efficiency is necessary but not sufficient. Absolute limits or prices on carbon, water and energy may be needed to constrain totals.

### AI for climate: the case

AI can help reduce emissions and adapt to climate change:

| Application | Evidence level |
|---|---|
| **Weather forecasting** (Google DeepMind GraphCast, ECMWF's AIFS, operational since 2025) | Strong. AI models match or beat traditional forecasts at far lower compute cost. |
| **Grid optimization and renewable integration** | Moderate to strong. Forecasting wind and solar output, demand response. |
| **Materials discovery** (batteries, catalysts) | Promising but early. Discovered candidates still need lab validation. |
| **Building energy management** | Moderate. DeepMind reported 30–40% cooling-energy reductions in Google data centers (2016–2018). |
| **Methane leak detection** from satellites | Strong (e.g., MethaneSAT, Carbon Mapper). |
| **Deforestation monitoring** | Strong (e.g., Global Forest Watch). |
| **Climate modeling** | Emerging (AI emulators of climate models). |

### AI for climate: the critique

- **Speculative offsets:** Claims that AI will reduce global emissions by 5–10% by 2030 (e.g., BCG/Google 2023) are **forecasts** often produced by interested parties.
- **AI for fossil fuels:** Oil and gas companies use AI for exploration and production efficiency, sometimes with the same cloud providers who tout climate benefits (Microsoft and others have faced employee and investor criticism).
- **Who builds what:** Most AI investment goes to consumer and enterprise products, not climate applications.
- **Accounting asymmetry:** Emissions from AI are certain and present. Avoided emissions are hypothetical and future.
- **Small models suffice:** Many high-value climate applications (forecasting, monitoring) use specialized models far smaller than frontier chatbots.

### A balanced position

1. AI's direct footprint is real and growing, but still a modest share of global emissions (about 0.5% of global CO₂ from data centers in UNU's 2025 estimate, with AI a fraction of that).
2. Some AI applications provide real, measured climate benefits.
3. Neither fact justifies the other. Each application should be evaluated on its own costs and benefits.
4. Claims of net benefit should be labeled as forecasts and scrutinized.

## Applying the frameworks

- **Consequentialism:** The right comparison is not "AI vs. no AI" but each use's specific costs and benefits.
- **Justice (intergenerational):** Future people bear the cost of today's emissions.
- **Evidence discipline:** Measured present costs vs. forecast future benefits.
- **Decency, world lens:** The planet as a stakeholder.

## Discussion questions

1. If efficiency gains increase total AI energy use, should we still celebrate efficiency? What else is needed?
2. Should AI companies' claims of climate benefits be subject to the same scrutiny as "greenwashing" in other industries?
3. Is it ethical for cloud providers to sell AI services to oil and gas exploration while marketing climate benefits?
4. Should public funding prioritize climate-focused AI over general-purpose models?

## Activity: claim evaluation (15 minutes)

Find one corporate or consultancy claim that AI will reduce emissions by a specific amount. Identify:

1. who made it and any interest they have,
2. whether it's measured, estimated or forecast,
3. what assumptions it depends on, and
4. whether rebound effects are considered.

## Key terms

- **Rebound effect / Jevons paradox:** Efficiency gains increasing total consumption.
- **Demand elasticity:** How much demand responds to price changes.
- **Avoided emissions:** Emissions prevented relative to a counterfactual (hard to verify).
- **Greenwashing:** Misleading environmental claims.
