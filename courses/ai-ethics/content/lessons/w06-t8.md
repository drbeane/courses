# 06-08 — Case Workshop: The Data Center Next Door

**Module:** 6 — Environmental Costs and Global Access
**Estimated time:** 75 minutes

**In this lesson:** You will advise a county commission on whether to approve a large AI data center, using everything from this module: sorting claims by how well they are supported, weighing who gains and who pays, judging the fairness of the process, and writing conditions that could actually be enforced. This workshop is the model for option (b) in A3.

## Scenario: Project Juniper

The scenario is fictional, but each element is drawn from real cases.

Halsey County has 48,000 residents and sits in a semi-arid region where the main aquifer is falling. Its median household income is below the state's. A developer working under the code name "Project Juniper," widely believed to represent a major cloud company, proposes a 300-megawatt AI data-center campus on 600 acres of former farmland, two miles from the town of Merritt.

**The developer says** the project will bring $4 billion in investment; 1,500 construction jobs over three years and 75 permanent jobs; $18 million a year in property taxes after a 15-year tax break that reduces taxes by 70 percent; "closed-loop cooling with minimal water use after the initial fill"; "100 percent renewable energy" through power purchase agreements; and $2 million for a community fund.

**Other information has emerged.** Serving the campus requires a new substation and transmission upgrades estimated at $300 million. How that cost is divided is still being negotiated, and residential customers might pay part of it. The utility plans to meet near-term demand partly with a new gas-fired power plant. The county water district says that in summer heat, closed-loop systems may need extra evaporative cooling, and the developer has not released any water projections. Backup diesel generators totaling about 300 megawatts would be tested monthly. The county commissioners signed a nondisclosure agreement with the developer eight months before the first public hearing. Merritt residents, about 30 percent of whom are Hispanic, say they learned about the project from a news leak.

**Local groups have taken sides.** The school district supports the project because of the eventual tax revenue. A farmers' cooperative opposes it over water. A building-trades union supports it. A wildlife group points out that the site borders a corridor used by migrating birds.

## Part 1: Claims Inventory (20 minutes)

List at least ten claims made by the developer, the utility, residents and advocates. For each, record who made it; whether it is measured, estimated, forecast or speculative; what evidence is available; and what would verify it.

## Part 2: Who Gains and Who Pays (20 minutes)

For at least six of these groups, list the benefits, the burdens, when each arrives (now or later), and how much power the group has to influence the decision: Merritt residents; county taxpayers generally; farmers; construction workers; electricity customers across the state; the cloud company's customers around the world; future residents; and local wildlife. Use the four kinds of justice from Lesson 01-06.

## Part 3: Was the Process Fair? (10 minutes)

Evaluate the decision process using Arnstein's ladder of participation from Lesson 01-08 and the idea of procedural justice. What would a fair process have looked like, and what would it have cost the county and the developer?

## Part 4: Recommendation (20 minutes)

As a consultant to the county commission, recommend one of four options: approve, approve with conditions, defer until more information is available, or deny.

If you recommend conditions, give at least five that could be enforced, for example on reporting and capping water use, dividing the cost of grid upgrades, limiting generator testing, hiring locally, **clawbacks** that require incentives to be repaid if promises about jobs or taxes are not kept, buffers to protect habitat, and public disclosure.

Whatever you recommend, state what the county gives up by your choice, and what evidence would change your recommendation.

## Part 5: Reflection (5 minutes)

Which ethical framework most shaped your recommendation? Whose interests were hardest to weigh?

## Check Your Understanding

**1. Why should the "100 percent renewable" claim be examined carefully?**

:::answer Answer
It is probably based on market-based accounting through power purchase agreements, while the campus's actual demand is partly met by a new gas plant.
:::

**2. Why is the $18 million in annual tax revenue less than it first appears?**

:::answer Answer
It applies only after a 15-year tax break that cuts taxes by 70 percent, so most of the revenue is deferred for a long time.
:::

**3. What is a clawback?**

:::answer Answer
A contract term requiring a company to repay incentives, such as tax breaks, if it does not deliver what it promised, such as jobs or tax revenue.
:::

## Key Terms

- **Tax abatement:** A temporary reduction in property taxes offered to attract investment.
- **Clawback:** A contract term requiring repayment of incentives if promises are not met.
- **Cost allocation:** How the costs of grid upgrades are divided among customers.
