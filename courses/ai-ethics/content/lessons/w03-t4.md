# 03-04 — AI Interviews, Personality Inference, and Disability

**Module:** 3 — Algorithmic Bias in Consequential Decisions
**Estimated time:** 35 minutes

**In this lesson:** Some hiring tools go beyond reading résumés. They interview candidates, score their voices and faces, or have them play games. This lesson asks whether these tools measure anything real, how they can shut out disabled applicants, and whether they might still be fairer than the human interviews they replace.

## The Interviewer That Read Your Face

From the mid-2010s, HireVue, one of the largest providers of video interviewing software, offered employers an AI that analyzed candidates' recorded answers. It looked at facial expressions, word choice and features of the voice, and produced scores meant to predict job performance. Millions of candidates were assessed this way.

In 2019 the Electronic Privacy Information Center, a U.S. advocacy group, filed a complaint with the Federal Trade Commission arguing that the facial analysis was unfair, deceptive and unsupported by science. In January 2021 HireVue announced that it had stopped analyzing faces, citing an independent audit and public concern. It continued to analyze speech and language.

By 2026 a newer kind of tool had become common in high-volume hiring: the AI interviewer, a conversational system that conducts an entire first-round interview by voice or video. Some vendors say these tools assess "soft skills," "communication" or "culture fit."

## The Case for Automated Interviews

The appeal of these tools rests on real problems with human interviewing.

Unstructured interviews, in which an interviewer chats with a candidate and forms an impression, are one of the weakest ways to choose employees. Decades of research in organizational psychology show they predict job performance poorly and are easily swayed by appearance, accent, confidence and similarity to the interviewer. **Structured interviews**, which ask every candidate the same job-related questions and score the answers against fixed criteria, do much better. An AI interviewer can, in principle, be perfectly structured: same questions, same scoring, every time.

Automated interviews are also available at any hour, which helps candidates who work shifts or care for children. They let employers interview everyone who applies, instead of only those whose résumés catch a recruiter's eye. And some candidates prefer them, finding a recorded answer less stressful than a live conversation with a stranger.

So the question is not whether AI interviews are perfect. It is whether a given tool actually captures what makes structured interviews work, or adds new problems of its own.

## Does It Measure What It Claims?

Psychologists judge a selection tool by its **validity**. It should have **construct validity**, meaning it measures a real trait that matters for the job. It should have **criterion validity**, meaning its scores actually predict how people perform once hired. And it should predict equally well for different groups.

Many AI assessment tools fall short on these tests.

The scientific basis for reading emotion or personality from faces is weak. A major review in 2019 found that facial expressions do not reliably reveal what people feel, a point Lesson 02-09 made. A tool trained to find "enthusiasm" in a face may be finding something else entirely.

The features a model uses can be irrelevant. In 2021, journalists at the German broadcaster Bayerischer Rundfunk tested one video assessment tool. The personality scores changed when the same actor put on glasses or a headscarf, and when a bookshelf appeared in the background.

Game-based assessments claim to measure thinking and emotional traits, but many of the studies supporting them were run by the vendors and never published.

And a tool trained to find candidates who resemble a company's current employees will reproduce the current workforce, whatever its strengths and gaps.

## Screened Out

The most serious and least visible harm falls on disabled applicants.

The Americans with Disabilities Act prohibits selection criteria that **screen out** people with disabilities unless the criteria are job-related and necessary for the business. It also requires **reasonable accommodation** during the application process: adjustments that give disabled applicants an equal chance.

AI interviews can screen people out in ways that nobody intends and nobody sees.

| What the tool measures | Who it can disadvantage |
|---|---|
| Eye contact or facial expression | Autistic people; blind people; people with facial differences or paralysis |
| Pace, fluency and tone of speech | People who stutter; deaf speakers; people with speech disabilities; non-native speakers |
| Timed games and reaction speed | People with motor, visual or processing disabilities |
| Gaps in employment | People who took medical leave |
| Personality "fit" | Neurodivergent people; people with psychiatric disabilities |

The harm is hard to detect for two reasons. A rejected applicant does not know which feature caused the rejection. And disability is rarely recorded, so audits like New York City's, which cover only sex and race, cannot find it.

In 2022 the Equal Employment Opportunity Commission issued guidance on the disability law and AI hiring tools. It advised employers to tell applicants when AI is used, to offer accommodations and alternatives, and to prefer tools that measure job skills directly over tools that infer traits. Some of the commission's AI guidance was withdrawn in 2025, but the obligations in the law itself remain.

## Performing for the Machine

Even a tool that measured something real would raise questions about dignity, of the kind Lesson 01-04 described.

Candidates now coach themselves to perform for algorithms: smile more, look into the lens, use the right keywords. Communication becomes a performance aimed at a system nobody can see. An AI interview also removes the reciprocity of a human conversation. The candidate cannot read the interviewer, ask what a question means, or make a case to someone who might be persuaded. Many people report feeling judged by a system they cannot reason with, and that experience is a cost in its own right.

## The Law

Several laws now address these tools directly, as of October 2026. The EU AI Act prohibits emotion recognition in the workplace, including in hiring, and treats AI used for recruitment as high-risk, with obligations that the 2026 amendments delayed to December 2027. Illinois's Artificial Intelligence Video Interview Act, in force since 2020, requires employers that use AI to analyze video interviews to notify applicants, explain how the AI works, get consent and delete recordings on request. Maryland requires consent before facial recognition is used in an interview.

## Where People Disagree

The core disagreement is whether AI interviews are a step toward fairer hiring or away from it.

One side holds that the tools are, at their best, structured interviews done at scale, with consistency no human panel can match, and that their biases are at least testable in a way an individual interviewer's are not. On this view the right response is to require validation, testing for disability effects and accommodation, not to ban the tools.

The other side holds that many of these tools measure the wrong things, that the people most likely to be wrongly screened out are the least able to discover it, and that the loss of a human exchange is itself a harm. On this view employers should at least be required to offer a non-AI alternative to anyone who asks.

A harder case tests both positions. Suppose a tool measures enthusiasm, and enthusiasm really does predict sales performance, but the tool disadvantages some autistic applicants. Is it acceptable? The disability law would ask whether the criterion is truly necessary for the job and whether an accommodation is possible. You will need to decide what you think.

## Check Your Understanding

**1. What is the difference between construct validity and criterion validity?**

:::answer Answer
Construct validity means the tool measures a real, job-relevant trait. Criterion validity means its scores actually predict job performance.
:::

**2. Why can structured interviews be fairer than unstructured ones?**

:::answer Answer
They ask everyone the same job-related questions and score answers against fixed criteria, which predicts performance better and reduces the influence of appearance, accent and similarity to the interviewer.
:::

**3. Why is disability discrimination by AI interviews hard to detect?**

:::answer Answer
Rejected applicants do not know which feature caused the decision, and disability is rarely recorded, so audits by sex and race cannot catch it.
:::

**4. What does it mean for a criterion to "screen out" disabled applicants?**

:::answer Answer
It excludes people with disabilities from consideration. Under the U.S. disability law, that is unlawful unless the criterion is job-related and necessary for the business.
:::

## Key Terms

- **Structured interview:** An interview with the same job-related questions and scoring for every candidate.
- **Construct validity:** The extent to which a tool measures a real, relevant trait.
- **Criterion validity:** The extent to which a tool's scores predict actual outcomes such as job performance.
- **Screen out:** To exclude people with disabilities through a selection criterion.
- **Reasonable accommodation:** An adjustment that gives a disabled person an equal opportunity, including when applying for a job.
