# 05-08 — Automation Bias and Professional Responsibility

**Module:** 5 — Work, Labor, and Economic Power
**Estimated time:** 35 minutes

**In this lesson:** People tend to trust machines too much, and the more reliable a machine is, the less closely they watch it. This lesson explains that tendency, shows where it has caused harm in medicine, aviation and law, and sets out what professionals owe the people who rely on them when they use AI.

## The Idea: We Defer to Machines

In a 2023 study published in the journal *Radiology*, Thomas Dratsch and colleagues asked radiologists to read mammograms with the help of what they were told was an AI system. In some cases the system suggested the correct category; in others it deliberately suggested a wrong one. When the AI was wrong, the radiologists' accuracy fell sharply. Less experienced radiologists were affected most, but even experienced readers were pulled toward the wrong answer.

Set this beside the Swedish mammography trial in Lesson 03-08, in which an AI system helped radiologists find about 20 percent more cancers. Both studies are about AI in breast screening. One shows what AI can add. The other shows the risk that comes with it: people tend to go along with what a machine suggests, even against their own judgment and training.

That tendency is called **automation bias**. The psychologists Raja Parasuraman and Dietrich Manzey describe it as favoring automated suggestions and discounting information that contradicts them. It produces two kinds of error. **Errors of commission** are following advice that is wrong. **Errors of omission** are failing to notice a problem because the system did not flag it.

A related tendency is **automation complacency**: watching a reliable system less closely. The more reliable a system is, the less carefully people monitor it, until the moment it fails.

## Why It Persuades

Automation bias explains a pattern that shows up across very different fields. It also explains why "a human reviews every decision," the safeguard Lesson 03-09 examined, so often fails.

Several conditions make it worse: heavy workloads and time pressure; a system that is usually right, which paradoxically increases complacency; the authority people attach to the word "AI"; lack of training on how the system fails; and incentives in organizations that make following the system safe and overriding it risky, since overriding has to be justified and following does not.

## Where It Has Done Harm

The pattern appears in many professions.

| Field | Example |
|---|---|
| Aviation | Air France Flight 447 in 2009, where pilots unpracticed in flying by hand mishandled a situation after the autopilot disconnected; the Boeing 737 MAX crashes in 2018 and 2019, where an automated system and inadequate pilot training contributed |
| Medicine | Radiologists swayed by wrong AI suggestions; a widely used sepsis-prediction tool that, when independently tested in 2021, missed many cases and produced many false alarms |
| Law | Lawyers filing AI-invented citations (Lesson 04-01); judges and risk scores (Lesson 03-06) |
| Policing | Facial recognition matches treated as proof (Lesson 02-04) |
| Software | In a 2023 study, programmers using an AI assistant wrote less secure code while believing it was more secure |
| Navigation | Drivers following GPS directions into lakes or closed roads |

## Losing the Skill

Long-term reliance can also wear away skill, a process called **deskilling**. Pilots who rarely fly by hand lose some of the ability to do it. A 2025 study in *The Lancet Gastroenterology & Hepatology* reported that doctors who had routinely used AI assistance in colonoscopies detected fewer precancerous growths when they later worked without it. And Lesson 05-03 described students who did worse on an exam after practicing with an unrestricted AI tutor.

Deskilling matters ethically because it makes professionals less able to catch the machine's errors at exactly the moment it matters, and it shifts power to whoever supplies the tool.

## What Professionals Owe

Professional codes are adapting, and they agree on one central point: **AI does not transfer professional responsibility.** The professional who relies on AI remains accountable for the result.

In medicine, the American Medical Association's principles on what it calls "augmented intelligence" stress that physicians remain responsible and must oversee the tools. In law, the American Bar Association's Formal Opinion 512, issued in 2024, applies lawyers' duties of competence, confidentiality, communication, candor and reasonable fees to the use of generative AI. In computing, the ACM Code of Ethics requires professionals to give comprehensive and thorough evaluations of computer systems and their impacts, including possible risks, and to avoid harm. Nursing organizations have said that nurses remain accountable for clinical judgments and must be able to override AI.

## Designing Against It

Organizations can reduce automation bias through design.

| Design choice | What it does |
|---|---|
| Show how confident the system is | Helps people trust it the right amount, if the confidence is itself accurate |
| Ask for the person's judgment before showing the AI's | Reduces **anchoring**, the pull of the first answer seen |
| Explain the basis for each suggestion | Lets people evaluate it critically |
| Train people on how the system fails | Builds appropriate skepticism |
| Track overrides and their outcomes | Reveals both too much and too little reliance |
| Keep people practicing without the tool | Prevents deskilling |
| Align incentives | Do not penalize justified overrides |

The goal is **appropriate reliance**: trusting the system when it is right and not when it is wrong.

## Where It Breaks

The emphasis on automation bias has its own risks.

It can make people distrust tools that are, in fact, better than they are. Lesson 03-08 described algorithm aversion: people abandoning a system after seeing it make one mistake, even when it makes fewer mistakes overall than they do. Too much skepticism costs lives too.

It can put all the responsibility on the individual professional, when the real failures are in how organizations design workflows, set workloads and buy tools.

And "keep a human in charge" can become a formality that makes everyone feel safe without making anyone safer.

## Where People Disagree

If an AI system is more accurate than the average doctor, is it ethical for a doctor to override it? Is it ethical *not* to? There may be no general answer, only an answer for each tool and each kind of case.

When a professional follows an AI recommendation that turns out to be wrong, who is responsible: the professional, the institution that adopted the tool, or the company that built it? Module 8 takes up this question of many hands.

And should professionals have to tell their clients and patients when they use AI? Disclosure respects autonomy. Critics say it is meaningless if everyone uses AI for everything.

## Check Your Understanding

**1. What is the difference between errors of commission and errors of omission in automation bias?**

:::answer Answer
Errors of commission are following automated advice that is wrong. Errors of omission are failing to notice a problem because the system did not flag it.
:::

**2. Why can a more reliable system increase the risk of complacency?**

:::answer Answer
The more often a system is right, the less closely people monitor it, so they are less likely to catch the rare failure.
:::

**3. What is deskilling, and why does it matter ethically?**

:::answer Answer
The loss of skills through reliance on automation. It leaves professionals less able to catch the machine's errors when it matters, and shifts power to tool providers.
:::

**4. Name two design choices that reduce automation bias.**

:::answer Answer
Any two of these: showing the system's confidence; asking for the person's judgment before showing the AI's; explaining each suggestion; training on failure modes; tracking overrides and outcomes; keeping people practicing without the tool; not penalizing justified overrides.
:::

## Key Terms

- **Automation bias:** The tendency to favor automated suggestions and discount contradictory information.
- **Automation complacency:** Reduced vigilance in monitoring a reliable automated system.
- **Deskilling:** The loss of skills through reliance on automation.
- **Anchoring:** Giving too much weight to the first information seen, such as an AI's suggestion.
- **Appropriate reliance:** Trusting a system when it is right and not when it is wrong.
