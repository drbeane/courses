# 02-09 — Inference and Profiling: What AI Can Guess About You

**Module:** 2 — Surveillance, Biometrics, and Privacy
**Estimated time:** 35 minutes

**In this lesson:** You do not have to tell a company something for it to know it. This lesson explains how models work out sensitive facts from harmless-looking data, why "anonymous" data so often is not, and the two separate problems with inference: when it works, and when it does not.

## The Model Knew First

In 2012 the *New York Times* published a story about Target's statisticians. They had built a "pregnancy prediction" score from purchases of items such as unscented lotion, supplements and cotton balls. The story included an anecdote: a father complained to a store that Target was mailing his teenage daughter coupons for baby products, and later learned that she was pregnant.

That anecdote may be embellished, and data scientists have questioned its details. But the capability it describes is real, and it has grown enormously since. Today a model can estimate far more than pregnancy from far less than a shopping basket.

## How Inference Works

A machine learning model finds patterns in data. Some of those patterns connect what people do in public with things they never disclosed. A variable that can be observed and that is correlated with something that cannot be observed is called a **proxy**. When a model uses proxies to estimate a sensitive attribute, it is making an **inference**.

Research has shown how far this goes. A 2013 study found that Facebook "likes" alone could predict sexual orientation, political views, religion and personality traits with considerable accuracy. Location data from phones reveals home and work addresses, daily routines and the places people visit. Purchases can suggest pregnancy or financial trouble. Writing style can identify an author. Some research has linked patterns in typing or voice to health conditions, though the reliability of those claims varies widely.

Large language models have made this cheaper. A 2023 study found that leading models could infer a person's location, income, age and other attributes from ordinary comments posted on Reddit, with high accuracy and at a small fraction of the time and cost a human analyst would need. Anyone who writes online now leaves clues that a model can assemble.

## Why Inference Is Valuable

Inference is not inherently sinister, and much of its use is welcome.

Doctors and researchers use it to spot disease early: patterns in scans, blood tests or health records that predict a condition before symptoms appear. Banks use it to detect fraud by noticing when a transaction does not fit a customer's habits. Recommendation systems use it to suggest a book or a song you are likely to enjoy. Public health agencies use aggregated location data to track the spread of disease. And researchers use it to study society, finding, for example, which neighborhoods lack access to services.

The ethical question is which inferences, about whom, used for what. A model that predicts that you will like a novel is very different from one that predicts that you are gay, in a country where that is a crime.

## Anonymous Data Usually Is Not

A common response to privacy concerns is to remove names and call the data anonymous. The history of this practice is a series of failures.

In the late 1990s, the computer scientist Latanya Sweeney showed that about 87 percent of Americans could be uniquely identified by just three facts: their ZIP code, birth date and sex. She used those facts to find the governor of Massachusetts in a set of hospital records that had been released as anonymous.

In 2006, AOL released supposedly anonymous search logs. Reporters identified one user, a woman in Georgia, from the content of her searches.

In 2008, two researchers showed that they could identify people in an anonymous dataset of Netflix movie ratings by matching it against public reviews on another website.

In 2013, a study of mobile phone data found that just four points of location and time were enough to identify 95 percent of the people in the dataset uniquely.

Linking "anonymous" data back to a specific person is called **re-identification**. AI makes it easier, because models can match people across datasets by their writing style, faces, way of walking or patterns of behavior.

## The Second Problem: When It Does Not Work

There is a separate problem that is easy to confuse with the first. Some claimed inferences have little or no scientific basis.

Emotion recognition is the clearest example. Many products claim to detect emotions from facial expressions. A major 2019 review of the evidence found that facial expressions are not reliable indicators of what people feel, across people or across cultures. A scowl can mean concentration as easily as anger. The EU AI Act now prohibits emotion recognition in workplaces and schools, except for medical or safety reasons.

Some published research has claimed to detect criminality or sexual orientation from photos of faces. Critics showed that the results were largely explained by differences in where the photos came from, how people groomed themselves and how they posed. These claims echo **physiognomy**, the discredited practice of judging character from appearance. Personality assessments based on video interviews have similar problems, which Module 3 covers.

So there are two distinct ethical problems. When inference works, it can violate privacy by revealing what people chose not to share. When it does not work but is used anyway, it harms people by making false judgments about them. Both can happen at once: a system can be accurate enough to be useful on average and wrong about you.

## Harm to Groups

Inference can harm groups as well as individuals. A model that predicts sexual orientation, religion or immigration status can be used to target a whole community, even if nobody's personal data was ever leaked. In some countries an inferred sexual orientation or political view is a danger to a person's liberty or life. **Group privacy** is the idea that groups have interests in not being profiled collectively, which individual consent cannot protect, since no individual's data is needed to profile the group.

## What the Law Says

Is an inference about you personal data? Under the European GDPR, generally yes, if it relates to an identifiable person. California's privacy law expressly includes "inferences drawn" to build a profile. In principle, then, people have rights to see and correct inferences about them.

In practice those rights are weak. Inferences are often not disclosed. They are expressed as probabilities, such as "72 percent likely," and it is not clear what it would mean to correct a probability. Companies may treat them as trade secrets.

Two scholars, Sandra Wachter and Brent Mittelstadt, have proposed a "right to reasonable inferences": high-risk inferences would have to be shown to be relevant, reliable and acceptable before they could be used to make decisions about people.

## Where People Disagree

One position holds that some inferences should be prohibited outright. Companies should not be allowed to infer health conditions, sexual orientation or religion at all, whether or not they act on the result, because the inference itself is the violation and its existence creates risk.

Another holds that the problem lies in use, not in inference. Medical research, fraud detection and public health all depend on inferring sensitive things, and a ban on inference would block them. On this view the law should prohibit harmful uses, such as discriminating on the basis of an inferred trait, and leave the rest alone.

A related dispute concerns research. Is it ethical to publish a study showing that a sensitive inference is possible? Doing so warns the public and regulators. It also shows others how to do it. Researchers who publish such work now often discuss their choices about what to release.

## Key Terms

- **Inference:** A conclusion about a person derived from data about them.
- **Proxy:** An observable variable correlated with an unobserved attribute.
- **Re-identification:** Linking "anonymous" data back to a specific person.
- **Group privacy:** The interest of a group in not being profiled collectively.
- **Physiognomy:** The discredited practice of judging character from appearance.
