# 03-09 — Explanation, Contestability, and Appeal

**Module:** 3 — Algorithmic Bias in Consequential Decisions
**Estimated time:** 35 minutes
**Content note:** Brief mention of deaths by suicide linked to a government debt program.

**In this lesson:** Even an accurate system will sometimes be wrong about someone. This lesson is about what that person is owed: an explanation they can use, a real person who can change the decision, and a process that does not make them prove their own innocence.

## The Idea: A Right to Challenge

The lessons so far have mostly asked how to make automated decisions fairer and more accurate. This one starts from a different idea. However good a system is, the people it decides about should be able to understand the decision and challenge it. This is **contestability**: the capacity of affected people to challenge a decision effectively and have it changed if it is wrong.

The idea has three parts. People are owed an **explanation** good enough to see whether something has gone wrong. They are owed **review** by someone with the time, information and authority to reach a different answer. And they are owed a **process** for contesting that is accessible and fair, including who has to prove what.

## Why It Persuades

Contestability rests on several arguments that reinforce one another.

It catches errors. People affected by a decision often know things the system does not, such as that a debt was paid or that a record belongs to someone else with the same name. Letting them speak is one of the cheapest ways to find mistakes.

It respects people. Lesson 01-04 argued that people should be treated as agents whose own reasons count. A decision that cannot be questioned treats a person as an input to be processed.

It is fair in itself. Procedural justice, from Lesson 01-06, holds that a fair process matters apart from the outcome, and people accept decisions more readily when they have been heard.

And it improves the system. Every successful challenge is information about where the system fails, if someone collects it.

Contestability also matters most where accuracy arguments are strongest. Lesson 03-08 showed that algorithms can outperform human judgment on average. Contestability is what protects the individual for whom the average was wrong.

## Explanations, and Who Needs Them

Different people need different explanations.

| Who | What they need to know | Example |
|---|---|---|
| The person affected | Why this decision, and what can I do? | "Your application was denied mainly because of X. If Y were different, the outcome might change. You can appeal by Z." |
| The operator | When should I trust or override this? | Confidence levels; known failure cases |
| An auditor or regulator | How does it behave overall, and is it lawful? | Performance by group; documentation |
| The developer | Why did it make this error? | Debugging and attribution tools |
| The public | Should this system exist at all? | Its purpose, governance and impact assessments |

There are also different kinds of explanation. A **global explanation** describes how a model works overall. A **local explanation** describes why it produced one particular output. A **counterfactual explanation** says what would have had to be different for the outcome to change, such as "you would have been approved if your income were $4,000 higher." Counterfactuals are useful because they tell a person what to do without revealing the whole model, though they can suggest changes a person cannot make. A **process explanation** says who designed the system, what data it used, how it was tested and who is responsible. For someone trying to contest a decision, this is often the most useful kind.

The computer scientist Cynthia Rudin has argued that for high-stakes decisions, organizations should not use a "black box" and then try to explain it afterward. They should use an **interpretable model**, one whose logic people can read directly, such as a short scoring rule. For many problems involving tabular data, such as loans or risk scores, interpretable models perform about as well as complex ones.

## Human Review That Works

The most common safeguard offered for automated decisions is a human in the loop: a person who reviews the output before it takes effect. Research suggests it often fails.

Reviewers tend to approve whatever the system recommends, a tendency called automation bias that Module 5 covers. They often have little time; some reviewers handle hundreds of cases an hour. They may see a score but not the reasons behind it. Overriding the system may require a written justification while agreeing with it requires none. And reviewers can add bias of their own by overriding selectively, as the Kentucky judges in Lesson 03-06 did.

The legal scholar Ben Green reviewed 41 policies that required human oversight of government algorithms and found little evidence that people could perform the oversight those policies assigned them. He argued that institutions should first justify why an algorithm is used at all, and then test whether their human oversight actually works.

Meaningful review needs five things: information beyond the score, time to consider it, authority to override without penalty, training on how the system fails, and records of overrides and their outcomes.

## What the Law Requires

As of October 2026, the European Union's GDPR limits decisions based solely on automated processing that have legal or similarly significant effects, and gives people a right to "meaningful information about the logic involved." In 2025 the EU's highest court held, in a case known as *Dun & Bradstreet Austria*, that this means explaining the procedure and principles actually applied, in a way the person can understand. The EU AI Act adds a right to an explanation of individual decisions made with high-risk AI systems. In the United States, rules are specific to sectors, such as the adverse action notices for credit in Lesson 03-05, plus newer state rules on automated decision-making, including California's, finalized in 2025.

## Where It Breaks

Contestability has real limits.

Explanations can mislead. Tools that explain complex models after the fact can give unstable or inaccurate reasons. An explanation that sounds clear but does not reflect how the model works can be worse than none.

Explanation can conflict with other goals. Revealing exactly how a fraud or security system works can teach people how to evade it.

Appeals cost money and time, and those costs can be passed back to the people who use the service.

Contest favors the capable. People with education, time and confidence appeal; others do not. A process that depends on individuals challenging decisions may protect those who need it least. That is why the most useful remedies also fix the underlying system, not just individual cases.

## The Test Case: Robodebt

From 2016 the Australian government ran an automated program to recover welfare overpayments, later known as Robodebt. It took a person's annual income from tax records, spread it evenly across the year in two-week periods, and compared the result with the income they had reported while receiving benefits. Where the averaged figure was higher, it issued a debt notice.

The method was flawed, because many people on benefits had irregular income: work in some weeks and none in others. Averaging made it look as though they had earned money in weeks when they had not. And the program reversed the burden of proof. People had to show the debt was wrong, often years later and without the payslips to do it.

About 470,000 debts were wrongly issued. In 2019 a court case established that the averaging method was unlawful, and in 2020 the government settled a class action for A$1.8 billion, including debts repaid and written off. A Royal Commission reported in 2023 that the scheme was "a crude and cruel mechanism, neither fair nor legal," and documented deaths by suicide linked to the debt notices.

Robodebt was not sophisticated AI. It was a simple rule applied at scale. It failed on every element of contestability: no meaningful explanation, no real human review, and a process that made people prove their own innocence. That is the lesson. The ethical risk lay less in the technology than in using it without the protections that let people challenge it.

## Key Terms

- **Contestability:** The capacity of affected people to challenge a decision effectively and have it changed if it is wrong.
- **Global explanation:** A description of how a model works overall.
- **Local explanation:** A description of why a model produced one particular output.
- **Counterfactual explanation:** A statement of what would have had to be different for the outcome to change.
- **Process explanation:** An account of who designed a system, what data it used, how it was tested and who is responsible.
- **Interpretable model:** A model whose logic people can read and understand directly.
- **Human in the loop:** Human review of automated outputs before they take effect.
- **Burden reversal:** Requiring affected people to disprove an automated conclusion.
