# 02-10 — Privacy-Protective Design and Governance

**Module:** 2 — Surveillance, Biometrics, and Privacy
**Estimated time:** 45 minutes (includes the A2 launch)

**In this lesson:** Much of the privacy harm in this module came from design choices that could have been made differently. This lesson covers the rules that require privacy to be designed in, the technologies that help, what each cannot do, and the assessment that organizations use to check a system before they launch it.

## Two Ways to Build the Same Feature

Two companies, both made up for this example, build the same feature: an AI that summarizes the notifications on your phone.

The first company sends every notification to its servers, keeps them for 18 months, uses them to improve its models, and lets contractors read samples to check quality.

The second company runs a small model on the phone itself. Only when a user asks for a complex summary does it send an encrypted request to a server that keeps nothing. It never uses customers' data for training unless they explicitly agree.

Customers see the same feature. The privacy consequences differ enormously. That is the central point of this lesson: privacy is largely a design choice, made by people who could have chosen otherwise.

## What the Rules Require

Privacy by design is the idea that privacy should be built into a system from the start, not added after something goes wrong. The privacy commissioner Ann Cavoukian set out seven principles for it in 2009, and the most important for practice are these: act before harm occurs, make the most private option the default, build privacy into the design itself, and aim for full functionality with privacy, not a trade between them.

The EU's GDPR turned this into law. Article 25 requires "data protection by design and by default." Organizations must build in protections and must set defaults so that only the data necessary for each purpose is processed.

In practice, privacy by design comes down to a set of questions a team asks at each stage.

| Lever | The question to ask |
|---|---|
| Minimize | Do we need this data at all, and at this level of detail? |
| Localize | Can the processing happen on the device? |
| Limit retention | Can we delete it as soon as it has been used? |
| Separate | Can identities be kept apart from content? |
| Restrict purpose | Is reuse for training or advertising off by default? |
| Control access | Who inside the company can see it, and is that access logged? |
| Inform | Do users understand what is happening at the moment it matters? |
| Empower | Can users see, export, correct and delete their data, including inferences and memory? |

## Technologies That Help

A group of methods called **privacy-enhancing technologies** protect data in specific ways. Each one is useful, and each has limits worth knowing.

**Differential privacy** adds carefully calibrated noise to results so that they reveal very little about any single individual. The U.S. Census Bureau used it for the 2020 census, and Apple and Google use it to collect usage statistics. The cost is accuracy, especially for small groups. The Census Bureau's use was controversial because it made counts for small towns and small populations less precise. How much privacy to buy with how much accuracy is set by a parameter that someone has to choose, which makes it a value judgment.

**Federated learning** trains a model across many devices without gathering the raw data in one place. Each device improves the model locally and sends back only the updates. It keeps raw data on devices, but the updates themselves can leak information, and the finished model can still make inferences about people.

**On-device processing** runs the model locally, as described in Lesson 02-07. It is limited by the power of the device, but models capable of running on phones and laptops are improving quickly.

**Confidential computing** processes data inside hardware that is isolated from the rest of the computer, so that even the company running the server cannot see it. It depends on trusting the hardware, which has had security flaws.

**Synthetic data** is artificial data generated to resemble real data, which can be shared or used for testing in place of the original. It can still reproduce real records if the generator memorized them, and it may not preserve the rare patterns or the fairness properties of the real data.

There is one limit all of these share, and it is the most important point in this section. These technologies protect data in specific ways. None of them makes an inappropriate use appropriate. A model trained with perfect differential privacy that infers people's sexual orientation still raises every problem in Lesson 02-09.

## When the Model Remembers

Large language models raise a special problem. They can memorize parts of their training data and reproduce them word for word. Researchers have extracted verbatim training examples from language models, including names, phone numbers and email addresses. Developers reduce this by removing duplicates and personal information from training data, training with differential privacy, and filtering what the model outputs. None of these methods is complete. Personal information that went into training may come out.

## Checking Before Launch: the DPIA

How does an organization know whether a system is privacy-protective before it launches? The main tool is the **data protection impact assessment**, or DPIA. Under Article 35 of the GDPR, an organization must carry one out before processing that is "likely to result in a high risk" to people, and many AI systems qualify.

A DPIA has four core steps. First, describe the processing: what data, for what purposes, flowing where, and kept for how long. Second, assess whether it is necessary and proportionate: is the data needed, and is there a less invasive way to achieve the purpose? Third, identify the risks to people's rights and freedoms, and how likely and serious each is. Fourth, identify measures to reduce those risks, and state what risk remains afterward.

The EU AI Act adds a related requirement, the **fundamental rights impact assessment**, for certain organizations that deploy high-risk AI systems; Module 8 covers it. Some U.S. state privacy laws, including California's and Colorado's, now require similar risk assessments for high-risk processing.

## Where It Breaks

Privacy by design and impact assessments have real weaknesses.

The company writes its own assessment. A DPIA is prepared by the organization that wants to launch the system, so it can easily become a formality that justifies a decision already made. Its value depends on who reviews it and whether anyone can stop the project.

Privacy-protective design costs something. On-device processing limits what a product can do. Differential privacy reduces accuracy. Deleting data limits future improvements. Some of these costs fall on users, who get a less capable product, and some fall on the public, as with the less precise census counts for small communities.

Defaults are powerful in both directions. Most people never change settings, so the most private default protects nearly everyone. But it also means most people never get a feature that some of them would have wanted.

And the rules can favor large companies. A small firm may struggle to afford the engineering and legal work, while the largest platforms have whole teams devoted to it.

## Key Terms

- **Privacy by design and by default:** Building privacy into systems from the start, with the most private option as the default.
- **Privacy-enhancing technology:** A technical method that protects personal data in a specific way.
- **Differential privacy:** A mathematical method that limits what results reveal about any individual by adding calibrated noise.
- **Federated learning:** Training a model across many devices without collecting the raw data in one place.
- **Memorization:** A model reproducing parts of its training data word for word.
- **Data protection impact assessment (DPIA):** A structured assessment of a high-risk processing activity, its necessity and its risks, carried out before it begins.
- **Fundamental rights impact assessment (FRIA):** An assessment, required by the EU AI Act for some deployers of high-risk systems, of a system's effects on fundamental rights.
