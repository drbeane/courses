# 07-09 — AI for Defenders: Finding and Fixing Vulnerabilities

**Module:** 7 — Open Weights, Agents, and AI Security
**Estimated time:** 35 minutes

**In this lesson:** The ability that let AI models break into Hugging Face can also find and fix flaws before attackers reach them. This lesson looks at the evidence that AI is helping defenders, why it might shift the balance between attack and defense, what it costs the people who maintain software, and who gets access to the best defensive tools.

## A Flaw Found Before Anyone Used It

In late 2024, researchers at Google reported that an AI agent they called Big Sleep had found a previously unknown, exploitable flaw in SQLite, a small database program built into billions of phones, browsers and devices. The flaw was in code that had not yet been included in an official release. The SQLite developers fixed it the same day it was reported, before any user was ever exposed to it.

It was among the first publicly reported cases of an AI system finding a real, previously unknown vulnerability in widely used software. In October 2025 Google DeepMind described a related agent, CodeMender, which proposes and tests fixes; it said that over about six months the agent had submitted 72 security fixes, each reviewed by a person, to open-source projects.

In August 2025, the U.S. Defense Advanced Research Projects Agency, DARPA, held the final round of its AI Cyber Challenge, a two-year competition for systems that automatically find and fix vulnerabilities in open-source software. The finalist systems found most of the flaws the organizers had planted in test software and patched many of them. They also discovered 18 real, previously unknown flaws, though they patched only some of those. The winning team, led by Georgia Tech, took a $4 million prize, and DARPA funded the finalists to prepare their systems for real-world use.

## Why This Matters

Most of the world's critical software rests on open-source components maintained by small teams, sometimes by one or two volunteers. These components run hospitals, power grids, banks and phones. A single flaw in a widely used library can expose millions of systems, and attackers need only one flaw to get in, while defenders must find and fix all of them. This imbalance, sometimes called the **defender's dilemma**, has long favored attackers.

AI could shift that balance. If automated tools can examine code faster and more thoroughly than people, defenders can find flaws at a scale that matches the scale of the software they protect. Tools that also propose fixes could help the many under-resourced projects that cannot afford security staff. And because the most important software is open source, defensive tools applied to it protect everyone who depends on it, including people who will never know they were protected.

This is the "system that helps" for this module, and it is the direct counterpart of the dual-use problem in Lesson 07-08.

## The Evidence So Far

The evidence is promising but still early. It is measured, in the sense of Module 5, that AI systems have found real, previously unknown flaws in widely used software, and that some have produced working fixes. It is less clear how much faster or more thoroughly AI finds flaws than experienced human researchers working with conventional automated tools such as **fuzzing**, which feeds programs huge numbers of random inputs to see what breaks. And it is not yet clear whether defenders or attackers will gain more, which is the question that matters most.

## Who Gains First?

The same capability is available to both sides, so the question is timing and access.

Defenders may gain more if they adopt the tools first and apply them systematically to the most important software, if the tools are better at fixing than at exploiting, and if trusted-access programs, described in Lesson 07-08, give defenders better models than attackers can get.

Attackers may gain more if they adopt the tools faster, as they often adopt new techniques; if finding a flaw is easier than fixing it across thousands of affected systems, which can take months or years; and if open models without safeguards give attackers the same capability with none of the restrictions.

The honest answer, as of 2026, is that nobody knows. Robust decision-making, from Lesson 07-08, suggests investing in defensive uses in any case, since they help whichever way it goes.

## What It Costs Maintainers

The benefits have a cost that falls on the people who maintain software.

Many open-source projects have been flooded with bug reports generated by AI. Some are real and valuable. Many are wrong, describing flaws that do not exist, but are written convincingly enough that a maintainer must spend time checking each one. The maintainer of curl, one of the most widely used pieces of internet software, has complained publicly about the burden of AI-generated reports that turn out to be false. For volunteers maintaining software in their spare time, a flood of plausible but false reports can be exhausting and can bury the real ones.

This is the hallucination problem from Lesson 04-01 in a new setting, and the verification duty from Lesson 04-02 applies: someone who submits an AI-found bug should check it first.

There is also a question of fairness. If AI companies and large security firms profit from finding flaws in open-source software, while volunteers do the unpaid work of fixing them, the gains and the burdens are unevenly shared, a question of justice from Lesson 01-06.

## Who Gets the Best Tools?

The most capable defensive tools are often restricted. Labs offer their strongest models to trusted security organizations while giving the public versions with more safeguards. That limits misuse. It also means that large companies with security teams get better protection than small organizations, independent researchers and volunteer maintainers. Hugging Face, in Lesson 07-06, was refused by a hosted model when it most needed help, and was added to a trusted-access program only after it had been breached.

## Where People Disagree

Should the best AI vulnerability-finding tools be made freely available to all open-source maintainers? It would protect the software everyone relies on. It would also make those tools available to attackers.

Who should pay for fixing the flaws AI finds: the companies whose tools find them, the companies whose products depend on the software, governments, or the volunteers who maintain it?

And should people who submit AI-generated bug reports be required to disclose it and to verify the report first? Some projects now require exactly that.

## Key Terms

- **Vulnerability:** A flaw in software that an attacker could use.
- **Defender's dilemma:** The imbalance in which attackers need one flaw but defenders must fix all of them.
- **Fuzzing:** Testing software by feeding it huge numbers of random or malformed inputs to see what breaks.
- **Trusted access:** Giving vetted organizations access to more capable models or tools than the public receives.
