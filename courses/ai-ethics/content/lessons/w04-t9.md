# 04-09 — Creative Rights, Copyright, and Training Data

**Module:** 4 — Synthetic Media, Information Integrity, and Human Agency
**Estimated time:** 40 minutes

**In this lesson:** Generative AI is trained on enormous amounts of writing, art and music made by people who were never asked. This lesson sets out what copyright law says so far about training, outputs and authorship, the cases that have shaped it, and the ethical questions about consent, payment and credit that the law leaves open.

## $1.5 Billion for Pirated Books

In 2024 the authors Andrea Bartz, Charles Graeber and Kirk Wallace Johnson sued Anthropic, alleging that it had trained its Claude models on their books without permission.

In June 2025, Judge William Alsup issued a split ruling. Training an AI model on books that had been lawfully acquired was **fair use**, he held. He called it "quintessentially transformative," comparing it to a reader learning to write by reading. But Anthropic had also downloaded millions of pirated books from online "shadow libraries" to build a permanent central library, and that, he held, was not fair use, and could go to trial.

Facing potentially enormous damages, Anthropic agreed in September 2025 to a $1.5 billion class settlement, about $3,000 for each of roughly 500,000 books, and to destroy the pirated copies. It was reported to be the largest copyright recovery in U.S. history, and the court approved it in July 2026.

Two days after Alsup's ruling, Judge Vince Chhabria decided a similar case, *Kadrey v. Meta*, also in Meta's favor. But his reasoning pointed in a different direction. Meta won, he said, because the authors had failed to show that the training harmed the market for their books. He suggested that in many cases, training on copyrighted works without permission could well be unlawful if that harm, including "market dilution" from a flood of AI-generated substitutes, were proven.

## What the Law Has to Decide

Generative AI raises three distinct legal questions.

The first is about **training**. Is copying works in order to train a model an infringement of copyright? In the United States the question is whether it is fair use. In the European Union and the UK, it is whether it falls within exceptions for **text and data mining**, the computational analysis of large collections of material.

The second is about **outputs**. When a model produces something very like a work it was trained on, or reproduces it word for word, is that infringement, and is the user or the developer responsible?

The third is about **authorship**. Can something generated by AI be copyrighted? The U.S. Copyright Office and the federal appeals court in Washington, D.C., in *Thaler v. Perlmutter* in 2025, have said that copyright requires a human author. Work made with AI assistance can be protected to the extent of the human's creative contribution.

## How Fair Use Works

U.S. courts decide fair use by weighing four factors: the purpose and character of the use, including whether it is **transformative**, meaning it adds a new purpose or character, and whether it is commercial; the nature of the original work; how much of it was used; and the effect on the market for the original.

So far, courts have been receptive to the argument that training is transformative. They have paid close attention to two other things: how the data was acquired, as the piracy finding in *Bartz* shows, and harm to the market, including the possibility that AI output floods the market with substitutes. A 2025 decision in *Thomson Reuters v. Ross* found that copying legal summaries to build a competing AI search tool was not fair use, because the product competed directly with the original.

## Other Cases

As of October 2026, several major cases were still developing. *The New York Times* was suing Microsoft and OpenAI over the use of its articles, including instances where models reproduced them nearly word for word. In the UK, Getty Images largely lost its case against Stability AI in November 2025, which had argued that an image model trained on Getty's photos was itself an infringing copy. Disney and Universal sued the image generator Midjourney in 2025 over images of their characters. And music publishers and record companies sued AI companies over lyrics and music, with some of those disputes ending in settlements and licensing deals in 2025.

## What the Law Leaves Open

Even where training turns out to be legal, many creators argue that it is wrong, and their arguments are serious.

They never consented to having their work used to build systems that compete with them. The value created from their work flows to AI companies, and they receive nothing. Prompts asking for work "in the style of" a named living artist trade on that artist's reputation without credit. Illustrators, voice actors, translators and stock photographers report losing work to AI. And some communities object to their cultural works, including Indigenous art and stories, being used without consent.

The arguments on the other side are also serious. People learn from others' work all the time without paying, and style itself has never been protected by copyright. Requiring a license for every piece of training data would favor the largest companies, which can afford licenses, and block researchers and smaller developers who cannot. Broad access to knowledge has great social value. And AI tools also help many creators work faster and do things they could not do before.

## What Is Emerging

Several responses are taking shape alongside the lawsuits. News organizations, image libraries, publishers and platforms have signed licensing deals with AI companies. The EU AI Act requires providers of general-purpose AI models to respect creators' choice to opt out of text and data mining and to publish summaries of their training data. Researchers at the University of Chicago released Glaze and Nightshade, tools that let artists alter their images in ways that disrupt AI models' attempts to imitate their style. Unions have negotiated protections in contracts for writers and performers. And some laws protect a person's voice and likeness specifically: Tennessee's ELVIS Act of 2024 protects musicians' voices against unauthorized AI imitation, and a federal bill, the NO FAKES Act, has been proposed.

## Where People Disagree

Judge Alsup compared AI training to a person reading books and learning to write. Is that a fair comparison? A person reads slowly, remembers imperfectly and produces a limited amount of work. A model reads everything at once and can produce competing work without limit.

Should there be a legal right to opt out of AI training? A requirement to opt in? Or a system of compulsory licenses, in which anyone may train on any work but must pay royalties through a collective, as radio stations do for music?

And even where it is legal, is it right to prompt for work "in the style of" a living artist? Should generators block it?

## Key Terms

- **Fair use:** A U.S. doctrine that permits some uses of copyrighted works without permission.
- **Transformative use:** A use that adds a new purpose or character to the original work.
- **Text and data mining exception:** EU and UK exceptions to copyright for computational analysis of large collections of material.
- **Market dilution:** Harm to creators from AI-generated substitutes flooding the market for their work.
- **Right of publicity:** The right to control commercial use of one's identity, including name, likeness and voice.
