# 01-09 — Law, Policy, Standards, and Ethics: Building a Defensible Argument

**Module:** 1 — Ethical Foundations and AI in Society
**Estimated time:** 40 minutes (includes the A1 launch)

**In this lesson:** "Is it legal?" and "is it right?" are different questions with different answers. This lesson separates the kinds of rule that apply to AI, sets the standard this course holds you to when you cite law, and shows how to build an ethical argument that can survive a challenge.

## The Law Moved Twice in One Year

Suppose you ran a company in Colorado in 2026 and used an AI tool to screen job applicants. What did the law require of you?

In May 2024, Colorado had passed the first broad state law in the United States on "high-risk" AI. It required organizations to run risk-management programs and to assess the impact of their systems, and it was due to take effect in February 2026. In August 2025 the legislature pushed the date back to June 30, 2026. In December 2025 a presidential executive order directed the Justice Department to challenge state AI laws it considered burdensome. In April 2026 a federal court put enforcement on hold, in a lawsuit that the Justice Department and the company xAI had joined. Then, on May 14, 2026, Colorado repealed the law and replaced it with a narrower one, taking effect on January 1, 2027. The new law drops the impact assessments and risk programs. It requires organizations that use automated decision-making to give notice, to explain a decision that goes against someone within 30 days, to let people correct their data, and to offer human review where that is commercially reasonable.

Through all of this, federal law against discrimination in employment applied without interruption, and a major lawsuit over an AI hiring tool, *Mobley v. Workday*, continued in federal court.

A company that waited to be told what was legal would have spent the year confused. A company that had asked what was right, and what it could defend in public, would have had the same answer in December as in January.

*These dates are as of October 2026. The law in this area changes quickly, so check the current status before relying on any of them.*

## Five Kinds of "Ought"

When someone says an organization "should" do something about AI, the word can rest on five different foundations. Keeping them apart is one of the most useful habits this course teaches.

| Source | Is it binding? | Who enforces it | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Law: statutes, regulations, court decisions | Yes | Courts and agencies | The EU AI Act; Illinois's biometric privacy law |
| Regulatory guidance | No, but it signals how an agency will enforce | Agencies | Business guidance from the U.S. Federal Trade Commission |
| Technical standards | Voluntary, unless a law or contract requires them | Certifiers and customers | ISO/IEC 42001; the NIST AI Risk Management Framework |
| Organizational policy | Binding on employees | Management | A police department's facial recognition policy |
| Ethics | Morally binding | Conscience, peers and the public | "Don't arrest on a match alone" |

The **law** is the only one that a court will enforce against everyone it covers. **Regulatory guidance** is an agency's statement of how it reads the law; it is not law, but ignoring it is risky, and it can be withdrawn, as some U.S. guidance on AI in hiring was in 2025. A **technical standard** is a published specification that organizations may choose to follow and be certified against. **Organizational policy** is what an employer requires of its own people. West Fargo's rule against arresting on a facial recognition match was policy: no law demanded it. And ethics is what remains true whether or not any of the other four says so.

The table also shows how good practice spreads. A rule often begins as someone's ethical conviction, becomes one organization's policy, is written into a standard, and ends up in law.

## Legal and Right Are Different Questions

The law and ethics come apart in both directions, and the examples are not hard to find.

Some things are legal and wrong. When Clearview AI collected billions of photos from the web to build a face-search database, doing so was lawful in most U.S. states. Only a few, including Illinois, Texas and Washington, had laws on biometric data.

Some things that were wrong later became illegal. Sexual deepfakes of real people were largely lawful in the United States until states began passing laws and Congress passed the TAKE IT DOWN Act in 2025. The wrong did not begin in 2025.

Some things are illegal and arguably right. A security researcher who probes an AI system without permission may break its terms of service, or a computer misuse law, in order to reveal a real danger. In 2022 the U.S. Justice Department announced that it would not prosecute good-faith security research under the main federal computer crime law.

And the law varies and changes. The same practice can be lawful in Texas, restricted in Illinois and prohibited in the European Union, and as Colorado shows, the answer in one place can change twice in a year.

None of this makes the law unimportant. Law is how a society enforces its minimum standards, and a professional who ignores it harms real people and their own organization. The point is that legality settles less than people assume. "It's legal" does not end an ethical argument, and "it's unethical" does not show that anything unlawful occurred.

## How to State the Law in This Course

You are not expected to give legal advice. You are expected not to misstate the law. Every legal claim you make in this course should do five things.

1. **Date it.** Write "as of October 2026" or whatever date applies.
2. **Locate it.** Name the jurisdiction: federal, a particular state, the EU, the UK.
3. **Source it.** Cite the statute, regulation, case or official guidance. A blog post about the law is not the law.
4. **Scope it.** Say whom the law applies to, such as developers, deployers or platforms, and above what threshold.
5. **Separate it.** Do not present a voluntary standard as a legal requirement, or a proposal as an enacted law.

Part of your grade in the debates and written assessments depends on this.

## Building an Argument That Holds

The frameworks from this week give you things to say about a case. An argument organizes them so that a skeptical reader can follow your reasoning and test it. A strong ethical argument has six parts.

1. **Claim.** State what you think should be done, in one sentence.
2. **Premises.** Give your reasons. Some will be factual and some will be about values.
3. **Evidence.** Support each factual premise, and say how good the evidence is.
4. **Framework.** Say which ethical approach supports each value premise.
5. **Strongest counterargument.** State the best objection as its own supporters would put it. This is called a **steelman**, the opposite of a straw man.
6. **Response.** Explain why your claim survives the objection, or how you would revise it.

The fifth step is the one people skip, and it is the one that makes an argument defensible. If you can only answer a weak version of the other side, you have not shown that you are right. You will practice this directly: in the debates for A1 you are assigned a side, whether or not you agree with it.

Six weaknesses turn up again and again in arguments about AI. One is confusing "can" with "should." Another is leaning on a single framework, usually a tally of benefits. A third is attacking a weak version of the opposing view. A fourth is treating allegations as established facts. A fifth is the appeal to inevitability: "it's going to happen anyway." The sixth comes in two forms. One says AI is so new that old ethics does not apply. The other says it is just like something familiar, so nothing new needs to be asked.

## A Worked Example

Here is the skeleton of an argument on the Colorado case.

The claim: an employer that screens résumés with AI should carry out an analysis of whether the tool disadvantages protected groups, and should publish a summary, whether or not the law currently requires it.

The premises, with their support. First, screening tools can reproduce historical bias; the evidence includes Amazon's abandoned recruiting tool, reported in 2018, and the allegations in *Mobley*, which remain allegations. Second, applicants cannot detect this bias for themselves, since they see only their own result. Third, employers have a duty not to discriminate, which is both a legal duty under federal employment law and an ethical one grounded in justice and decency. Fourth, publishing the result creates accountability, because outsiders can check it.

The strongest counterargument: publishing invites lawsuits and exposes proprietary methods, and small employers cannot afford audits. On this view, a requirement like this would push small firms back to slower human screening that may be no fairer. The tool also has real benefits that the claim ignores: it lets an employer consider every application, which a human screener with limited time may not manage.

The response: summary statistics reveal little about how a tool works. An audit costs less than a lawsuit. A requirement scaled to the size of the employer is workable; New York City already requires published summaries of bias audits for automated hiring tools. And the benefit of considering every applicant is a reason to check that the tool treats them fairly. It does not justify leaving the tool unchecked.

Notice that the response concedes what is true in the objection and narrows the claim to fit it. Conceding a fair point makes the argument stronger.

## Check Your Understanding

**1. What are the five sources of "ought," and which is binding on everyone it covers?**

:::answer Answer
Law, regulatory guidance, technical standards, organizational policy and ethics. Only law is enforced by courts against everyone it covers. Policy binds an organization's own employees.
:::

**2. Give one example of something that was legal but arguably wrong.**

:::answer Answer
Clearview AI's collection of photos from the web to build a face database, which was lawful in most U.S. states. Sexual deepfakes of real people before state laws and the 2025 TAKE IT DOWN Act are another.
:::

**3. What five things should every legal claim in this course do?**

:::answer Answer
Date it, locate it (the jurisdiction), source it, scope it (whom it applies to) and separate it (do not present a standard or a proposal as binding law).
:::

**4. What is a steelman, and why include one?**

:::answer Answer
The strongest version of the opposing argument, stated as its supporters would state it. Answering it shows that your position holds up against the best objection, not just a weak one.
:::

## Key Terms

- **Law:** Statutes, regulations and court decisions, enforced by courts and agencies.
- **Regulatory guidance:** An agency's non-binding statement of how it interprets and will enforce the law.
- **Technical standard:** A published specification that is voluntary unless a law or contract requires it.
- **Organizational policy:** Rules an organization sets for its own employees.
- **Steelman:** The strongest version of an opposing argument.
- **Preemption:** Federal law displacing state law.
