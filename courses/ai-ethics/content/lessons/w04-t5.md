# 04-05 — Elections, Fraud, and the Liar's Dividend

**Module:** 4 — Synthetic Media, Information Integrity, and Human Agency
**Estimated time:** 35 minutes

**In this lesson:** Generative AI was expected to flood elections with convincing fakes. This lesson looks at what actually happened, at a quieter danger that may matter more, the ability to dismiss real evidence as fake, and at what the law can and cannot do about political deception.

## "Save Your Vote for November"

Two days before New Hampshire's presidential primary in January 2024, thousands of voters received a phone call in what sounded like President Biden's voice. It told them not to vote in the primary: "Your vote makes a difference in November, not this Tuesday."

The voice was generated by AI. A political consultant, Steve Kramer, said he had commissioned the call to demonstrate how dangerous AI could be. The consequences came from several directions. In February 2024 the Federal Communications Commission ruled that AI-generated voices count as "artificial" under the Telephone Consumer Protection Act, which makes such robocalls illegal without the recipient's consent. It later fined Kramer $6 million. The telecom company that carried the calls agreed to a $1 million settlement. But in June 2025, a New Hampshire jury acquitted Kramer of state charges of voter suppression and impersonating a candidate.

## What Actually Happened in 2024

More than 60 countries held national elections in 2024. Many commentators predicted that deepfakes would overwhelm them. The record was more mixed.

There were real incidents. In Slovakia's 2023 election, a fake audio recording of a candidate apparently discussing how to rig the vote was released during the period just before voting when media are barred from election coverage. In India, parties used cloned voices and avatars, including "resurrected" deceased politicians, with their families' or parties' consent. Indonesia saw deepfake videos. And AI companies' own threat reports described foreign influence operations using AI-generated content.

But several careful reviews after the elections, including one by the Alan Turing Institute in the UK, found little evidence that deepfakes changed any outcome. AI was used mostly to make cheap content, memes and translations, not decisive fakes.

That finding needs a caution. Not finding evidence of an effect is not the same as finding no effect. Influence on how people vote is very hard to measure, and the technology improved sharply between 2024 and 2026.

## What AI Does for Democratic Politics

Generative AI also has uses in elections that most people would welcome, and they are easy to overlook.

It lowers the cost of reaching voters in their own languages. In India, with its many languages, parties used AI to translate candidates' speeches so that voters could hear them in their own language. A small campaign with no budget for translators can now do what only large campaigns could do before.

It can make information more accessible: summarizing a ballot measure in plain language, or answering questions about a candidate's record.

And it can correct false beliefs. In a 2024 study in *Science*, Thomas Costello, Gordon Pennycook and David Rand had people who believed a conspiracy theory discuss it with an AI that presented evidence. Belief dropped by about 20 percent on average, and the effect lasted at least two months. Persuasive AI is not inherently harmful. What matters is whether it is truthful, transparent and used with consent.

## Persuasion at Scale

The same capacity to persuade raises concerns beyond deepfakes. AI can write personalized political messages for millions of people at little cost, and studies in 2024 and 2025 found that AI-written arguments can shift people's views on policy questions by measurable amounts, sometimes as much as arguments written by people. It can create the appearance of grassroots support through many fake accounts posting plausible content, a practice called **astroturfing**. And chatbots can simply get election facts wrong: studies in 2024 found popular chatbots giving incorrect information about how, when and where to vote.

## The Liar's Dividend

The legal scholars Bobby Chesney and Danielle Citron identified a danger that may be larger than any single fake. They called it the **liar's dividend**. As people learn that convincing fakes exist, real evidence becomes easy to deny. A politician caught on a genuine recording can simply say, "That's a deepfake."

This has already happened. Public figures in several countries have dismissed authentic recordings as AI fabrications. In court, defendants have argued that video evidence against them might be fake, and courts are working out new standards for authenticating evidence.

The liar's dividend may be the larger long-term harm because it does not depend on anyone being fooled by a particular fake. It erodes the shared factual basis that democratic accountability needs. If any recording can be dismissed, recordings stop holding anyone to account.

## What the Law Can Do

As of October 2026, responses fall into several groups. Many U.S. states require disclaimers on political ads that use AI, and Google and Meta require advertisers to disclose AI-altered political ads. Several states prohibit materially deceptive deepfakes of candidates within a set period before an election. The FCC's 2024 ruling covers robocalls. In the European Union, guidelines under the Digital Services Act address election integrity, and the AI Act requires deepfakes to be disclosed. Technology companies also signed a voluntary accord in Munich in 2024 to combat deceptive AI in elections, though later reviews found little follow-through.

In the United States, the First Amendment sets hard limits. Political speech receives the strongest protection. The Supreme Court held in 2012, in *United States v. Alvarez*, that false statements are not automatically unprotected. Satire is protected. Some state laws have already failed: a federal court blocked one California law on election deepfakes in 2024 and struck it down later, and struck down a second in 2025. Rules aimed narrowly at deception about the mechanics of voting, such as how, when and where to vote, or rules that require disclosure, are much more likely to survive than bans on content.

*Legal status is as of October 2026 and should be checked before teaching.*

## Where People Disagree

Does intent matter? Kramer said he made the robocall to warn people about AI. If the warning was real, is the act less wrong? Many people think the harm to voters who stayed home is the same either way.

Should campaigns be allowed to use AI versions of their own candidates, for example to speak to voters in languages the candidate does not speak? It extends access. It also blurs what a candidate actually said.

Is the liar's dividend a reason to restrict deepfake technology, or a reason to invest in ways of proving what is real, such as the provenance tools in Lesson 04-06, and in teaching people to verify?

And if AI conversations can reduce belief in conspiracy theories, should governments use them? The answer depends on who decides what counts as a conspiracy theory, which is exactly the kind of power democracies are wary of giving the state.

## Check Your Understanding

**1. What did the FCC decide about AI-generated voices in 2024?**

:::answer Answer
That they count as "artificial" voices under the Telephone Consumer Protection Act, so robocalls using them are illegal without the recipient's consent.
:::

**2. What did reviews of the 2024 elections find about deepfakes, and what caution applies?**

:::answer Answer
Little evidence that deepfakes changed outcomes; AI was mostly used for cheap content and translation. But the absence of measured impact is not proof of no impact, and the technology has improved since.
:::

**3. What is the liar's dividend?**

:::answer Answer
The ability to dismiss real evidence as fake because people know convincing fakes exist.
:::

**4. Why are U.S. laws targeting deception about voting procedures more likely to survive than bans on deepfakes?**

:::answer Answer
Political speech has strong First Amendment protection, false statements are not automatically unprotected and satire is protected. Narrow rules about how, when and where to vote, or disclosure requirements, burden speech less.
:::

## Key Terms

- **Liar's dividend:** The ability to dismiss real evidence as fake because fakes exist.
- **Astroturfing:** Simulating grassroots support, for example with many fake accounts.
- **Materially deceptive media:** A term in some state laws for synthetic media likely to mislead voters about a candidate.
