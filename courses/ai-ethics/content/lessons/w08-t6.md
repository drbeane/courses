# 08-06 — Documentation: Model Cards, Datasheets, and Impact Assessments

**Module:** 8 — Governance, Accountability, and Responsible Deployment
**Estimated time:** 40 minutes

**In this lesson:** Documentation is how an organization tells others, and itself, what a system is for, how well it works, for whom it fails, and what could go wrong. This lesson describes the main kinds of AI documentation, what good versions contain, the ways documentation fails, and how it becomes part of your A5 dossier.

## The System Card That Came Late

When AI labs release major models, they increasingly publish a **system card**: a document describing the model's capabilities, the results of its safety tests, including tests for dangerous capabilities (Lesson 07-08), its known limitations and the safeguards around it.

In 2025, several labs were criticized for releasing models before publishing their system cards, for publishing thin ones, or for leaving out details of their tests. Google, for example, released its Gemini 2.5 Pro model weeks before its model card, and the card, when it came, was criticized as sparse. California's SB 53, effective January 2026, now requires large developers of the most advanced models to publish safety frameworks and transparency reports when they deploy.

Documentation is only useful if it is timely, complete and honest.

## Kinds of Documentation

| Document | Where it came from | What it records | Main audience |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Datasheet for a dataset** | Timnit Gebru and colleagues, 2018 | Why a dataset was created, what is in it, how it was collected and processed, and how it should and should not be used | People using the data; auditors |
| **Model card** | Margaret Mitchell and colleagues, 2019 | A model's details, intended uses, how it performs, broken down by group, and its limitations | Developers, deployers and auditors |
| System card | AI labs, from 2023 | How a whole system behaves, including safeguards and safety test results | The public, regulators and researchers |
| Algorithmic impact assessment | Canada, 2019; researchers, 2018 | The context, the people affected, the risks, the safeguards and who was consulted | Decision-makers; the public |
| Data protection impact assessment | The EU's GDPR | Privacy risks (Lesson 02-10) | Privacy officers; regulators |
| Fundamental rights impact assessment | The EU AI Act | Risks to fundamental rights from deploying high-risk AI | Deployers; regulators |
| Transparency reports | Platforms; California's SB 53 | Aggregate data on incidents and enforcement | The public |

## What a Good Model Card Contains

Mitchell and colleagues proposed that a model card cover nine things: who made the model, when, which version and under what license; its intended uses and users, and uses it is *not* suited for, called **out-of-scope uses**; the groups, instruments and settings that might affect its performance; the measures of performance used and why; the data it was evaluated on; as much as possible about its training data; results broken down by group and by combinations of groups, called **disaggregated evaluation**; ethical considerations; and caveats and recommendations.

The disaggregated results are the heart of it. An average accuracy hides who the system fails. Breaking results down by group is how the problems in Modules 2 and 3 come to light.

## Impact Assessments

The EU AI Act requires certain deployers of high-risk systems, including public bodies, private organizations providing public services, and companies using AI for credit scoring or life and health insurance pricing, to carry out a **fundamental rights impact assessment** before first use. It covers the processes in which the system will be used, how long and how often, who will be affected, the specific risks to them, the human oversight in place, and what will happen if the risks materialize, including how people can complain. The results go to the national regulator. Under the 2026 amendments, these duties follow the delayed high-risk timeline.

Canada offers a practical public model. Its federal directive on automated decision-making requires government departments to complete an online questionnaire that scores a system's impact on a scale from I to IV. The higher the level, the more is required: peer review, human involvement, explanation and notice. Completed assessments are published.

## Why Documentation Persuades

Documentation does more than inform others. Writing it forces a team to state its assumptions and confront its system's limits, which is often where problems are first noticed. It makes explicability, from Lesson 01-07, concrete. It creates a record that auditors, regulators and courts can examine. And honest documentation is a form of the institutional candor that Lesson 08-05 asked for.

## Where It Breaks

Documentation fails in familiar ways. It becomes box-checking, with templates filled in with boilerplate. It discloses selectively, reporting favorable averages instead of results by group. It goes stale, not updated after the model changes. It is unreadable, too technical for the people affected or too vague for auditors. It omits warnings about foreseeable misuse. It arrives too late, after deployment, as in the opening case. And it changes nothing, because nobody reads it before decisions are made.

There is also a tension. Because documentation can be used in audits and lawsuits, it gives organizations reasons both to be diligent and to be vague.

## Where People Disagree

Should model cards and system cards be legally required for every commercially deployed model? Which sections are essential?

How much detail about tests of dangerous capabilities should be public? Detail helps researchers and regulators. It might also show bad actors what to try.

Should impact assessments be published, as Canada does, or kept confidential? And who should write them: the deployer, an independent party, or the communities affected?

## Check Your Understanding

**1. What is the difference between a datasheet and a model card?**

:::answer Answer
A datasheet documents a dataset: why it was made, what it contains, how it was collected and how it should be used. A model card documents a model: its intended uses, performance broken down by group, and limitations.
:::

**2. Why are disaggregated results central to a model card?**

:::answer Answer
An average hides who the system fails. Results broken down by group, and combinations of groups, reveal unequal performance.
:::

**3. What does the EU's fundamental rights impact assessment cover?**

:::answer Answer
How and how often the system will be used, who will be affected, the specific risks to them, human oversight, and what happens if risks materialize, including complaint mechanisms.
:::

**4. Name three ways documentation fails.**

:::answer Answer
Any three of these: box-checking; selective disclosure; going stale; being unreadable; omitting foreseeable misuse; arriving too late; changing no decisions.
:::

## Your Turn

For your A5 system, draft two short documents. First, a one-page model or system card summary covering intended use, out-of-scope uses, key measures broken down by group, and known limitations. Second, an impact assessment summary covering the groups affected, the three biggest risks and how each is addressed, oversight, and how people can complain or get redress. Mark every place where you lack information, and say how you would get it. Honest treatment of missing information is part of the A5 rubric.

## Key Terms

- **Datasheet for a dataset:** Documentation of a dataset's motivation, contents, collection and intended uses.
- **Model card:** Documentation of a model's intended uses, performance by group and limitations.
- **System card:** Documentation of a whole AI system's behavior, safeguards and safety test results.
- **Disaggregated evaluation:** Performance results broken down by group.
- **Out-of-scope use:** A use the developer warns the system is not suited for.
- **Fundamental rights impact assessment:** An assessment, required by the EU AI Act for some deployers, of a system's effects on fundamental rights.
