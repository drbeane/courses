# 01-08 — Stakeholders and Sociotechnical Systems

**Module:** 1 — Ethical Foundations and AI in Society
**Estimated time:** 40 minutes

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

1. Explain why AI outcomes are produced by sociotechnical systems, not by models alone.
2. Build a stakeholder map that distinguishes interest, impact and power.
3. Identify "invisible" stakeholders who are often left out of AI analyses.

## Opening case: what failed in Fargo?

Who is responsible for Angela Lipps's wrongful arrest (as alleged)?

- **The facial recognition vendor** produced a candidate match.
- **A West Fargo analyst and a state intelligence center** ran the search and passed along the lead. West Fargo's own policy forbids arrests based solely on a match.
- **The source image** reportedly came from a fake ID, not surveillance footage.
- **The Fargo detective** allegedly ignored contrary evidence.
- **The Fargo Police Department** had no policy, training or supervision for facial recognition at the time.
- **Prosecutors and a judge** approved charges and an extradition.
- **The jail** allegedly withheld medication.
- **The city** had adopted the tool without governance.

The "AI failure" is really a chain of human, institutional and technical decisions. Analysts call this a **sociotechnical system**.

## Core ideas

### Sociotechnical systems

A sociotechnical system includes:

- **technology:** model, data, hardware, interface;
- **people:** developers, operators, decision-makers, affected individuals;
- **organizations:** incentives, policies, training, culture;
- **rules:** laws, contracts, standards, norms; and
- **environment:** physical setting, infrastructure, other systems.

Selbst et al. (2019) identified five "traps" that arise when engineers abstract the social context away:

1. **Framing trap:** Modeling only the algorithm, not the system it sits in.
2. **Portability trap:** Assuming a solution built for one context works in another.
3. **Formalism trap:** Assuming a mathematical definition, such as of fairness, captures a social concept.
4. **Ripple effect trap:** Ignoring how the technology changes behavior and institutions.
5. **Solutionism trap:** Assuming technology is the answer, when sometimes the best option is not to build it.

### Stakeholders

A **stakeholder** is anyone who affects or is affected by a system. Types:

| Type | Examples |
|---|---|
| **Direct users** | Police analyst; HR recruiter; chatbot user |
| **Decision subjects** | Suspect; applicant; borrower |
| **Indirect affected parties** | Bystanders on camera; family of an arrested person; people depicted in deepfakes |
| **Builders** | Model developers; data labelers; vendor |
| **Deployers and owners** | City government; employer; bank |
| **Overseers** | Regulators; courts; auditors; journalists |
| **Society and the future** | Public trust; the information ecosystem; future generations |
| **Non-humans** | Ecosystems affected by data centers (06-07) |

### Mapping interest, impact and power

A useful stakeholder map plots each group by:

- **Impact:** how much the system affects them,
- **Power:** how much they can influence the system (design, purchase, refusal, appeal, litigation), and
- **Voice:** whether they were consulted.

The ethically critical quadrant is **high impact, low power**: applicants who can't see why they were rejected, residents near a data center, people depicted in non-consensual images, workers under algorithmic management.

### Invisible stakeholders

Commonly overlooked:

- **Data subjects** whose information trained the model.
- **Data workers** who labeled and moderated training data (05-07).
- **People excluded** from the system entirely (non-users, people without digital access).
- **Bystanders** recorded by sensors (02-08).
- **Future users** affected by decisions locked in now.
- **Downstream deployers** of open-weight models who inherit risks (07-02).

### Participatory approaches

Meaningful participation goes beyond a comment period:

- involvement early, before the decision to build or buy,
- power to change or stop the project,
- compensation for affected people's time and expertise, and
- feedback on how input was used.

Arnstein's "ladder of citizen participation" (1969) distinguishes manipulation and tokenism from genuine partnership and control. It is still a useful test for "community engagement" claims.

## Applying the frameworks

Use the justice dimensions (01-06) on the Fargo stakeholder map:

- **Procedural justice:** Did the public have any say in adopting facial recognition? (Many U.S. cities have no public process.)
- **Corrective justice:** Who among these stakeholders owes Lipps a remedy?
- **Decency:** Which stakeholders failed to give "reasonable weight" to her interests?

## Discussion questions

1. In the Fargo chain, which single link, if changed, would most likely have prevented the harm? Is that the link most responsible?
2. Which of Selbst's five traps is most common in your own technical training?
3. Who are the invisible stakeholders of a large language model chatbot?
4. When is it acceptable to deploy a system over the objections of a high-impact, low-power group?

## Key terms

- **Sociotechnical system:** Technology plus people, organizations, rules and environment.
- **Decision subject:** The person about whom a system makes or informs a decision.
- **Solutionism:** The belief that technology is the answer to social problems.
- **Ladder of participation:** Arnstein's scale from manipulation to citizen control.
