# 06-06 — Compute Concentration and Global Divides

**Module:** 6 — Environmental Costs and Global Access
**Estimated time:** 35 minutes

**In this lesson:** The ability to build and run advanced AI is concentrated in a few countries and companies, and AI works best in a handful of languages. This lesson asks whether that concentration is unjust, what it costs the rest of the world, and what countries are doing to avoid being left behind or left dependent.

## Who Has the Chips?

By 2025 a small number of countries and companies controlled most of the world's capacity for AI computing. The United States and China hosted the large majority of data-center capacity able to run advanced AI. Since 2022 the United States had restricted exports of advanced AI chips to China. In January 2025 the U.S. government issued a rule that would have sorted nearly every country into tiers with different levels of access to American chips; it was withdrawn in May 2025 and replaced with deals negotiated country by country, including large agreements with the United Arab Emirates and Saudi Arabia.

A 2024 study from the Oxford Internet Institute found that only about 30 countries host public cloud regions offering the graphics processors that AI depends on. Much of Africa, Latin America and South Asia has little or none.

Language shows the same pattern. The world has roughly 7,000 languages. Most AI training data is in English and a few dozen others.

## The Idea: Access as a Matter of Justice

The central claim of this lesson is that who can use AI, and who controls it, is a question of justice, of the kind Lesson 01-06 introduced.

The capabilities approach from that lesson says justice is about what people are actually able to do and to be. If AI tools become important for learning, working, getting medical information and taking part in public life, then people who cannot use them well, because they lack access or because the tools do not work in their language, are losing real capabilities. And if a few countries control the technology, others become dependent on decisions made elsewhere, over which they have no say.

## Concentration on Every Level

Concentration runs through every layer of AI.

| Layer | How concentrated it is |
|---|---|
| Chips | NVIDIA dominates AI accelerators; TSMC makes most advanced chips; one Dutch company, ASML, makes the machines needed to produce the most advanced ones |
| Computing capacity | Dominated by U.S. cloud companies and their Chinese counterparts |
| Leading models | A few U.S. labs and several Chinese labs |
| Talent | Concentrated in the U.S., China, the UK and a few other hubs, drawing skilled people away from the Global South |
| Data | Held by large platforms; the web is dominated by English |
| Investment | U.S. private investment in AI far exceeds any other country's |

## When AI Does Not Speak Your Language

AI's uneven performance across languages has several consequences.

Accuracy is worse. Large language models perform noticeably worse in **low-resource languages**, those with little digital text available for training, such as Yoruba, Amharic or Quechua, in accuracy, in safety and in cultural knowledge.

Safety is weaker. Safety training done mostly in English can be bypassed in other languages; a 2023 study showed that translating harmful requests into low-resource languages often got around the safeguards of a leading model.

It costs more. AI systems break text into units called tokens, and some languages need many more tokens per word than English. Since users often pay by the token and models have limits on how many tokens they can handle, speakers of those languages can pay more for the same content and fit less of it into a conversation.

And the defaults are Western. Models often reflect U.S. or Western norms, values and examples, even when answering people elsewhere.

Many groups are working to change this, including Masakhane, a community of researchers working on African languages; AI4Bharat in India; SEA-LION in Southeast Asia; multilingual open models such as BLOOM and Aya; and many national projects.

## Why Concentration Has Defenders

Concentration is not only the result of unfairness, and it has some defensible features.

Advanced AI is extraordinarily expensive and difficult to build. A handful of companies with huge resources may be the only ones able to push the frontier, and their products then reach billions of people, often for free or at low cost. Much of the world uses AI tools it could never have built.

Export controls on chips have a security rationale. Governments argue that the most advanced AI has military and intelligence uses, and that controlling access to the chips is one of the few levers available.

And some of what looks like exclusion is temporary. Prices for using AI have fallen steeply, and models whose weights are released openly, which Module 7 discusses, can be downloaded and run anywhere.

## Extraction

Critics describe a darker pattern. The scholars Nick Couldry and Ulises Mejias call it **data colonialism**: data and labor flow out of the Global South, as with the data workers in Lesson 05-07 and the mines in Lesson 06-01; the value is captured in the Global North; and local communities bear environmental costs, as with the data centers in Chile and Uruguay described in Lesson 06-03. On this view, AI repeats an old pattern in which poorer regions supply raw materials and labor while richer ones keep the profits and the power.

## Sovereign AI

Many countries are pursuing what is often called **sovereign AI**: the capacity to build and run AI without depending on foreign providers. Strategies include national investments in computing, such as India's AI Mission and programs in France, Japan, the United Arab Emirates and Saudi Arabia; national or regional models, such as Mistral in France, Falcon in the UAE and Sarvam in India; reliance on open-weight models that can be run locally; public computing for researchers, such as the U.S. pilot of a National AI Research Resource and the EU's "AI Factories"; and regional cooperation, such as the African Union's 2024 continental AI strategy.

Sovereign AI has its own problems. It can mean independence, and it can also mean state control and surveillance. Small countries cannot match the investment of the largest. And open models reduce dependence on foreign companies but not on foreign chips.

## Where People Disagree

Is it unjust that AI works best in English, and whose responsibility is it to fix? Some say the companies that profit globally owe the world tools that work in its languages. Others say it is a natural result of where the data and the customers are, and that public and community efforts are the right answer.

Should exports of AI chips be governed by national security, by global fairness, or both? The two goals often conflict.

And are open-weight models the best path to access for the world? They let anyone run AI locally, and they also let anyone remove its safeguards, a debate Module 7 takes up.

## Key Terms

- **Compute:** The computing resources used to build and run AI, especially specialized chips.
- **Export controls:** Government restrictions on exporting technology.
- **Low-resource language:** A language with little digital text available for training AI.
- **Sovereign AI:** A country's capacity to build and run AI without depending on foreign providers.
- **Data colonialism:** The extraction of data and value in ways that echo colonial patterns.
