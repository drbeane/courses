# 06-03 — Water, Land, and Local Communities

**Module:** 6 — Environmental Costs and Global Access
**Estimated time:** 40 minutes

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

1. Explain how data centers use water and why local context matters more than global totals.
2. Analyze community conflicts over data-center siting, including environmental-justice dimensions.
3. Evaluate the trade-offs local governments face: tax revenue, jobs, rates, water and air.

## Opening case: turbines across the state line

In June 2024, xAI began operating **Colossus**, a large AI supercomputer, in a former factory in South Memphis, Tennessee, near predominantly Black neighborhoods already burdened by industrial pollution. To get power quickly, xAI installed **gas-fired turbines**.

- **Colossus 1:** Up to 35 turbines ran without the required air permits, according to the Southern Environmental Law Center (SELC). After the SELC sent a notice of intent to sue on behalf of the NAACP, xAI removed them and obtained permits for its remaining 15 turbines.
- **Colossus 2** began operating in late 2025, powered by **27 gas turbines** (up to 495 MW) installed across the state line in **Southaven, Mississippi**.
- **February 2026:** SELC and Earthjustice, representing the NAACP, sent a new notice of intent to sue under the Clean Air Act, alleging the turbines were operating without permits.
- **Pollutants:** The turbines emit nitrogen oxides, fine particulates and formaldehyde. The Southaven facility could become the largest industrial NOx source in the Memphis metro area, which already fails federal smog standards. Some residents live about half a mile away.
- **Plans:** xAI plans a third Memphis-area data center.

*Status: allegations by advocacy groups in legal notices; xAI's responses should be checked ⚠.*

## Core ideas

### How data centers use water

- **Direct (onsite) use:** Evaporative cooling towers consume water. Usage varies widely by climate, cooling design and season. Some facilities use millions of liters a day.
- **Indirect use:** Water used to generate the electricity, especially from thermoelectric power plants.
- **Supply chain:** Water for chip fabrication.

**Water usage effectiveness (WUE)** measures liters per kWh. Alternatives include:

- **air cooling,** which uses more energy and less water,
- **closed-loop liquid cooling,** which uses minimal water after the initial fill, and
- **recycled or non-potable water.**

**The trade-off:** Reducing water use often increases energy use, and vice versa.

### Why local context matters

A data center's water use may be tiny globally but significant locally:

- in drought-prone regions (the American Southwest, parts of Texas, Chile, Uruguay, Spain),
- where it competes with agriculture or municipal supply, and
- where groundwater is depleting.

**Examples:**

- **Uruguay (2023):** Protests over a planned Google data center during the worst drought in decades. Google revised its plans to use air cooling.
- **Chile (2024):** A court partially halted Google's Santiago data center permit over water concerns, and Google redesigned it.
- **The Dalles, Oregon:** Google's water use became public after a 2022 lawsuit by *The Oregonian*. It was about a quarter of the city's water.

### Electricity rates and grid strain

Large data centers can require grid upgrades. Regulators and consumer advocates debate **who pays**:

- In the PJM region (mid-Atlantic and Midwest U.S.), capacity-auction prices spiked in 2024–2025, partly attributed to data-center demand. That raises bills for all customers ⚠.
- Some states and utilities created special tariffs requiring large loads to pay more of their costs, such as Ohio's 2025 AEP data-center tariff ⚠.

### Community opposition (2025–2026)

Opposition grew rapidly. Data Center Frontier (March 2026) reported:

- **San Marcos, Texas:** The council voted 5–2 against rezoning for a $1.5 billion campus whose power demand could reach 2.5 times the city's peak load.
- **Monterey Park, California:** A 45-day moratorium, and a petition with about 5,000 signatures for a permanent ban.
- **Montour County, Pennsylvania:** A rezoning was denied.
- **New Brunswick, New Jersey:** Data centers were removed as a permitted use in a redevelopment plan.
- **Scale:** Data Center Watch estimated about $98 billion in projects delayed or blocked in a single quarter of 2025. The Guardian reported 26 cancellations in December 2025–January 2026.

**Common concerns:**

- water,
- electricity rates,
- noise from cooling systems,
- diesel backup-generator emissions,
- loss of farmland and open space,
- few long-term jobs (often dozens for a large facility after construction),
- tax abatements, and
- **secrecy**: projects negotiated under NDAs with code names, so residents learn late.

### Environmental justice

Environmental justice asks whether environmental burdens fall disproportionately on marginalized communities, and whether those communities have a meaningful voice. The Memphis case illustrates classic EJ patterns:

- siting in a community with existing pollution burdens,
- rapid deployment before permits, and
- limited notice or public input.

## Applying the frameworks

- **Distributive justice:** Benefits are global, burdens local.
- **Procedural justice:** NDAs and rapid permitting deny communities a voice (01-06, Arnstein's ladder, 01-08).
- **Rights:** Clean air and water as basic interests.
- **Decency, community and non-human lenses:** Local ecosystems, aquifers and neighbors.

## Discussion questions

1. Should data-center developers be required to disclose water and energy projections publicly before local approval?
2. Are NDAs in economic-development negotiations ever justified?
3. If a data center brings $20 million a year in tax revenue to a poor county but raises electricity rates and water stress, how should residents weigh the trade-off? Who should decide?
4. Is opposition to data centers "NIMBYism" or legitimate self-determination?

## Activity: stakeholder positions (15 minutes)

For a proposed 300 MW data center in a drought-prone county (A3 option b), write a two-sentence position statement for each of:

1. the county commissioner,
2. a farmer relying on groundwater,
3. a construction union,
4. the utility,
5. a resident near the site, and
6. the developer.

Then identify which positions are based on measured, estimated, forecast or speculative claims.

## Key terms

- **Evaporative cooling:** Cooling by evaporating water, which consumes it.
- **WUE:** Water usage effectiveness (L/kWh).
- **Capacity market:** An electricity market paying for available generation capacity.
- **Environmental justice:** Fair distribution of environmental burdens and meaningful participation.
- **Large-load tariff:** A special utility rate for very large electricity users.
