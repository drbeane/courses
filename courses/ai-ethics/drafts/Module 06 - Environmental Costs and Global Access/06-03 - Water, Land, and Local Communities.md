# 06-03 — Water, Land, and Local Communities

**Module:** 6 — Environmental Costs and Global Access
**Estimated time:** 40 minutes

**In this lesson:** AI's environmental costs land in particular places: a neighborhood breathing exhaust from gas turbines, a town in a drought, a county negotiating in secret. This lesson looks at what data centers offer the places that host them, what they take, and why communities across the United States began saying no.

## Turbines Across the State Line

In June 2024 the AI company xAI began running Colossus, a large AI supercomputer, in a former factory in South Memphis, Tennessee. The site is near predominantly Black neighborhoods that already bear heavy industrial pollution. To get power quickly, xAI installed gas-fired turbines.

According to the Southern Environmental Law Center, up to 35 turbines ran at the first site without the required air permits. After the Center sent a notice that it intended to sue on behalf of the NAACP, xAI removed them and obtained permits for its remaining 15.

A second facility, Colossus 2, began operating in late 2025. Its power came from 27 gas turbines, with up to 495 megawatts of capacity, installed across the state line in Southaven, Mississippi. In February 2026 the Southern Environmental Law Center and Earthjustice, representing the NAACP, sent a new notice of intent to sue under the Clean Air Act, alleging that these turbines too were running without permits. The turbines emit nitrogen oxides, fine particles and formaldehyde. The groups said the Southaven facility could become the largest industrial source of nitrogen oxides in the Memphis area, which already fails federal smog standards. Some residents live about half a mile away. xAI has announced plans for a third data center in the area.

These are allegations made by advocacy groups in legal notices. xAI's responses, and any later rulings, should be checked before teaching.

## What a Data Center Offers a Community

Before turning to the conflicts, it is fair to say why many places compete to host data centers.

They bring very large capital investment, often billions of dollars, and with it property tax revenue that can fund schools and services, sometimes in places with few other sources of growth. Construction employs hundreds or thousands of workers for several years. Some companies pay for local infrastructure, such as grid upgrades or water treatment, that the community could not otherwise afford. Some heat nearby buildings with their waste heat, as Meta's data center in Odense, Denmark, does for the town's district heating system. And the services that run in these buildings, from hospital systems to the search engines and maps residents use every day, have to run somewhere.

For a county with a shrinking tax base, a data center can look like the best economic news in decades. The question is whether the terms are fair.

## How Data Centers Use Water

Data centers use water in three ways. **Evaporative cooling**, which cools equipment by evaporating water, consumes it on site; some large facilities use millions of liters a day, depending on climate, design and season. Generating the electricity they use consumes more water, especially at thermal power plants. And making the chips, as Lesson 06-01 described, uses large amounts of very pure water.

A facility's water efficiency is measured by **water usage effectiveness**, the liters of water used per kilowatt-hour of computing. There are alternatives to evaporative cooling: air cooling, which uses more electricity and less water; closed-loop liquid cooling, which uses little water after it is first filled; and recycled or non-drinking water. But there is a trade-off: reducing water use often increases energy use, and the reverse.

## Why Local Context Matters

A data center's water use may be tiny on a global scale and large where it is built. It matters most in places prone to drought, such as the American Southwest, parts of Texas, Chile, Uruguay and Spain; where it competes with farms or town water supplies; and where groundwater is already running down.

There have been real conflicts. In Uruguay in 2023, people protested a planned Google data center during the worst drought in decades, and Google changed its plans to use air cooling. In Chile in 2024 a court partly halted Google's permit for a data center near Santiago over water concerns, and Google redesigned it. In The Dalles, Oregon, Google's water use became public only after a newspaper sued in 2022; it turned out to be about a quarter of the city's water.

## Who Pays for the Grid?

Large data centers can require new power lines, substations and power plants, and regulators and consumer advocates argue over who pays for them. In the large power market covering much of the mid-Atlantic and Midwest, prices in the auctions that pay for available generating capacity spiked in 2024 and 2025, partly because of expected data-center demand. Those costs flow into everyone's electricity bills. Some states and utilities responded with special rates that make very large customers pay more of the costs they cause; Ohio approved one in 2025.

## Communities Say No

Opposition grew quickly in 2025 and 2026. In San Marcos, Texas, the city council voted 5 to 2 against rezoning land for a $1.5 billion campus whose power demand could have reached two and a half times the city's peak. Monterey Park, California, adopted a 45-day moratorium, and about 5,000 residents signed a petition for a permanent ban. A rezoning was denied in Montour County, Pennsylvania. New Brunswick, New Jersey, removed data centers from the uses permitted in a redevelopment plan. One tracker estimated that about $98 billion in projects were delayed or blocked in a single quarter of 2025.

Residents' concerns are consistent: water; electricity rates; noise from cooling equipment; emissions from diesel backup generators; the loss of farmland and open space; how few permanent jobs there are once construction ends, often only dozens for a large facility; the size of the tax breaks offered to win the project; and **secrecy**. Many projects are negotiated under nondisclosure agreements and code names, so that residents learn about them only when the terms are settled.

## Environmental Justice

**Environmental justice** asks whether environmental burdens fall disproportionately on communities that are already marginalized, and whether those communities have a real voice in decisions. The Memphis case shows the classic pattern: a facility sited in a community already burdened by pollution, deployed quickly before permits were in place, with little notice or chance for the public to be heard. Lesson 01-06 called the first part of that distributive justice and the second procedural justice, and Lesson 01-08's ladder of participation describes how little say residents often have.

## Where People Disagree

If a data center brings $20 million a year in tax revenue to a poor county but raises electricity rates and strains the water supply, how should residents weigh it, and who should decide? Some say the county's elected officials, who represent everyone. Others say the people living nearest should have a stronger voice, since they bear the most.

Is local opposition legitimate self-determination, or simply "not in my backyard"? Data centers have to go somewhere, and if every community refuses, the services they support either stop growing or move to places with even less power to refuse.

And are nondisclosure agreements in economic development ever justified? Companies say secrecy keeps land prices from spiking and protects competitive plans. Residents say it denies them any say until it is too late.

## Check Your Understanding

**1. What benefits can a data center bring to a community?**

:::answer Answer
Large investment and property tax revenue, construction jobs, sometimes infrastructure the community could not otherwise afford, and occasionally waste heat for nearby buildings.
:::

**2. What is the trade-off between water and energy in cooling?**

:::answer Answer
Methods that use less water, such as air cooling, generally use more electricity, and methods that use less electricity, such as evaporative cooling, use more water.
:::

**3. Why can a data center's water use be small globally but significant locally?**

:::answer Answer
Its share of global water use is tiny, but in a drought-prone area or where groundwater is falling, it can compete directly with farms and households.
:::

**4. What does environmental justice ask?**

:::answer Answer
Whether environmental burdens fall disproportionately on already marginalized communities, and whether those communities have a meaningful voice in decisions.
:::

## Key Terms

- **Evaporative cooling:** Cooling equipment by evaporating water, which consumes it.
- **Water usage effectiveness:** The liters of water a data center uses per kilowatt-hour of computing.
- **Capacity market:** An electricity market that pays for generating capacity to be available.
- **Large-load tariff:** A special electricity rate for very large users.
- **Environmental justice:** The fair distribution of environmental burdens and meaningful participation in decisions about them.
