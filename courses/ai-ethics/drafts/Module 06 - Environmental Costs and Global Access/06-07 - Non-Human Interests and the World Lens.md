# 06-07 — Non-Human Interests and the World Lens

**Module:** 6 — Environmental Costs and Global Access
**Estimated time:** 35 minutes

**In this lesson:** Most AI ethics is about people alive today. This lesson widens the circle to animals, ecosystems and people not yet born, using the last two of the four lenses from Lesson 01-05, and asks how much weight their interests should carry in decisions about AI.

## Listening to Whales

Project CETI, the Cetacean Translation Initiative, began in 2020 with an ambitious aim: to use machine learning to understand the clicking sounds, called codas, that sperm whales use to communicate. In 2024 the project's researchers reported in *Nature Communications* that sperm whale codas have a combinatorial structure, something like a phonetic alphabet, far richer than scientists had known. Similar AI projects now study the calls of elephants, bats and birds.

At the same time, the expansion of data centers, mines and power infrastructure driven partly by AI destroys habitat. And AI is used to make industrial fishing more efficient and to automate factory farms.

AI can extend moral attention to other beings, helping people understand and protect them. It can also intensify their exploitation. Which it does depends on the choices people make.

## The Idea: Moral Concern Does Not Stop at Humans

Lesson 01-05 introduced four lenses drawn from Todd May's account of decency: self, others and community, non-humans, and the world. This lesson takes up the last two.

Many ethical traditions extend moral concern beyond humans. The utilitarian tradition, from Jeremy Bentham to Peter Singer, holds that what matters is the capacity to suffer. In Bentham's phrase, "The question is not, Can they reason? nor, Can they talk? but, Can they suffer?" On this view, the suffering of sentient animals counts in the moral calculation. **Sentience** is the capacity to have experiences, including pain.

Rights theorists such as Tom Regan argue that animals who are "subjects of a life," with their own experiences and interests, have value in themselves, not just as resources for people.

Environmental ethics goes further, to whole ecosystems. Aldo Leopold's **land ethic** holds that "a thing is right when it tends to preserve the integrity, stability, and beauty of the biotic community."

And many Indigenous and relational traditions see people as living in relationships of reciprocity with land, water and other beings, relationships that carry obligations.

## Why It Persuades

These views draw on intuitions most people already hold. Nearly everyone thinks it is wrong to cause an animal pointless suffering, and many people feel that destroying a wetland or a species is a loss even when no human is harmed. A framework that counts only human interests cannot explain those reactions.

The view also corrects a blind spot. Lesson 01-03 warned that what is easy to measure crowds out what is not. The interests of animals and ecosystems are rarely measured at all, so in ordinary cost-benefit analyses they count for nothing. Naming them is the first step to giving them weight.

## AI and Non-Humans: Both Directions

AI affects animals and ecosystems in both directions, and often the same technology appears on both sides.

| Harmful uses and effects | Beneficial uses |
|---|---|
| Habitat lost to data centers, mines and power lines | Monitoring biodiversity with camera traps, sound recorders and DNA from water samples |
| Water withdrawals affecting rivers and wetlands | Planning anti-poaching patrols |
| Precision livestock farming used to confine more animals | Detecting disease in wild animals |
| Making industrial fishing more efficient | Detecting illegal fishing from satellite data, as Global Fishing Watch does |
| Electronic waste and toxic contamination | Identifying species, as the iNaturalist and Merlin apps do |
| Light and noise from facilities | Research on animal communication, such as Project CETI |

**Precision livestock farming**, which uses sensors and AI to monitor individual farm animals, shows how one technology serves different values. It can detect illness early and improve animals' welfare. It can also be used to keep more animals in less space and push their productivity harder, which makes their welfare worse.

## The World and Future Generations

May's world lens asks about effects on society as a whole, on the planet and on the shared conditions of life: the climate, the quality of public information discussed in Module 4, democratic institutions, global stability, and the conditions for future flourishing, human and non-human.

Many decisions about AI have long-lasting effects. Carbon dioxide stays in the atmosphere for centuries. Data centers and power plants, once built, operate for decades, as Lesson 06-03 suggested. Institutions and political arrangements around AI may be hard to reverse once they set. And some people argue that the most advanced AI could pose risks to humanity's long-term future, a question Module 7 takes up.

How much should people living now owe to those who will live later? There are three broad positions. A strong position holds that future people count exactly as much as present people; since there may be vastly more of them, their interests may outweigh ours. This view is called **longtermism**. A moderate position holds that future people count, but that uncertainty about the distant future reduces the weight we can reasonably give to it. And a critical position warns that longtermism can be used to justify neglecting present, measurable harms in favor of speculative futures, the problem Lesson 01-03 described, and points out that present injustice also shapes the future. Rawls proposed a middle path he called the **just savings principle**: each generation should pass on to the next the conditions for a just society.

## Where It Breaks

Extending moral concern raises hard problems.

It is hard to compare interests across species. How should the suffering of an animal be weighed against a human benefit? Different traditions give very different answers.

It can be hard to act on. Almost every human activity affects some animals or ecosystems. A principle that counts all of them equally could rule out almost anything.

The interests of ecosystems and future people must be represented by someone else, which raises the question of who speaks for them, and whether their spokespeople are really advancing their own views.

And longtermism, taken to its extreme, can make present people's urgent needs seem small next to vast imagined futures.

## Key Terms

- **Sentience:** The capacity to have experiences, including suffering.
- **Land ethic:** Aldo Leopold's ethic of preserving the integrity of the biotic community.
- **Precision livestock farming:** Using sensors and data to monitor and manage individual farm animals.
- **Longtermism:** The view that improving the long-term future is a key moral priority.
- **Just savings principle:** Rawls's principle that each generation should pass on the conditions for a just society.
