# 01-06 — Justice, Fairness, and Distribution

**Module:** 1 — Ethical Foundations and AI in Society
**Estimated time:** 35 minutes

**In this lesson:** Even when a system does more good than harm, it matters who gets the good and who gets the harm, and who had a say. This lesson sets out what justice asks and why "fair" turns out to mean several different things.

## Who Pays for the Data Center?

A company proposes a very large data center in a rural county. The scenario is a composite of several real proposals from 2025 and 2026. The company promises jobs and tax revenue. The county offers a large tax break to win the project. Residents worry about the water supply in a region prone to drought, about higher electricity bills and about noise. Most of the people who will use the services running in that building live somewhere else. So do most of the shareholders.

Suppose you did the consequentialist sum from Lesson 01-03 and the total benefit came out larger than the total cost. A question would remain that the sum cannot answer. The benefits go to people far away, and the costs stay with the people who live there. Is that fair? And did the people who live there have any real part in the decision?

## Four Questions Justice Asks

Justice is about how people stand in relation to one another: what each is owed, and whether each is treated as an equal. It helps to separate four questions.

**Distributive justice** asks who gets which benefits and which burdens. When AI makes a company more productive, do the gains go to its workers or its owners? In the data center case, who gets the cloud services and who gets the noise?

**Procedural justice** asks whether the decision was made fairly. Were the residents told about the project before the vote on the tax break? Could they change the outcome? In a different setting, can a job applicant appeal a rejection? A fair process matters in itself, apart from what it produces. People will accept a loss more readily when they were heard.

**Corrective justice** asks whether wrongs are put right. Is a person who was wrongly arrested compensated? Is a fake image taken down?

**Relational justice** asks whether people are treated as equals in their dealings with each other and with institutions. Surveillance concentrated on certain neighborhoods may never result in a wrongful arrest and still send a message that the people who live there are suspects by default.

## Choosing Without Knowing Who You Will Be

The most influential modern account of justice is John Rawls's, in *A Theory of Justice* (1971). Rawls proposed a thought experiment. Imagine choosing the rules for your society from behind a **veil of ignorance**. You do not know what your race, sex, class, talents or health will be, or where in society you will end up. Whatever rules you choose, you will then have to live under them from a position you cannot predict.

Rawls argued that people choosing this way would agree on two principles. The first is equal basic liberties for everyone. The second is that social and economic inequalities are acceptable only under two conditions: they must be attached to positions open to all under fair equality of opportunity, and they must work to the benefit of the least advantaged. That last condition is the **difference principle**. An inequality is just if it leaves the worst-off better off than they would otherwise be.

The veil is easy to apply to an AI system, and it is often revealing. Would you accept a hiring algorithm if you did not know whether you would be a 25-year-old with a degree from a famous university or a 55-year-old with a disability and a gap in your résumé? Would you accept live facial recognition if you did not know whether you would live in the neighborhood where the cameras go up? Would you sign the data center deal not knowing whether you would be a shareholder or a farmer whose well is running low?

The veil does not always say no. Behind it, you might well choose a medical screening tool that is imperfect but catches disease earlier for everyone, or a fraud detector that occasionally freezes an honest account. It asks whether you could accept the arrangement from every position in it, including the worst one.

## Why It Persuades

Justice supplies what the earlier frameworks lacked. A consequentialist total is indifferent to who gains and who loses, and justice makes that the central question. Rights protect individuals one at a time, while justice looks at the pattern across a whole society. Character matters, but a system can be unjust even when everyone in it means well.

It also has a practical advantage. The veil of ignorance is a test anyone can run. It asks you to stop reasoning from your own position, which for most people who design and buy AI systems is a comfortable one.

## Other Views of Justice

Rawls is not the only account, and the alternatives pull in different directions.

The **libertarian** view, associated with Robert Nozick, holds that a distribution is just if it came about through just steps: things were acquired fairly and transferred voluntarily. If people consented to give up their data and the market was free, the outcome is just, whatever it looks like. Critics reply that this ignores how unequal people's starting positions are and how thin consent often is, a point Lesson 01-04 made.

The **capabilities approach** of Amartya Sen and Martha Nussbaum says justice is about what people are actually able to do and to be: to be healthy, to be educated, to take part in political life, to form relationships. An AI system should be judged by whether it widens or narrows those real freedoms. The data center might widen them through jobs and school funding, or narrow them by threatening the water supply. It might do both, for different people.

Iris Marion Young described **structural injustice**: harm that arises when many people each act within the normal rules and no one of them does anything clearly wrong. Nobody set out to make data labeling a poorly paid job done far from the companies that profit from it. Young's conclusion is that everyone who takes part in such a structure shares some responsibility for changing it. That applies with special force to AI supply chains.

## Fair in What Sense?

People appeal to fairness constantly in arguments about AI, and they often mean different things by it. Fair can mean treating everyone exactly the same. It can mean treating people the same when they are alike in the ways that matter. It can mean producing similar outcomes across groups. It can mean giving more to those who need more. Or it can mean giving people what they deserve.

These ideas conflict. A lender that applies one rule to everyone may produce very different approval rates across groups. A lender that equalizes approval rates must treat some individual applicants differently. Module 3 shows that this is more than a matter of opinion: several common statistical definitions of fairness cannot all be satisfied at the same time. So when someone says a system is fair, the useful reply is "fair in what sense?" Choosing among the senses is an ethical decision. No amount of engineering makes it for you.

## Who Decides

Justice also concerns power: who gets to make the decision in the first place.

AI development is concentrated. A handful of companies control the most capable models, most of the computing capacity and much of the data. The communities and individuals affected by a system are rarely in the room when it is designed or bought. Four questions help expose this. Who chose to deploy the system, and could the people affected have refused? Who is able to inspect it? Who profits, and who carries the risk if it fails? And whose knowledge is treated as evidence: the vendor's test results, or the residents' experience of their own water supply?

Several real data center projects in 2025 were negotiated under nondisclosure agreements, so that residents learned of them only when the terms were already settled. A deal like that can turn out well for a town and still be procedurally unjust.

## Where It Breaks

Justice arguments have limits too.

There is no agreement on which account is right. Rawls and Nozick reach opposite verdicts on the same facts, so "this is unjust" can simply mean "this offends the theory I hold."

A focus on distribution can lose sight of the total. An arrangement that leaves everyone better off, but some far more than others, may be worth having. The difference principle actually accepts such arrangements when the worst-off gain, but people who invoke justice loosely sometimes oppose them anyway.

Fair process is slow and costly. Real consultation delays projects, including good ones, and somebody bears the cost of the delay.

And structural accounts spread responsibility so widely that it can evaporate. If everyone is responsible for the conditions of data workers, it can be hard to say what any one person or company must do.

## Back to the County

Apply the four questions to the opening case. On distribution, the benefits flow outward to distant users and shareholders, while the water use, the noise and the higher electricity rates stay local. Local jobs and tax revenue are the offsetting gain, and it matters a great deal how many jobs there really are and how large the tax break is. On procedure, the question is whether residents knew and could affect the outcome before the vote. On correction, what happens if the wells do run low: is there a commitment, in writing, about who pays? On relations, is this county being treated as a partner or as a convenient place to put something nobody else wanted?

Then put it behind the veil. If the deal still looks acceptable from the position of the farmer with the failing well, it may be a just one. If it looks acceptable only to the shareholder, the total benefit does not rescue it.

## Check Your Understanding

**1. What is the difference between distributive and procedural justice?**

:::answer Answer
Distributive justice concerns who receives the benefits and burdens. Procedural justice concerns whether the process of deciding was fair, including whether affected people were informed and heard.
:::

**2. What is the veil of ignorance?**

:::answer Answer
Rawls's thought experiment: choose the rules for society without knowing your own race, sex, class, abilities or position, so that you must be able to accept the rules from any position, including the worst.
:::

**3. What does the difference principle say?**

:::answer Answer
Social and economic inequalities are just only if they work to the benefit of the least advantaged members of society.
:::

**4. Why is "fair" not a single standard?**

:::answer Answer
It can mean identical treatment, similar treatment of similar people, similar outcomes across groups, more for those in greater need, or what people deserve. These conflict, and choosing among them is an ethical decision.
:::

## Key Terms

- **Distributive justice:** Fairness in who receives benefits and burdens.
- **Procedural justice:** Fairness in how a decision is made.
- **Corrective justice:** The righting of wrongs through remedy or compensation.
- **Relational justice:** Treating people as equals in social relationships.
- **Veil of ignorance:** Choosing principles without knowing one's own place in society.
- **Difference principle:** Inequalities are just only if they benefit the least advantaged.
- **Capabilities approach:** Justice measured by people's real freedoms to be and to do.
- **Structural injustice:** Injustice produced by many people acting within normal rules, without a single wrongdoer.
