# 04-07 — Synthetic Media That Helps: Translation, Access, and Restored Voices

**Module:** 4 — Synthetic Media, Information Integrity, and Human Agency
**Estimated time:** 35 minutes

**In this lesson:** The technology behind deepfakes can also give a person back their own voice, let someone be understood in a language they do not speak, and describe a film to someone who cannot see it. This lesson looks at those uses, who gains, what can still go wrong, and why consent is what separates them from the harms earlier in this module.

## A Speech in Her Own Voice

In 2023 Jennifer Wexton, a member of the U.S. House of Representatives from Virginia, announced that she had progressive supranuclear palsy, a rare neurological disease that was steadily taking away her ability to speak clearly. In July 2024 she gave a speech on the floor of the House. The voice that delivered it was her own, as it had sounded before the illness. It had been rebuilt by an AI voice company from recordings of her earlier speeches, and it spoke the words she had written. It was reported to be the first time a member had addressed the House in an AI-generated voice.

The technique was the same voice cloning that, as Lesson 04-03 described, lets criminals impersonate a chief financial officer or a grandchild. The difference was that Wexton asked for it, controlled it and used it to say her own words.

*Details of this case should be checked against current reporting before teaching.*

## Restored Voices

Losing the ability to speak, through diseases such as ALS, through throat cancer or through stroke, is one of the most isolating things that can happen to a person. Devices that turn typed text into speech have existed for decades, but for most of that time they offered a small set of generic voices. People spoke through a voice that was not theirs and often did not match their age, sex or accent.

**Voice banking** changed that. A person who expects to lose their speech records themselves while they still can, and the recordings are used to build a synthetic version of their voice. Older methods required many hours of careful recording, often too much for someone already losing strength. AI has cut the amount needed dramatically. In 2023 Apple added a feature called Personal Voice to its phones, which builds a synthetic voice from about fifteen minutes of recorded phrases, processed on the device itself. Some companies can reconstruct a voice from old home videos or voicemails, for people who never recorded deliberately, as in Wexton's case. Some AI voice companies have offered these services free to people with ALS.

People who use their banked voices describe the difference as being heard as themselves. Their children hear a parent's voice. Their colleagues hear someone they recognize.

## Understood in Any Language

Generative AI has also changed translation. Speech can now be translated into another language and spoken in a voice that resembles the original speaker's. Text can be translated among hundreds of languages, including many that commercial translation tools long ignored because they had few speakers or little written material. Video can be dubbed so that the speaker's lips match the new language.

The people who gain most are often those with least access to professional translation: immigrants dealing with schools, clinics and government offices; patients receiving medical information; small businesses selling to customers abroad; and speakers of smaller languages who can now read material that was never translated for them. Public health agencies, schools and courts can publish information in many more languages than they could afford before.

## Seeing and Hearing for Others

Synthetic media also helps people with sensory disabilities beyond the assistive tools in Lesson 02-08. AI can generate **audio description**, a spoken narration of what is happening on screen, for films and videos that would otherwise be inaccessible to blind viewers, at a fraction of the cost of a human describer. It can turn speech into captions for deaf viewers in real time, and turn text into natural-sounding speech for people with dyslexia or low vision.

## What Can Still Go Wrong

These uses are not free of risk, and some of the risks are serious.

**Consent can be stretched.** A voice built with someone's consent, for their own use, is very different from a voice used after their death, or for purposes they never agreed to. Before his death in 2024, the actor James Earl Jones agreed to let a company recreate his voice for the character Darth Vader. That was consent given in life. Many other uses of dead performers' voices have been made with only their estates' agreement, or none.

**A voice model is a security risk.** A synthetic voice good enough to speak for someone is good enough to impersonate them. Banking a voice creates a valuable file that must be protected.

**Translation errors can matter enormously.** A mistranslation in a casual conversation is an inconvenience. In a medical consultation, a court hearing or an asylum interview, it can be dangerous. AI translation is least reliable in exactly the smaller languages where it is most needed, because less training material exists.

**It can replace people as well as help them.** Voice actors, translators, interpreters and audio describers have seen work disappear. In 2024 and 2025, video game voice actors represented by the union SAG-AFTRA went on strike, with protections against AI replicas of their voices a central issue. A technology that helps a patient speak can also cost an interpreter a living. Module 5 takes up these questions.

**Depending on a company is fragile.** A person whose voice lives on a company's servers depends on that company's prices, policies and survival.

## Consent Is the Line

Set this lesson beside Lesson 04-04 and the contrast is stark. The tools are related. The difference is almost entirely about consent and control.

In the Grok case, a real person's likeness was used without their knowledge, for others' purposes, against their interests. In voice banking, a person's likeness is used at their request, for their own purposes, under their control. Translation and audio description depict no one who has not agreed to it, or depict no one at all.

This suggests what good practice looks like. The person whose voice or face is used gives informed consent, specific to the purpose. They can withdraw it. The model is protected against theft and misuse. Output is labeled where people might be deceived. And in high-stakes settings, such as medicine, courts and asylum claims, translation is checked by a qualified person.

## Where People Disagree

Should synthetic voices of people who have died be allowed, and if so, who should decide: the person while alive, their family, or no one? Some see it as a way to remember people. Others see it as putting words in the mouths of people who can no longer object.

Should public services replace human interpreters with AI translation to reach more languages and more people? It would serve many people who now get no translation at all. It would also mean errors in settings where errors can be grave, and the loss of skilled jobs.

And should laws written to stop harmful deepfakes include exceptions for these uses? A rule against synthetic voices that ignores consent would harm people like Wexton. A rule that exempts "assistive" uses would need a way to tell them apart from fraud.

## Check Your Understanding

**1. What is voice banking, and how has AI changed it?**

:::answer Answer
Recording one's voice while still able to speak, so a synthetic version can be built for later use. AI has greatly reduced the recording needed, and can sometimes rebuild a voice from old recordings.
:::

**2. Who gains most from AI translation, and where is it least reliable?**

:::answer Answer
People with least access to professional translation, such as immigrants, patients and speakers of smaller languages. It is least reliable in smaller languages, where less training material exists.
:::

**3. Name two risks that remain even when synthetic media is used with consent.**

:::answer Answer
Any two of these: the voice model can be stolen and used for impersonation; translation errors in high-stakes settings; loss of work for voice actors, translators and describers; dependence on a company's services and policies; consent being stretched to uses the person never agreed to.
:::

**4. What distinguishes voice banking from the Grok case?**

:::answer Answer
Consent and control. In voice banking the person asks for their likeness to be used, for their own purposes, under their control. In the Grok case it was used without consent, for others' purposes, against the person's interests.
:::

## Key Terms

- **Voice banking:** Recording one's voice so that a synthetic version can be built for later use.
- **Audio description:** Spoken narration of what is happening on screen, for blind and low-vision viewers.
- **Synthetic voice:** Speech generated by software to sound like a particular person.
