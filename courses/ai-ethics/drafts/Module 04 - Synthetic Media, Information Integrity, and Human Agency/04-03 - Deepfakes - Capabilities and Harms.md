# 04-03 — Deepfakes: Capabilities and Harms

**Module:** 4 — Synthetic Media, Information Integrity, and Human Agency
**Estimated time:** 35 minutes

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

1. Describe current capabilities for synthetic faces, voices and video.
2. Categorize deepfake harms: fraud, reputational, sexual, political and epistemic.
3. Evaluate legitimate and beneficial uses of synthetic media, and where lines might be drawn.

## Opening case: the video call that cost $25 million

In early 2024, Hong Kong police reported that a finance employee at the engineering firm Arup had transferred about HK$200 million (roughly US$25 million) after a video conference with what appeared to be the company's UK-based chief financial officer and several colleagues. **Every other participant on the call was a deepfake**, generated from publicly available video and audio. The employee had initially been suspicious of an email request, but the video call convinced them.

By 2026, real-time face and voice swapping run on consumer hardware. Voice cloning needs only a few seconds of sample audio.

## Core ideas

### Capabilities

| Modality | Capability (as of 2026) | Inputs needed |
|---|---|---|
| **Voice cloning** | Realistic voice in real time, in multiple languages | Seconds to minutes of audio |
| **Face swap (video)** | Real-time swap on live video calls | A few photos or short video |
| **Lip-sync / dubbing** | Make a person appear to say new words | Video plus target audio |
| **Full synthetic video** | Text-to-video of realistic scenes and people (Sora 2, Veo 3 and successors) | A text prompt; sometimes reference images |
| **Image editing** | Photorealistic edits of real photos, including "undressing" (04-04) | One photo |

Detection has not kept pace. Human ability to distinguish synthetic from real faces and voices is near chance for high-quality fakes, and automated detectors generalize poorly to new generation methods (see 04-07).

### A taxonomy of harms

| Harm | Description | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Fraud** | Impersonation for money or access | Arup; CEO voice scams; "grandparent" voice scams; bypassing voice authentication at banks |
| **Sexual abuse** | Non-consensual intimate imagery (NCII) | Nudify apps; Grok (04-04) |
| **Reputational** | False depictions damaging someone | Fake videos of executives, teachers or classmates; a Baltimore-area school athletic director created a deepfake audio of the principal making racist remarks (2024) |
| **Political** | Fabricated statements by candidates; voter suppression | Biden robocall (04-06); deepfakes in elections in India, Slovakia and elsewhere |
| **Epistemic** | Eroding trust in all evidence | The "liar's dividend": real recordings dismissed as fakes (04-06) |
| **Harassment and bullying** | Mocking or humiliating content | School cases worldwide |
| **Identity and likeness** | Unauthorized commercial use of a person's face or voice | Unauthorized celebrity endorsements in scam ads; disputes over voice actors' cloned voices |

### Who is targeted?

- **Women and girls** overwhelmingly, for sexual deepfakes. Studies since 2019 consistently find that the large majority of deepfake content online is non-consensual pornography, almost all depicting women.
- **Older people,** for voice-clone scams.
- **Public figures,** for political and reputational fakes, but increasingly ordinary people, because only a few photos are needed.
- **Employees,** for business email and video compromise.

### Legitimate uses

Synthetic media isn't inherently harmful:

- **Accessibility:** Voice banking for people losing speech to ALS (e.g., restoring a person's own voice).
- **Film and dubbing:** De-aging; localization; posthumous performances, which are controversial.
- **Education and history:** Reconstructions, clearly labeled.
- **Satire and art:** A long tradition of parody, which First Amendment law protects.
- **Privacy:** Anonymizing witnesses or sources in documentaries (e.g., *Welcome to Chechnya*, 2020).

The ethical line usually depends on:

1. **consent** of the person depicted,
2. **deception**: will viewers believe it's real?
3. **purpose and harm**, and
4. **labeling**.

## Applying the frameworks

- **Deontology:** Using someone's likeness without consent, especially sexually or to deceive, treats them merely as a means.
- **Consequences:** Fraud losses and harassment vs. creative and accessibility benefits.
- **Justice:** Harms fall disproportionately on women, minors and the elderly.
- **Decency, world lens:** What happens to trust, evidence and journalism when seeing is no longer believing?

## Discussion questions

1. Is a clearly labeled deepfake of a politician for satire ethically acceptable? What about an unlabeled one?
2. Should the creators of voice-cloning tools be required to verify consent from the person whose voice is cloned? Is that feasible?
3. Who should bear the loss in the Arup case: the employee, the company, the bank, the video-platform provider, or the AI tool developer?
4. Can you think of a use of deepfake technology that would be beneficial but is currently prohibited or stigmatized?

## Activity: family safe word (10 minutes)

Many security experts now recommend families and teams agree on a **verification phrase** for urgent requests by phone or video. Draft a short policy for (a) your family or (b) a small finance team covering:

1. when verification is required,
2. how it's done (call-back to a known number, a safe word, a second approver), and
3. what to do if verification fails.

Discuss: what does it say about society that this is now necessary?

## Key terms

- **Deepfake:** Synthetic media realistically depicting a real person doing or saying something they didn't.
- **Voice cloning:** Synthesizing speech in a specific person's voice.
- **NCII:** Non-consensual intimate imagery.
- **Right of publicity:** Legal right to control commercial use of one's name, image and likeness.
