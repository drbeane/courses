# 05-03 — What AI Assistance Does for Workers: Productivity and Skill

**Module:** 5 — Work, Labor, and Economic Power
**Estimated time:** 35 minutes

**In this lesson:** Most of this module is about what AI takes from workers. This lesson looks at the best evidence on what it gives them: measured gains in speed and quality, which tend to be largest for the least experienced. It also asks when that help turns into dependence, and whether people still learn when a machine does part of the work.

## A Call Center Learns Faster

In 2020 a large software company began giving some of its customer-support agents an AI assistant. The agents worked by text chat, helping small businesses with technical problems. The assistant watched each conversation and suggested responses and links to relevant documentation. Agents could use the suggestions or ignore them. Because the tool was rolled out to different agents at different times, economists Erik Brynjolfsson, Danielle Li and Lindsey Raymond could compare agents who had it with similar agents who did not yet have it. The study covered more than 5,000 agents.

The results, published in the *Quarterly Journal of Economics* in 2025, were striking. On average, agents with the assistant resolved about 14 percent more issues per hour. But the average hid the most interesting finding. For the least experienced and least skilled agents, productivity rose by about 34 percent. For the most experienced and skilled agents, it barely changed.

The assistant appeared to be spreading the know-how of the best agents to everyone else. New agents with the tool performed, after two months, about as well as agents without it did after six months. Customers were more polite to agents who had the assistant, and agents were less likely to quit.

## What the Evidence Shows

The call-center study is one of several careful experiments with similar findings.

In a 2023 study published in *Science*, Shakked Noy and Whitney Zhang gave several hundred college-educated professionals realistic writing tasks, such as press releases and short reports, and randomly gave some of them access to ChatGPT. Those with the tool took about 40 percent less time, and independent graders rated their work about 18 percent higher in quality. Again, the weaker writers gained most, so the gap between strong and weak writers narrowed.

In a 2023 experiment, researchers at Microsoft and elsewhere asked programmers to write a small web server in JavaScript. Those given GitHub Copilot, an AI coding assistant, finished about 56 percent faster.

And in a large field experiment with the consulting firm Boston Consulting Group, published as a working paper in 2023, consultants using GPT-4 completed realistic consulting tasks faster and with higher-quality results, with the largest gains again among those who started out weaker.

*Figures from these studies should be checked against the published versions before teaching.*

## Who Gains

Three patterns run through this evidence.

The least experienced workers often gain most. AI seems to work partly as a way of passing on the knowledge of experts, which narrows the gap between beginners and veterans.

Workers who gain can do better work, not only faster work. In several studies, quality rose along with speed.

And the work can become less stressful. In the call-center study, customers were more courteous and agents stayed longer.

This matters for the distribution questions that run through this module. A technology that helps beginners most could, in principle, open opportunities to people without elite training. Whether it does depends on who captures the gains, which is the subject of Lesson 05-04.

## Where the Help Runs Out

The same studies, and others, show clear limits.

The help is uneven, and the boundary is hard to see. In the Boston Consulting Group experiment, the researchers also gave consultants a task that was designed to fall outside what GPT-4 could do well. On that task, consultants using the AI did *worse* than those without it, because they trusted its plausible but wrong answers. The researchers called this the **jagged frontier**: AI is very good at some tasks and poor at others that look similar, and users cannot easily tell which is which.

Experts do not always gain. In a 2025 study by the research group METR, experienced open-source software developers working on code they knew well were about 19 percent *slower* when using AI tools, even though they believed the tools had made them faster. Their own estimates and the measurements pointed in opposite directions.

And the gains are in particular tasks, not whole jobs. A study of one task does not tell you how a job made of dozens of tasks will change.

## Learning or Leaning?

The deepest question is about skill. If AI helps beginners perform like experts, do they become experts, or do they come to depend on the tool?

The evidence is mixed. In the call-center study, agents who had used the assistant kept some of their improvement during periods when the tool was briefly unavailable, which suggests they had learned from it. But a 2024 experiment with high school math students in Turkey, by Hamsa Bastani and colleagues, found something more worrying. Students who practiced with an unrestricted GPT-4 tutor did much better on practice problems. When the tool was taken away for the exam, they did worse than students who had never used it. A version of the tutor designed to give hints instead of answers largely avoided that harm.

The difference between those two designs is the important finding. Whether AI builds skill or erodes it depends a great deal on how the tool is designed and how people are taught to use it. Lesson 05-08 looks at the related danger of **deskilling** in professions, where reliance on automation wears away abilities that professionals need when the automation fails.

## Where People Disagree

Is AI that narrows the gap between beginners and experts good news for workers? For beginners, plainly yes: they become productive sooner. For experienced workers, it may mean their hard-won skill commands less of a premium. And employers may respond by hiring fewer people overall, which connects this lesson back to the missing rung in Lesson 05-02.

Should schools and employers restrict AI use for learners? Restriction protects learning but holds back productivity and leaves people unpracticed with tools they will use at work. Design that guides instead of answering may be a middle path.

And who should get the gains? A worker who becomes 34 percent more productive might reasonably expect some of that in pay. Whether they get it depends on markets and bargaining power, not on the technology.

Think of a task you do regularly for a class or a job where AI could help. Describe how you would use it in a way that makes you better at the task over time, and how you would use it in a way that leaves you dependent on it. Then write a rule for yourself that keeps you on the first path.

## Key Terms

- **Augmentation:** AI making workers more productive in their existing jobs.
- **Jagged frontier:** The uneven and hard-to-see boundary between tasks AI does well and tasks it does poorly.
- **Deskilling:** The loss of skills through reliance on automation.
