# 05-04 — Algorithmic Management and Bossware

**Module:** 5 — Work, Labor, and Economic Power
**Estimated time:** 35 minutes

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

1. Define algorithmic management and describe its main functions: directing, evaluating and disciplining.
2. Analyze the effects of algorithmic management on worker autonomy, health and bargaining power.
3. Evaluate employer justifications and worker objections.

## Opening case: "Time Off Task"

Amazon fulfillment centers have used systems that track workers' productivity in real time, including scan rates and "Time Off Task" (TOT), the minutes when a worker's scanner is idle. Reporting since 2019 (e.g., *The Verge*, *The New York Times*, the Strategic Organizing Center) described workers receiving warnings or terminations generated automatically from these metrics, and workers skipping bathroom breaks to avoid TOT.

- **2024:** France's data-protection authority (CNIL) fined Amazon France Logistique **€32 million**, finding monitoring of scanner inactivity at a minute-level granularity "excessively intrusive" and certain indicators unlawful under GDPR.
- **Legislation:** California (AB 701, 2021), New York (2022), Washington and Minnesota passed laws requiring warehouse employers to disclose quotas and prohibiting quotas that prevent breaks.
- **Amazon's position:** It said its systems aren't used to fire workers without human review and that it has adjusted TOT practices.

## Core ideas

### What is algorithmic management?

**Algorithmic management** is the use of software and data to perform functions traditionally done by human managers (Kellogg, Valentine & Christin, 2020):

| Function | Examples |
|---|---|
| **Direct** | Task assignment; scheduling; route optimization; pacing |
| **Evaluate** | Productivity scores; customer ratings; activity tracking; sentiment analysis |
| **Discipline** | Automated warnings; deactivation; pay adjustments; termination recommendations |

It began in gig work and warehouses but has spread to call centers, retail, healthcare (nurse scheduling), trucking, hospitality and office work.

### "Bossware"

**Bossware** refers to employee-monitoring software, especially for remote or office workers. It can capture:

- keystrokes and mouse activity,
- screenshots or screen recordings,
- webcam images,
- application and website usage,
- email and chat content,
- location, and
- "productivity scores" combining these.

Surveys in 2025–2026 suggest workplace monitoring is common and growing. One Australian survey reported that nearly half of workplaces use AI to monitor employees ⚠ (verify the methodology). AI enables new layers of monitoring, such as sentiment analysis of messages, "flight risk" predictions and automated summaries of employee activity.

### Effects on workers

Research and testimony describe:

- **Intensification:** Pace set by algorithm; less recovery time; higher injury rates (warehouse injury studies).
- **Loss of autonomy:** Workers can't use discretion or judgment.
- **Opacity:** Workers don't know how they're evaluated or why decisions were made.
- **Stress and health:** Constant monitoring is associated with stress and lower job satisfaction (APA *Work in America* surveys).
- **Gaming and distrust:** Workers learn to perform for the metric (Goodhart's law, 01-03), such as "mouse jigglers."
- **Power asymmetry:** Employers know everything about workers. Workers know little about the system.
- **Chilling of organizing:** Monitoring can detect and deter union activity. The NLRB General Counsel warned about this in a 2022 memo ⚠ (status after 2025).

### Employer justifications

- **Efficiency and coordination** at scale.
- **Fairness:** Objective metrics may be less biased than managers' favoritism.
- **Safety:** Fatigue monitoring for truck drivers, for example.
- **Security and compliance:** Data-loss prevention; regulated industries.
- **Remote work accountability.**

### The middle ground

Some algorithmic tools help workers: fair scheduling with advance notice, transparent task allocation, and safety alerts. The ethical questions are:

- **Proportionality:** Is monitoring proportionate to a legitimate purpose?
- **Transparency:** Do workers know what's collected and how it's used?
- **Voice:** Were workers consulted?
- **Human review:** Are consequential decisions reviewed by humans with discretion?
- **Data use limits:** Is data used only for its stated purpose?

## Applying the frameworks

- **Deontology and dignity:** Treating workers as inputs to be optimized; minute-level surveillance of bodily needs.
- **Consequences:** Efficiency gains vs. injuries, turnover and stress.
- **Justice:** Monitoring is most intense for low-wage workers with the least bargaining power.
- **Decency, self lens:** Would you build this system? Would you work under it?

## Discussion questions

1. Is measuring "Time Off Task" inherently disrespectful, or is it acceptable with sufficient break allowances?
2. Are algorithmic metrics fairer than human managers' judgments? When?
3. If you were asked as an engineer to build employee-sentiment analysis of Slack messages, what would you do?
4. Should workers have a right to see all data collected about them at work and how it's used?

## Activity: monitoring proportionality test (15 minutes)

For each practice, judge it **proportionate / disproportionate / depends** and explain in one sentence:

1. GPS tracking of delivery drivers during shifts.
2. GPS tracking of delivery drivers' personal phones after hours.
3. Keystroke logging of remote accountants.
4. AI analysis of customer-service calls for quality coaching.
5. AI analysis of employees' Slack messages to predict resignation.
6. Webcam snapshots every 10 minutes for remote workers.

## Key terms

- **Algorithmic management:** Using software to direct, evaluate and discipline workers.
- **Bossware:** Employee-monitoring software.
- **Time Off Task (TOT):** Idle time measured by scanner inactivity.
- **Work intensification:** Increased pace and effort demands.
- **Proportionality:** Matching the intrusiveness of a measure to the importance of its purpose.
