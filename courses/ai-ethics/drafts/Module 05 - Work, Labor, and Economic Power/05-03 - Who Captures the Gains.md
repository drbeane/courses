# 05-03 — Who Captures the Gains?

**Module:** 5 — Work, Labor, and Economic Power
**Estimated time:** 35 minutes

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

1. Explain how productivity gains from AI can be distributed among workers, firms, consumers and capital owners.
2. Analyze market concentration in the AI industry and its implications for economic power.
3. Evaluate proposals for broadly sharing AI's benefits.

## Opening case: productivity up, for whom?

Suppose an AI system makes a team of 10 customer-service agents 30% more productive. The firm could:

1. **Keep all 10 agents** and handle more customers (growth),
2. **Cut 2–3 agents** and keep output constant (cost savings),
3. **Raise wages** to share the gains,
4. **Cut prices**, passing gains to customers, or
5. **Return the savings** to shareholders.

Technology doesn't choose among these. Market structure, bargaining power, competition and policy do. Daron Acemoglu and Simon Johnson's *Power and Progress* (2023) argues that history shows productivity gains are shared broadly only when **countervailing powers**, such as unions, regulation and competition, force sharing.

## Core ideas

### Historical patterns

- **The Industrial Revolution:** Wages for many workers stagnated for decades ("Engels' pause") before rising.
- **Post-WWII U.S. (1948–1973):** Productivity and wages rose together.
- **Since the 1970s:** U.S. productivity has grown much faster than typical workers' compensation. The labor share of income has declined, though measurement is debated.
- **Automation vs. new tasks:** Acemoglu & Restrepo (2019) show that automation displaces labor, while new tasks reinstate it. Outcomes depend on which dominates.

### "So-so automation"

Acemoglu and Restrepo warn against **so-so automation**: technologies good enough to replace workers but not productive enough to generate large gains, such as self-checkout kiosks. These shift income from labor to capital without much overall growth. Some AI deployments, like chatbots that frustrate customers but cut call-center staff, may fit this pattern.

### Concentration in AI

The AI value chain is highly concentrated:

| Layer | Leading players (2026) | Concentration concern |
|---|---|---|
| Chips | NVIDIA (dominant in AI accelerators); TSMC (manufacturing); ASML (lithography) | Single points of failure; pricing power |
| Cloud compute | AWS, Microsoft Azure, Google Cloud | Control of access to compute |
| Frontier models | OpenAI, Anthropic, Google DeepMind, Meta, xAI, plus Chinese labs (DeepSeek, Alibaba Qwen, Moonshot) | High fixed costs; partnerships with cloud giants |
| Distribution | Operating systems, search, productivity suites | Bundling AI into dominant platforms |

**Concerns** (raised by the FTC's 2024 6(b) study of AI partnerships, the UK CMA and the EU Commission):

- cloud-model partnerships (Microsoft–OpenAI, Amazon/Google–Anthropic) may entrench incumbents,
- "acqui-hires" of startups (e.g., Microsoft–Inflection, Google–Character.AI) avoid merger review,
- access to proprietary data creates moats, and
- the open-weight debate (Module 7) is partly about concentration.

### Proposals for broad sharing

| Proposal | Idea | Debate |
|---|---|---|
| **Worker bargaining** | Unions negotiate AI terms (05-09) | Union density is low in most AI-affected sectors |
| **Profit sharing / employee ownership** | Workers share automation gains | Voluntary; limited uptake |
| **Tax policy** | Tax capital more, or tax automation ("robot tax") | Hard to define automation; may slow beneficial adoption |
| **Universal basic income** | Unconditional cash payments | Costly; evidence from pilots on work effects is mixed |
| **Public options / compute** | Public AI infrastructure (e.g., the U.S. NAIRR pilot; national compute programs) | Scale relative to private investment |
| **Competition policy** | Prevent monopolization | Enforcement priorities vary by administration |
| **Data dividends** | Pay people for data contributions | Valuation problems |
| **Directed innovation** | Steer R&D toward worker-augmenting AI | How to measure "augmenting" |

## Applying the frameworks

- **Justice (Rawls):** The difference principle permits inequality only if it benefits the least advantaged. Do current AI gains?
- **Structural injustice:** No single actor causes gains to concentrate. Market structures do.
- **Consequences:** Rapid adoption may maximize growth, but distribution affects social stability and well-being.
- **Decency, world lens:** What kind of economy should AI build?

## Discussion questions

1. If AI raises productivity but lowers the labor share of income, is that a problem? For whom?
2. Is "so-so automation" a fair description of most current AI deployments you've seen?
3. Should governments steer AI development toward augmenting workers rather than replacing them? How?
4. Which proposal for sharing gains is most promising? Which is least?

## Activity: distribution scenario (15 minutes)

Return to the opening case. Assume a 30% productivity gain worth $300,000 a year. Propose a distribution among workers, customers, shareholders and reinvestment. Justify it using two frameworks. Then explain what would have to be true about markets and power for your distribution to actually happen.

## Key terms

- **Labor share:** The portion of national income paid to workers.
- **So-so automation:** Replacing workers with marginal productivity gains.
- **Countervailing power:** Forces (unions, regulation, competition) that check concentrated power.
- **Acqui-hire:** Acquiring a company mainly for its staff.
