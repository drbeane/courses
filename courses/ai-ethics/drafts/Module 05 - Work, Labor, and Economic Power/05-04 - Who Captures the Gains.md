# 05-04 — Who Captures the Gains?

**Module:** 5 — Work, Labor, and Economic Power
**Estimated time:** 35 minutes

**In this lesson:** If AI makes work more productive, someone gets the benefit: workers, customers, owners or all three. This lesson argues that technology does not decide who, and looks at history, at the concentration of the AI industry, and at the proposals for sharing the gains more widely.

## Productivity Up, for Whom?

Lesson 05-03 described an AI assistant that made customer-service agents about 14 percent more productive on average. Suppose a company gets a gain like that, or larger, from its own team of ten agents. It has several choices.

It could keep all ten agents and serve more customers, growing the business. It could cut two or three agents and serve the same number of customers at lower cost. It could raise the agents' pay. It could cut its prices. Or it could return the savings to its owners. Most real companies would do some mix of these.

The technology does not choose among them. The choice is shaped by competition, by how much bargaining power the workers have, and by law and policy.

## The Idea: Gains Are Shared When Someone Makes Them Shared

The economists Daron Acemoglu and Simon Johnson, in their 2023 book *Power and Progress*, argue that history supports a simple and uncomfortable claim. New technology raises productivity, but the gains are shared broadly only when **countervailing power**, meaning forces such as unions, regulation and competition that check concentrated power, makes them shared. Without it, the gains tend to go to those who own the technology.

The idea has a long history behind it. During the early Industrial Revolution in Britain, wages for many workers stagnated for decades even as output rose, a period economists sometimes call "Engels' pause" after the observer who documented it. In the United States from 1948 to 1973, by contrast, productivity and typical workers' pay rose together, in an era of strong unions and rising regulation. Since the 1970s, U.S. productivity has grown much faster than the pay of typical workers, and the share of national income paid as wages, the **labor share**, has fallen, although economists debate exactly how to measure it.

## Why It Persuades

The idea explains why the same technology can lead to very different outcomes in different places and times. It shifts attention from the technology, which is hard to stop, to institutions, which people can change. And it fits the evidence from Lesson 05-03: a technology that makes beginners as productive as veterans could raise beginners' pay, or could let employers hire cheaper beginners in place of veterans. Which happens depends on bargaining, not on the software.

The economists Acemoglu and Pascual Restrepo add a second idea. Automation displaces workers from tasks, but technology also creates new tasks, which reinstates demand for labor. How workers fare depends on which effect is larger. They warn especially about **so-so automation**: technology that is good enough to replace workers but not productive enough to generate much overall gain. Self-checkout machines are their example. Such technology shifts income from workers to owners without making the economy much richer. Some AI deployments may fit this pattern, such as a chatbot that frustrates customers but lets a company cut its call-center staff.

## A Concentrated Industry

The question of who captures AI's gains is also a question about the AI industry itself, which is highly concentrated.

| Layer | Leading companies (2026) | Concern |
|---|---|---|
| Chips | NVIDIA, dominant in AI accelerators; TSMC, which manufactures them; ASML, which makes the machines that make them | Single points of failure; pricing power |
| Cloud computing | Amazon Web Services, Microsoft Azure, Google Cloud | Control over who gets computing power |
| Leading models | OpenAI, Anthropic, Google DeepMind, Meta and xAI, plus Chinese labs such as DeepSeek, Alibaba and Moonshot | Very high fixed costs; partnerships with cloud giants |
| Distribution | Operating systems, search engines, office software | AI bundled into products people already depend on |

Competition authorities in the United States, the UK and the EU have raised concerns. Partnerships between cloud companies and model developers may entrench the largest firms. Large companies have hired away startups' staff and licensed their technology in ways that avoided merger review. Control of proprietary data can protect incumbents from competition. And the debate over openly releasing model weights, which Module 7 covers, is partly a debate about concentration.

Concentration matters here because the companies that capture the most value from AI are in a strong position to keep it.

## Proposals for Sharing

Many proposals aim to spread the gains more widely, and each has defenders and critics.

| Proposal | The idea | The debate |
|---|---|---|
| Worker bargaining | Unions negotiate the terms of AI use (Lesson 05-09) | Few workers in AI-affected fields are unionized |
| Profit sharing and employee ownership | Workers share in automation's gains | Voluntary; limited uptake |
| Tax policy | Tax capital more, or tax automation | Hard to define automation; could slow helpful adoption |
| Universal basic income | Regular unconditional cash payments | Expensive; evidence from pilots on work is mixed |
| Public computing | Government-funded AI infrastructure for researchers | Small compared with private investment |
| Competition policy | Prevent monopolization | Priorities change with each administration |
| Data dividends | Pay people for the data their activity produces | Hard to value data |
| Directed innovation | Steer research toward AI that helps workers instead of replacing them | Hard to measure which is which |

## Where It Breaks

The countervailing-power account has critics.

Some economists argue that technology gains do spread broadly over time through lower prices and new products, even without unions or regulation, and that the post-war era was unusual. Consumers, they point out, are also workers, and cheaper goods and services raise everyone's real income.

The measurement of the labor share is disputed, and some of its decline may reflect changes in housing, accounting or global trade, not technology.

And measures to force sharing can backfire. A tax on automation may slow adoption of technology that would have made everyone better off. Strong bargaining in one country can push investment to another.

## Where People Disagree

If AI raises productivity but lowers the share of income paid to workers, is that a problem? For whom? Rawls's difference principle, from Lesson 01-06, would ask whether the least advantaged end up better off.

Should governments try to steer AI toward helping workers instead of replacing them? Supporters say the direction of technology is a choice, and public money already shapes it. Opponents say governments are poor at picking technologies.

And which proposal for sharing the gains is most promising? Each puts the burden in a different place.

## Check Your Understanding

**1. What is countervailing power, and what role does it play in Acemoglu and Johnson's argument?**

:::answer Answer
Forces such as unions, regulation and competition that check concentrated power. They argue that technology's gains are shared broadly only when such forces make them shared.
:::

**2. What is so-so automation?**

:::answer Answer
Technology good enough to replace workers but not productive enough to generate large gains, so it shifts income from workers to owners without much overall growth.
:::

**3. What is the labor share, and what has happened to it in the United States since the 1970s?**

:::answer Answer
The portion of national income paid to workers. It has declined, as productivity has grown faster than typical pay, though its measurement is debated.
:::

**4. Give one criticism of the countervailing-power account.**

:::answer Answer
Any of these: gains may spread through lower prices and new products even without unions or regulation; the decline in the labor share may have other causes; forcing sharing can slow helpful technology or push investment elsewhere.
:::

## Key Terms

- **Labor share:** The portion of national income paid to workers.
- **Countervailing power:** Forces such as unions, regulation and competition that check concentrated power.
- **So-so automation:** Automation that replaces workers with only small gains in productivity.
