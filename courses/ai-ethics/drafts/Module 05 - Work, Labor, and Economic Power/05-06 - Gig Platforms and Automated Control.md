# 05-06 — Gig Platforms and Automated Control

**Module:** 5 — Work, Labor, and Economic Power
**Estimated time:** 35 minutes

**In this lesson:** Ride-hailing and delivery platforms are managed almost entirely by software, which assigns work, sets pay and can end a worker's access with an email. This lesson looks at the flexibility these platforms offer, how their algorithms control workers, the courts' and lawmakers' responses, and what workers have done for themselves.

## Deactivated by Email

In 2021, drivers in the UK and Portugal, supported by the App Drivers & Couriers Union and the advocacy group Worker Info Exchange, took Uber and the ride-hailing company Ola to court in Amsterdam, where both had European headquarters. The drivers had been "deactivated," meaning cut off from the app and therefore from their work, on the basis of automated fraud-detection systems, sometimes with little or no human involvement and no explanation. Uber had flagged some of them for "fraudulent activity" based on location data, and the drivers could not find out what they were supposed to have done.

The court ordered Uber to reinstate some drivers and to give them information about the automated decisions, finding violations of the GDPR's limits on decisions made solely by machines. In April 2023 the Amsterdam Court of Appeal went further, requiring more transparency about how the automated decisions worked, including the factors behind Ola's "fraud probability score" and how Uber matched drivers to rides.

## Why People Choose Gig Work

Before looking at the harms, it is worth understanding why millions of people choose this work.

The main attractions are extra income and flexibility. A driver can log on for two hours before picking up children from school, work late on weekends, or stop for a week without asking anyone's permission. In a 2021 Pew Research Center survey, earning extra money and being able to set their own schedule were among the reasons gig workers most often gave for doing the work. For students, caregivers, people with health conditions that make a fixed schedule hard, and people between jobs, that flexibility can be the difference between working and not working.

The platforms also lower the barrier to entry. There is no interview and often no résumé. For immigrants and others who face discrimination in traditional hiring, an app that gives everyone the same access can be a way in. And for customers, the platforms have made rides and deliveries cheaper and more available, including in neighborhoods that taxis once avoided.

The question is whether the platforms can offer this flexibility without the forms of control that come with it.

## Management by Algorithm

Gig platforms manage through software at every step.

| Function | How it works | What workers experience |
|---|---|---|
| Assignment | Matching algorithms decide who gets which ride or delivery | They cannot see why some get better jobs |
| Pay | Prices and per-task pay are set by algorithm, with bonuses and "quests" | Unpredictable earnings; different pay for similar work |
| Rating | Customer ratings determine access to work | Customers' biases become employment decisions |
| Discipline | Automated deactivation for low ratings, declined jobs or fraud flags | Losing a livelihood without explanation or appeal |
| Identity checks | Workers take selfies that are matched to their profile | More failures for darker-skinned workers |

That last row has a well-known case. Pa Edrissa Manjang, a Black Uber Eats courier in the UK, was removed from the app after the facial verification system repeatedly failed to match his selfies. He brought a race discrimination claim, which was settled in 2024.

## Algorithmic Wage Discrimination

The legal scholar Veena Dubal describes a practice she calls **algorithmic wage discrimination**: platforms use detailed data about each worker to pay different workers different amounts for the same work, based on predictions of what each will accept. Workers describe it as a gamble. The same trip pays differently on different days or to different drivers, and incentives change without explanation.

Critics see three problems. It breaks the long-standing norm of equal pay for equal work. It exploits the platform's vastly greater information. And it makes earnings unpredictable, which makes it hard for workers to plan their lives. Platforms answer that dynamic pay balances supply and demand, getting drivers where they are needed, and that it is part of what makes the flexible model work.

## Employee or Contractor?

Most worker protections, including the minimum wage, unemployment insurance, anti-discrimination law and the right to bargain collectively, depend on being an employee. Platforms classify their workers as independent contractors. Whether that is accurate is a long-running fight.

In 2021 the UK Supreme Court held in *Uber v. Aslam* that Uber drivers are "workers," entitled to the minimum wage and paid holidays. California passed a law in 2019 presuming that workers are employees, but voters approved a ballot measure in 2020 exempting app-based drivers, which the California Supreme Court upheld in 2024. The European Union's Platform Work Directive, adopted in 2024, creates a **rebuttable presumption** of employment, meaning platform workers are presumed to be employees when the facts show the platform controls their work, unless the platform proves otherwise. Member states must put it into law by December 2026.

## The EU's Rules on Algorithmic Management

The Platform Work Directive also contains the most comprehensive rules on algorithmic management to date, and they apply to all platform workers, including genuine contractors. Platforms must be transparent about the automated systems that monitor workers and make decisions about them. They may not process data about workers' emotional or psychological states, their private conversations, their activities while off duty, or anything meant to predict collective organizing. People must oversee automated decisions and evaluate them regularly. Workers have a right to an explanation and a review of significant decisions, with a human they can contact. Decisions to restrict, suspend or end a worker's account must be made by a person. And platforms must consult workers' representatives before introducing or changing these systems.

*The status of the Directive's transposition and of other legal developments is as of October 2026 and should be checked before teaching.*

## What Workers Have Done

Workers have not waited for the law. Worker Info Exchange uses workers' rights under the GDPR to request their own data, and then uses it to challenge decisions. App-based workers have formed associations and organized coordinated strikes. Some have built their own tools that pool their data to reveal patterns in pay. And some cities have acted: New York City set a minimum pay rate for app-based delivery workers in 2023, and Seattle set its own standards.

## Where People Disagree

Should platforms be forbidden to pay different workers different amounts for the same task? It would protect fairness and predictability. It might also reduce the flexibility of pay that helps match drivers to demand.

Should every deactivation require a human decision? The EU now says yes. It costs platforms money, and they argue it slows their response to genuine fraud and danger.

Should customer ratings, which reflect customers' biases, be used to discipline workers? They are the platforms' main tool for quality. They also import discrimination.

And is the flexibility of gig work a real benefit that regulation might destroy? Many workers say it is. Others say that true flexibility requires predictable pay and protection from arbitrary dismissal, which current platforms do not offer.

Paying different workers different amounts for the same work, based on the platform's predictions of what each will accept.

:::answer Answer

:::answer Answer

## Key Terms

- **Deactivation:** Removing a gig worker's access to a platform.
- **Algorithmic wage discrimination:** Personalized pay for the same work, based on predictions of what each worker will accept.
- **Misclassification:** Treating employees as independent contractors.
- **Rebuttable presumption:** A legal assumption that holds unless disproven.
