# 05-01 — Exposure Is Not Displacement: Reading Labor Evidence

**Module:** 5 — Work, Labor, and Economic Power
**Estimated time:** 35 minutes

**In this lesson:** Claims about AI and jobs range from "no effect" to "the end of work," and many of them are built on different kinds of evidence that answer different questions. This lesson gives you the vocabulary to tell them apart and a set of questions to ask of any headline about AI and employment.

## Two Headlines, One Month

In August 2026, readers could find both of these headlines:

- "Stanford Finds No Broad AI Job Displacement"
- "AI Driving Decline in Entry-Level Hiring, Survey Finds"

They look like a contradiction. They are not. Both were accurate summaries of real research. Total employment can hold steady while a particular group, in this case young workers in the jobs most exposed to AI, sees real losses. At the same time, some companies announcing layoffs said AI was the reason, and others were accused of blaming AI for cuts they were making for other reasons.

Making sense of AI's effects on work starts with knowing what each kind of evidence can and cannot show.

## The Idea: Four Different Questions

Most confusion about AI and jobs comes from treating four different questions as one.

**Exposure** asks how much of a job's tasks AI *could* affect. It is usually estimated by breaking occupations into tasks and judging which tasks current AI can do. A widely cited 2023 study by Tyna Eloundou and colleagues estimated that about 80 percent of U.S. workers have at least 10 percent of their tasks exposed to large language models.

**Adoption** asks whether employers and workers actually use AI, and for what. The evidence comes from surveys and usage data, such as the U.S. Census Bureau's business surveys and reports AI companies publish about how their products are used.

**Displacement** asks whether jobs or hours are actually lost because of AI. The evidence comes from employment and payroll records and from studies of particular firms.

**Augmentation** asks whether AI makes workers more productive in the jobs they already have. The evidence comes from field experiments and productivity studies. Lesson 05-03 looks at that evidence closely.

The single most important point in this lesson is that **exposure is not destiny**. A task that AI could do may be automated, may be done faster by a person working with AI, or may be left alone, depending on cost, quality, regulation, liability and the choices organizations make. A headline that says "300 million jobs exposed to AI" is reporting a forecast of exposure. It is not a measurement of jobs lost.

## How Strong Is the Claim?

This course asks you to label claims by how they were produced, and nowhere is that more useful than in debates about jobs.

| Label | What it means | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Measured** | Directly observed in data | Employment of 22-to-25-year-olds in highly exposed jobs was 19 percent below the comparison trend in payroll data |
| **Estimated** | Derived from data using modeling assumptions | The share of tasks in an occupation that AI could perform |
| **Forecast** | A projection of the future | Millions of jobs will be displaced by 2030 |
| **Speculative** | A plausible scenario without strong evidence | Advanced AI will make most human work unnecessary within a decade |

None of these labels means a claim is worthless. Forecasts are useful for planning. But they should not be presented, or acted on, as if they were measurements.

## Why This Discipline Matters

Getting the evidence right has practical consequences in both directions.

If policymakers treat speculative forecasts as facts, they may adopt expensive or restrictive policies against harms that never arrive, and slow down uses of AI that would have helped people. If they wait for perfect measurement, they may act only after a generation of workers has already paid the cost. Students choosing a field of study face the same problem on a personal scale.

The discipline also protects against being used. Companies, consultancies, AI developers and advocacy groups all produce numbers about AI and work, and each has interests. Asking who produced a number, and with what method, is part of reading it.

## Reading a Layoff Announcement

When a company says it is cutting jobs because of AI, the claim may be true. It may also be convenient. Attributing cuts to AI signals innovation to investors and can deflect blame from other causes, such as having hired too many people earlier, higher interest rates or trade costs. Attributing decisions to AI for reputational reasons is sometimes called **AI-washing**.

Four questions help. Which roles were actually cut? Was AI really put to work on those tasks? Did the company hire more people elsewhere? And what does the company say in its formal filings, where misstatements carry legal risk?

## Why Distribution Matters More Than Totals

Even if total employment holds steady, AI can still change a great deal about work. It can shift work from some occupations, ages or regions to others. It can change the quality of jobs, making them more intense or more closely monitored, which Lesson 05-05 covers. It can change workers' bargaining power. And it can shift income from wages to profits, the subject of Lesson 05-04.

This is the lesson of Lesson 01-03 applied to labor: a total can look fine while a particular group bears a concentrated loss. A steady unemployment rate is little comfort to a 23-year-old who cannot get a first job in the field they trained for.

## Where It Breaks

The four-concept framework has limits too.

Exposure estimates depend on judgments about what AI can do, and those judgments change quickly; a list made in 2023 may be badly out of date by 2026.

Measured effects arrive late. By the time payroll data show a change clearly, it may be well under way.

Cause is hard to establish. Labor markets are moved by many forces at once, and separating AI from interest rates, trade and the business cycle requires careful methods and often cannot be done with confidence.

And some effects are hard to measure at all, such as changes in how satisfying or stressful a job is, or in what a worker learns.

## Check Your Understanding

**1. What is the difference between exposure and displacement?**

:::answer Answer
Exposure is how much of a job's tasks AI could affect. Displacement is jobs or hours actually lost because of AI. High exposure does not necessarily lead to displacement.
:::

**2. Why can both headlines in the opening case be true?**

:::answer Answer
Total employment can be stable while a particular group, young workers in exposed jobs, sees real declines.
:::

**3. Label this claim: "AI will eliminate 40 percent of jobs by 2035."**

:::answer Answer
A forecast, or speculative if it rests on little evidence. It is not a measurement.
:::

**4. Name two questions to ask about a company's claim that it cut jobs because of AI.**

:::answer Answer
Any two of these: which roles were cut; whether AI was actually deployed for those tasks; whether hiring increased elsewhere; what the company's formal filings say.
:::

## Key Terms

- **Exposure:** The share of a job's tasks that AI could affect.
- **Adoption:** The actual use of AI by employers and workers.
- **Displacement:** Jobs or hours lost because AI replaces labor.
- **Augmentation:** AI making workers more productive in their existing jobs.
- **AI-washing:** Attributing decisions to AI for reputational or strategic reasons.
