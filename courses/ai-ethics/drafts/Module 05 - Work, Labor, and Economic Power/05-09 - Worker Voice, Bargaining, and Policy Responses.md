# 05-09 — Worker Voice, Bargaining, and Policy Responses

**Module:** 5 — Work, Labor, and Economic Power
**Estimated time:** 45 minutes (includes the A3 launch)

**In this lesson:** Workers and governments are not simply waiting to see what AI does to work. This lesson looks at the first major union contracts on AI, the other ways workers get a say, the policy choices governments face, and the choices you will make as someone who builds these systems. It also launches A3.

## The Writers' and Actors' Strikes

In 2023 the Writers Guild of America struck for 148 days, and the actors' union SAG-AFTRA for 118. AI was one of the central issues.

The writers' contract, agreed in September 2023, set several rules. AI cannot write or rewrite scripts, and material generated by AI does not count as source material, which protects writers' credit and pay. Writers may choose to use AI, but studios cannot require them to. Studios must tell writers if material handed to them was generated by AI. And the union reserved the right to argue that using writers' work to train AI is prohibited.

The actors' contract, agreed in November 2023, required informed consent and payment for creating and using "digital replicas" of performers, and set limits on fully synthetic performers, with notice to the union. Video game performers, represented by the same union, struck in 2024 and 2025 over similar protections and reached an agreement in 2025.

These were among the first major collective agreements anywhere that governed generative AI. Their significance is in what they did not do. They did not ban AI. They used bargaining power to shape how it would be used, on terms the workers could accept.

*The status of later agreements should be checked before teaching.*

## Ways Workers Get a Say

Collective bargaining is one of several ways workers influence the AI used in their workplaces.

| Mechanism | What it is | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Collective bargaining | Unions negotiate terms with employers | The writers' and actors' contracts; the Communications Workers of America on call-center AI; National Nurses United on clinical AI |
| Works councils | Elected worker bodies with legal rights to share in decisions | Germany; the EU AI Act's requirement to inform workers' representatives about high-risk AI |
| Consultation requirements | Laws requiring employers to consult before introducing technology | The EU Platform Work Directive; a proposed UK requirement |
| Framework agreements | Agreements between unions and technology companies | The AFL-CIO and Microsoft's 2023 partnership on AI and workers |
| Professional bodies | Standards set by professions | Medical, legal and engineering codes (Lesson 05-08) |
| Individual rights | Rights to notice, access and to contest decisions | California's privacy and automated-decision rules |

Most American workers are not in unions, and union membership is especially low in many of the fields most affected by AI. For them, individual rights and laws matter more.

## What Governments Can Do

Governments face a menu of options, each aimed at a different goal.

| Goal | Options | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Cushion transitions | Modernized unemployment insurance; **wage insurance**, which tops up the pay of displaced workers who take lower-paying jobs; retraining | The evidence on retraining is mixed. The best programs are **sectoral**, designed with employers in a specific industry |
| Protect job quality | Limits on monitoring; quota laws; rules on algorithmic management (Lessons 05-05 and 05-06) | |
| Preserve entry routes | Incentives for apprenticeships (Lesson 05-02) | |
| Share the gains | Tax policy; profit sharing; employee ownership (Lesson 05-04) | |
| Steer innovation | Public research funding for AI that helps workers; standards in government purchasing | |
| Improve the data | Official statistics on AI adoption and job changes | Better data makes better policy possible (Lesson 05-01) |
| Set income floors | Basic income pilots; expanded tax credits | Contested |

## Why This Approach Persuades

Giving workers a voice has a strong case behind it. It follows from procedural justice, Lesson 01-06's principle that people affected by a decision should have a say in it. It draws on knowledge that managers and engineers often lack: workers know which tasks are hard, which shortcuts are dangerous, and which tools actually help. Lesson 05-03 showed that AI's benefits depend heavily on how it is introduced; people who will use a tool can help introduce it well. And workers who have shaped a technology are more likely to accept and use it.

## Where It Breaks

Worker voice and policy responses have real limits.

Bargaining can slow beneficial adoption. A union that protects its members' current jobs may resist a tool that would make the work better or the service cheaper for the public.

It protects insiders. Union contracts cover the workers who have them. They do little for the young people who are never hired, the missing rung of Lesson 05-02.

Policy is slow and often aims at yesterday's problems. Retraining programs built for factory closures may not fit the workers AI affects.

And there is a question of who pays. Transition support, wage insurance and income floors cost money, and the debates over who should bear that cost, taxpayers, employers or AI companies, are unresolved.

## What AI Companies Owe

Some AI companies publish research on how their products affect work, such as reports on how their tools are used across occupations, and fund economic policy research. Two questions follow. Is that research independent of the companies' commercial interests? And do developers owe more than research, such as funding transitions or designing products that help workers instead of replacing them?

## Your Role

Many people taking this course will build or deploy AI in workplaces. The choices that matter are often small and made early: whether to automate a task or help a person do it; whether to involve the workers affected in the design; whether to monitor, and if so how proportionately; whether to design for skill growth or for dependence; and whether to raise concerns, through legitimate channels, when something seems wrong.

**2. Why is collective bargaining a limited answer for most American workers?**

:::answer Answer

:::

**3. What is wage insurance?**

:::answer Answer
Payments to displaced workers who take lower-paying jobs, topping up part of the difference.
:::

:::answer Answer

## Key Terms

- **Collective bargaining:** Negotiation between employers and workers' representatives.
- **Digital replica:** An AI-generated likeness of a performer's voice or image.
- **Wage insurance:** Payments to displaced workers who take lower-paying jobs.
- **Sectoral training:** Job training designed with employers in a specific industry.
