# 08-10 — Portfolio Defense and Course Synthesis

**Module:** 8 — Governance, Accountability, and Responsible Deployment
**Estimated time:** 60 minutes plus the defense

**In this lesson:** The course ends with three things: defending your deployment decision, reviewing a classmate's, and stepping back to see what the whole course adds up to. You will finish by writing commitments you can carry into your own work with AI.

## Part 1: Your Defense (A5)

Your defense is a ten-minute recorded presentation, followed by written answers to two questions from reviewers. A structure that works:

| Minutes | Content |
|---|---|
| 0–1 | The system and the decision: what you recommend, in one sentence |
| 1–3 | Purpose, the alternatives, and the people affected |
| 3–5 | Key evidence: your test results, including one failure and what changed |
| 5–7 | The biggest risks and the safeguards against them, with owners and triggers |
| 7–8 | Rights, obligations and redress, with dates |
| 8–9 | Your recommendation, the cost of the alternative, and the conditions for reconsidering |
| 9–10 | What you are least sure about |

Lead with the decision; do not bury it. Show evidence, not adjectives: "an impact ratio of 0.67 for Black applicants," not "some bias." Name your uncertainties before reviewers do. And if you recommend deploying, say who is accountable for each risk that remains.

## Part 2: Peer Review (A6)

You will review one classmate's dossier using the A5 rubric. Your review, of 500 to 700 words, must summarize the recommendation in two sentences, to show you understood it; identify the two weakest claims in its evidence and explain why; propose one stakeholder or harm it missed; judge whether the recommendation follows from the evidence; and pose two questions for the author to answer in writing.

Good reviews are specific, citing sections and claims; focused on evidence, criticizing the reasoning and not the author's values; constructive, suggesting how to strengthen the work; and **calibrated**, matching the strength of each criticism to the strength of the problem. Being severe earns no credit. Being accurate does. The author then responds in 200 words or fewer.

## Part 3: What the Course Adds Up To

### Module by Module

| Module | The central lessons |
|---|---|
| 1 | Reason with several frameworks; law and ethics are different; systems are more than models; always ask "compared to what?" |
| 2 | Privacy is about appropriate flows; facial recognition errors fall on real people; the same sensing that threatens bystanders gives blind and deaf people access |
| 3 | Bias enters through labels and proxies; fairness measures conflict; algorithms can beat human judgment, so the baseline matters; people deserve to contest decisions |
| 4 | Generative AI fabricates; deepfakes harm real people, especially women and girls; with consent, the same tools restore voices and cross languages; checking is an ethical duty |
| 5 | Exposure is not displacement; AI helps beginners most and threatens the first rung; who gains depends on power; workers need a voice |
| 6 | AI has a physical footprint whose burdens are local; efficiency alone is not enough; AI also forecasts floods and storms; non-humans count |
| 7 | Openness trades concentration against diffusion; agents turn errors into events; the skill that attacks also defends |
| 8 | Governance is plural and changing; accountability must be designed; deployment is a judgment that counts the cost of no as well as yes |

### The Threads That Ran Through It

**Who bears the error?** In every module, the costs of AI's mistakes fell hardest on people with less power.

**Compared to what?** A system is never good or bad in the abstract, only better or worse than the realistic alternative, and refusing a system that helps also has victims.

**The law moved; ethics did not.** Colorado's law, the EU's amendments, the fight over preemption: legal requirements changed repeatedly in 2025 and 2026. The ethical questions stayed the same.

**Evidence discipline.** The best analyses separated what was measured from what was forecast, and allegations from facts.

**Power and concentration.** Who builds, who decides, and who can refuse.

**The four lenses.** Self, others, non-humans and the world.

## Part 4: Your Professional Ethic

Your final reflection is ungraded but required to complete the course. In 400 to 600 words, answer five questions. Which case in this course changed your thinking most, and how? Which framework do you find most useful in practice, and which least persuasive? Write three commitments you will carry into your work with AI, making them specific and checkable: "I will not deploy a model that affects people without results broken down by group," not "I will be ethical." Describe one situation in which you expect those commitments to be tested, and how you will respond. And what question about AI and society do you think will matter most in five years that this course did not address?

## A Closing Thought

Todd May's decency asks something modest: do not treat others merely as means, give their interests reasonable weight, and do not take advantage of their vulnerability. Most of the failures in this course were not failures of genius. They were failures of decency at scale: an arrest without corroboration, a tool released without safeguards against foreseeable misuse, a test environment that was not really isolated, a debt notice with no way to answer it.

The successes were mostly decency at scale too: a voice rebuilt at its owner's request, a forecast that gave people days to prepare, a flaw fixed before anyone was hurt.

Many of you will build, buy or govern these systems. The aim of this course is that you will be the person in the room who asks: who bears the error, who would this help, what is the evidence, and what would change our minds?

## Key Terms

- **Decision dossier:** The integrated A5 document supporting a deployment recommendation.
- **Peer review:** An evidence-focused critique of a classmate's work (A6).
- **Calibration:** Matching the confidence of a claim, or the strength of a criticism, to the strength of the evidence.
