# 08-02 — The United States: Agencies, States, and Preemption

**Module:** 8 — Governance, Accountability, and Responsible Deployment
**Estimated time:** 40 minutes

**In this lesson:** The United States has no comprehensive AI law. This lesson explains what governs AI there instead: older laws that apply to any technology, shifting federal enforcement, a growing body of state laws, and a fight over whether Washington can override the states. It ends with how a practitioner should cope with all of it.

*Everything in this lesson is changing quickly. Dates and statuses are as of October 2026 and should be checked before teaching.*

## The Colorado Story, in Full

Lesson 01-09 introduced Colorado's AI law. Here is the full sequence.

In May 2024, Colorado passed SB 24-205, the first broad state AI law in the United States, modeled partly on the EU's approach. Developers and deployers of "high-risk" AI used in consequential decisions, about employment, lending, housing, health care, education, insurance and legal services, would have had to use reasonable care to avoid algorithmic discrimination, carry out impact assessments, give notice and allow appeals. The governor signed it "with reservations."

In August 2025, a special session of the legislature delayed it to June 30, 2026.

On December 11, 2025, the President signed an executive order titled "Ensuring a National Policy Framework for Artificial Intelligence." It directed the Justice Department to create a task force to challenge state AI laws, told the Commerce Department to identify "onerous" state laws, naming Colorado's as an example, and asked agencies to consider tying certain federal funds, including broadband funding, to states' AI policies. The Attorney General set up the task force on January 9, 2026.

In April 2026, a federal court put enforcement of Colorado's law on hold, in a challenge that the Justice Department and the AI company xAI had joined.

On May 14, 2026, Colorado replaced the law with a narrower one, SB 26-189, effective January 1, 2027. It dropped the high-risk framework, the risk-management programs and the impact assessments. For **automated decision-making technology** that materially influences consequential decisions, it requires deployers to keep records for three years, give notice, explain an adverse decision in plain language within 30 days, let people correct inaccurate data, and offer meaningful human review "to the extent commercially reasonable."

## The Law That Already Applies

The absence of an AI statute does not mean AI is unregulated. Many existing laws apply whatever technology is used.

| Area | Laws | How they reach AI |
|---|---|---|
| Civil rights | Federal employment, age and disability discrimination laws; the Fair Housing Act; the Equal Credit Opportunity Act | Hiring, housing and lending (Module 3) |
| Consumer protection | The Federal Trade Commission Act's ban on unfair or deceptive practices | Deceptive claims about AI; unfair data practices; enforcing the TAKE IT DOWN Act (Lesson 04-04) |
| Credit | The Fair Credit Reporting Act; the Equal Credit Opportunity Act | Adverse action notices (Lesson 03-05) |
| Privacy | Children's privacy law, health privacy law, state privacy laws, biometric laws | Children's data, health data, biometrics (Module 2) |
| Product liability | State law on defective products | Lawsuits over harmful AI products are emerging |
| Copyright | The Copyright Act | Training and outputs (Lesson 04-09) |
| Communications | The Telephone Consumer Protection Act | AI-voiced robocalls (Lesson 04-05) |

## Federal Enforcement Has Shifted

In 2023 and 2024, federal agencies emphasized that existing law applies to AI. Four agencies issued a joint statement saying so in April 2023, the Federal Trade Commission brought cases against deceptive AI claims, and a 2023 executive order directed safety testing and reporting for powerful models.

In 2025 and 2026, the emphasis changed. The 2023 executive order was revoked in January 2025. The administration's AI Action Plan of July 2025 stressed innovation, infrastructure, open models and removing regulatory barriers. Agencies withdrew some AI guidance, including the Equal Employment Opportunity Commission's guidance on AI in hiring. An executive order told agencies to give less priority to disparate-impact cases. The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau's capacity was sharply reduced. The Federal Trade Commission continued some AI enforcement, including against deceptive claims, under the TAKE IT DOWN Act, and in its inquiry into companion chatbots (Lesson 04-08).

In Congress, a proposed ten-year ban on state AI laws was stripped from the 2025 budget bill by a Senate vote of 99 to 1. Proposals to override state laws continued in 2026. The TAKE IT DOWN Act of 2025 is one of the few federal laws aimed specifically at AI.

## The States Step In

States have passed hundreds of AI-related laws. The broad categories include laws on high-risk uses, such as Colorado's original law and Texas's narrower law effective January 2026, which mainly covers government use and certain prohibited purposes; transparency laws for the most powerful models, such as California's SB 53, effective January 2026, which requires large developers to publish safety frameworks, report critical safety incidents and protect whistleblowers; employment laws (Lesson 03-03); laws on deepfakes and elections, now in most states (Lessons 04-04 and 04-05); laws on companion chatbots (Lesson 04-08); and privacy laws, including rules on automated decisions, in about 20 states.

The governors of California, Colorado and New York said they would keep enforcing their AI laws despite the executive order, and state legislators of both parties said they would keep legislating.

## Should Washington Override the States?

**Preemption** is federal law displacing state law. The arguments for it are that a patchwork of 50 state laws burdens interstate commerce and innovation; that AI is a national, strategic technology, especially in competition with China; and that states may impose rules that conflict.

The arguments against are that states are "laboratories of democracy" that act when Congress does not; that preempting state law without passing a federal one leaves a vacuum, with no rules at all; that consumer protection and civil rights are traditional state roles; and that tying unrelated funding, such as broadband money, to AI policy raises constitutional questions.

Legally, an executive order cannot by itself override state law. Preemption generally requires an act of Congress or a valid federal regulation. The Justice Department's challenges rely on other constitutional arguments, such as limits on state laws that unduly burden interstate commerce, and free speech. Courts are deciding these questions, and the Supreme Court may eventually do so.

## How to Cope as a Practitioner

In a landscape this unsettled, five practices help. Comply with the laws that apply regardless of technology, since they apply now. Track the strictest jurisdiction you operate in, often California, New York, Illinois or, for global firms, the EU. Use voluntary frameworks such as the NIST AI Risk Management Framework, in Lesson 08-04, to build practices you can defend. Document your decisions, since good records protect you under any rules. And expect change: build governance that can adapt.

## Where People Disagree

Should AI be regulated mainly by Washington or by the states? What would you put in a federal AI law?

Was Colorado's replacement prudent, given the litigation and federal pressure, or a capitulation that left people with weaker protection?

And if federal agencies give less priority to disparate-impact enforcement, what should companies do? Lesson 01-09 suggests that what is right did not change when enforcement did.

## Key Terms

- **Preemption:** Federal law displacing state law.
- **Executive order:** A directive from the President to the executive branch, which is not legislation.
- **Automated decision-making technology:** Software that makes or materially influences decisions about people.
- **Laboratories of democracy:** The idea that states can experiment with policies that others may later adopt.
