# 08-03 — Global Governance: UK, China, and International Efforts

**Module:** 8 — Governance, Accountability, and Responsible Deployment
**Estimated time:** 35 minutes

**In this lesson:** AI crosses borders instantly, but rules for it are written country by country. This lesson compares how the UK, China and others regulate AI, describes the attempts at international cooperation, and asks whether coordination on AI is possible at all.

## From Bletchley to Paris and Beyond

In November 2023, the UK hosted the first international AI Safety Summit at Bletchley Park, where codebreakers worked during the Second World War. Twenty-eight countries, including both the United States and China, along with the European Union, signed the Bletchley Declaration, which recognized risks from the most advanced AI and committed to cooperation.

At a follow-up summit in Seoul in May 2024, sixteen companies signed voluntary commitments to publish safety frameworks.

By the time of the next summit, in Paris in February 2025, the mood had changed. Renamed the AI Action Summit, it emphasized innovation and inclusion. More than sixty countries signed a declaration on inclusive and sustainable AI. The United States and the United Kingdom declined to sign, and the U.S. Vice President warned against "excessive regulation."

India hosted a further summit in New Delhi in February 2026.

The arc, from cooperation focused on safety toward divergence focused on competition, shows how fragile international AI governance is.

## How Countries Differ

As of October 2026, the main approaches look like this.

| Jurisdiction | Approach |
|---|---|
| European Union | A comprehensive, risk-based law grounded in fundamental rights (Lesson 08-01) |
| United States | Existing laws applied sector by sector, an innovation-focused federal policy, active states and a fight over preemption (Lesson 08-02) |
| United Kingdom | "Pro-innovation" and based on principles: existing regulators apply five principles, safety, transparency, fairness, accountability and contestability, without a general AI law. Its AI Safety Institute was renamed the AI Security Institute in 2025. Specific laws cover online safety (Lesson 04-04), and rules for police facial recognition are planned (Lesson 02-05). A law for the most advanced AI has been repeatedly signaled but not passed. |
| China | Targeted rules issued one after another: on recommendation algorithms (2022), synthetic media (2023), generative AI (2023) and labeling AI-generated content (2025). Companies must register algorithms with the internet regulator and pass security assessments, and content must reflect "core socialist values." Strong state control sits alongside promotion of the domestic AI industry, including many open-weight releases. |
| South Korea | A basic AI law, passed in January 2025 and effective January 2026, with a risk-based framework for "high-impact" AI |
| Japan | A light-touch law promoting AI, passed in May 2025 |
| Canada | A proposed AI law died when Parliament was suspended in January 2025; a voluntary code covers generative AI |
| Brazil | An AI bill advancing through the legislature |
| African Union | A continental AI strategy (2024) and national strategies in Kenya, Nigeria, Rwanda and elsewhere |

*Statuses change frequently and should be checked before teaching.*

## Different Values, Different Rules

These differences are not only technical. They reflect different priorities. The EU puts fundamental rights and its single market first. The United States emphasizes innovation, competition with China and free expression, and recently deregulation. China prioritizes social stability, state control of information and industrial development. The UK has positioned itself as a pro-innovation hub with expertise in safety.

The same tool can serve different ends. Both China and the EU require AI-generated content to be labeled. In China, labeling supports the state's control over content. In the EU, it supports individuals' ability to judge what they see.

## International Instruments

Several international agreements and bodies now address AI.

| Instrument | What it is | Status |
|---|---|---|
| OECD AI Principles (2019, updated 2024) | Non-binding principles | 47 countries adhere |
| UNESCO Recommendation on the Ethics of AI (2021) | Non-binding recommendation | Adopted by 193 member states |
| G7 Hiroshima Process (2023) | Principles and a code of conduct for advanced AI | Voluntary, with a reporting framework from 2025 |
| Council of Europe Framework Convention on AI (2024) | The first legally binding international AI treaty, on human rights, democracy and the rule of law | Signed by the EU, the UK, the U.S. under the previous administration, and others; ratifications pending |
| UN General Assembly resolutions (2024) | Non-binding | Resolutions led by the U.S. and by China both adopted |
| UN scientific panel and global dialogue on AI (2025) | Advisory | Launched |
| International Network of AI Safety Institutes (2024) | Technical cooperation among government testing bodies | Members include the U.S., UK, EU, Japan, South Korea, Singapore, Canada, France, Kenya and Australia |
| International AI Safety Report (2025, 2026) | A scientific synthesis led by Yoshua Bengio with more than 30 countries | Published |

## Why Coordination Might Work

There are reasons for optimism. Countries share interests in preventing catastrophic misuse, such as AI help with biological weapons or attacks on critical systems. There are precedents: nuclear non-proliferation, the international body that sets aviation safety standards, and the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change, a model for a scientific panel. Government testing institutes already cooperate on technical work. And companies that operate globally want rules that fit together.

## Why It Might Not

There are also strong reasons for skepticism. Strategic competition between the United States and China, including export controls on chips (Lesson 07-03), cuts against cooperation. Values diverge: rights, control and innovation point in different directions. AI is diffuse and dual-use, which makes compliance hard to verify; some propose governing access to computing power, which is concentrated and measurable, as the most verifiable point of control. And political commitments are volatile, as the shift from Bletchley to Paris showed.

## Where People Disagree

Should there be an international agency for AI, modeled on the International Atomic Energy Agency? What would it inspect, and could it work when AI, unlike uranium, can be copied freely?

Is China's targeted, step-by-step approach more effective than the EU's comprehensive one? By what measure?

And how should the Global South be represented in setting global AI rules, given that it has often been left out, as Lesson 06-06 described?

## Check Your Understanding

**1. How did international AI summits change between 2023 and 2025?**

:::answer Answer
They moved from a focus on safety and cooperation at Bletchley to a focus on innovation and inclusion at Paris, where the U.S. and UK declined to sign the declaration.
:::

**2. How does the UK regulate AI?**

:::answer Answer
Through existing regulators applying five principles, without a general AI law, plus specific laws such as the Online Safety Act.
:::

**3. What is notable about the Council of Europe's Framework Convention?**

:::answer Answer
It is the first legally binding international treaty on AI, covering human rights, democracy and the rule of law.
:::

**4. Why is governing computing power proposed as a point of control?**

:::answer Answer
Computing power is concentrated and measurable, which makes compliance easier to verify than for diffuse, easily copied AI models.
:::

## Key Terms

- **Bletchley Declaration:** The 2023 international statement on risks from advanced AI.
- **Framework Convention on AI:** The Council of Europe's binding international AI treaty.
- **AI Security Institute:** The UK government body that tests advanced AI; the U.S. counterpart is the Center for AI Standards and Innovation.
- **Compute governance:** Regulating AI through control of computing resources.
