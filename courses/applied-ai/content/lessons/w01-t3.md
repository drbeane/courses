The AI systems in this course are built on **large language models**, or LLMs. Understanding roughly how they work will help you predict where they succeed and where they fail.

There are other kinds of models, such as ones that generate images or turn speech into text. In this course, "model" means a large language model unless we say otherwise.

## A Next-Token Predictor

An LLM reads and writes text as **tokens**, which are small pieces of text. Long or unusual words are split into several tokens, and in English a token averages about three-quarters of a word. Although a token is not typically a single word, for our purposes it is fine to think of tokens as words and pieces of punctuation. Different models split text into tokens in different ways. The [Tokenization](/appendix/tokenization/) page in the Appendix provides more information about tokenization, and includes a [Tokenizer Explorer](/demos/tokenizer/) demo.

When an LLM processes text, it works one token at a time. Given the text so far, the model calculates a probability for every possible next token, picks one, adds it to the text, and repeats. A full answer is thousands of these predictions in a row.

If you give a model "The capital of France is", the token "Paris" is very likely, and the model will almost always produce it. For an open question with many reasonable answers, the probabilities are spread out, and the model's choice could go many ways.

Here is what the model's prediction might look like for two different input strings. The probabilities shown are made up to show the idea.

| "The capital of France is" | | "My favorite thing about autumn is" | |
|---|---|---|---|
| Paris | 97% | the | 31% |
| a | 1% | how | 14% |
| located | 1% | watching | 9% |
| every other token | 1% | every other token | 46% |

On the left, one answer dominates, and the model gives it nearly every time. On the right, hundreds of continuations are reasonable, and different runs will go in different directions. Notice that the model handles both examples in essentially the same way. It has no separate mode for facts and another for opinions. A question with one right answer and a question with many are both next-token prediction, and the difference is only in how the probabilities are spread.

This is a simple mechanism with surprising results. To predict text well across a huge range of subjects, a model has to absorb a great deal about language, facts, reasoning patterns, and how people explain things. But it is important to remember what it is doing: producing **plausible** continuations. Plausible often coincides with true. Not always.

### Where the Knowledge Lives

A model doesn't store the text it was trained on. The training process involves adjusting billions of numbers inside the model, called **parameters** or weights, until its predictions are good. What the model "knows" is spread across those numbers as patterns. It keeps no list or database of facts that it could look through.

This explains several things you'll see throughout this term. A model can explain a famous novel's plot well and still misquote its first line, because it kept the gist and not the wording. It can't tell you where it learned something. And it has no reliable sense of which things it knows, which is why it can state a wrong detail as smoothly as a right one.

## Trained in Two Stages

Large language models are trained in two main stages.

1. **Pretraining.** The model learns to predict the next token in a string by repeatedly analyzing an enormous amount of text and learning the patterns that are likely to occur. The result has broad knowledge and fluent language, but it is not yet a helpful assistant; given a question, it might continue with more questions.
2. **Tuning into an assistant.** Further training teaches the model to follow instructions, answer helpfully, take on an assistant's manner, and decline some requests. This stage typically relies on people: examples written by people, and people's judgments about which responses are better, a technique called reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF). Some developers also use feedback generated by AI models.

## The Knowledge Cutoff

Pretraining is not a continual process. It has to end at some point, and the text the model learns from ends with it. The date at which that training text stops is called the **knowledge cutoff**. The model knows nothing about events after it unless a tool provides the information: a web search, a file you upload, or a connection to another service. When you ask about anything recent, ask yourself whether the tool you're using actually looked it up or is answering from what it learned during training.

## Why Answers Vary

Models don't always choose the single most likely token. They **sample** from the probabilities, which makes their writing more natural and varied. The consequence is that the same prompt can produce different answers on different runs: different wording, different structure, and sometimes different facts. You saw this in the widget above: the autumn sentence came out differently on nearly every run. The [Next-Token Sampler](/demos/next-token-sampler/) demo shows the same effect on a factual question.

Many tools control how adventurous this sampling is with a setting called **temperature**. At a low temperature, the model strongly favors the most likely tokens, so its answers are more consistent. At a high temperature, less likely tokens get a better chance, so answers are more varied and more likely to go wrong. Chat apps usually set the temperature for you, but you can choose it when you use a model through an API (Week 11). The Next-Token Sampler demo lets you change it and see the effect.

## What an LLM Is Not

To better understand what an LLM is and is not, it helps to compare it with tools you are already familiar with.

| Tool | How it answers | What follows |
|---|---|---|
| **Search engine** | Finds pages that exist and shows them to you | You can see the source. A model alone gives you no source to see. |
| **Database** | Looks up a stored record and returns it exactly | The same question gets the same answer. A model's answer can vary. |
| **Calculator** | Computes by fixed rules | Always right. A model predicts digits, and can slip. |
| **LLM** | Generates likely text from learned patterns | Flexible and fluent, with no built-in check that the result is true |

Modern AI products often combine these. A chat app may run a web search, look something up in your files, or run code for a calculation, and then have the model write the answer from the results. When that happens the product is more reliable than the model alone. Much of this course is about recognizing when a tool was used and when the model was answering from memory.

## Where to Expect Reliability

The mechanism lets you predict, roughly, where a model will do well:

| More reliable | Less reliable |
|---|---|
| Topics written about widely and consistently | Niche, local, or very recent topics |
| Explaining, summarizing, and rewording text you supply | Recalling exact quotations, figures, and citations from memory |
| Common tasks with many examples: emails, outlines, standard code | Unusual tasks with few examples to learn from |
| Questions where many phrasings of the answer would be fine | Questions where one exact value is required |

## What This Means for You

Fluency is not accuracy, and confidence is not accuracy. A model sounds just as sure when it is wrong. Unless it has a tool, it does not look anything up. Your role, throughout this course, is to decide what to ask, describe it well, and check what comes back.

## Key Terms

- **Token**: a piece of a word; the unit models read, write, and are billed in.
- **Large language model (LLM)**: a generative AI system trained on huge amounts of text to predict and produce language.
- **Knowledge cutoff**: the date after which a model's training data stops.
- **Sampling**: choosing the next token from a probability distribution rather than always the top choice.
- **Temperature**: a setting that controls how much randomness a model uses when sampling; low values give more consistent answers and high values give more varied ones.
- **Parameters**: the numbers inside a model that training adjusts; also called weights.
- **Pretraining**: the first stage of training, in which a model learns to predict text.
