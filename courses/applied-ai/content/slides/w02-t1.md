## Description

> How you communicate the task decides most of what you get back.

The model can't read your mind. It only sees what's in its context: your words, your files, and whatever the product adds.

- Everything you know about the task but leave out, it has to guess.
- Its guesses are usually sensible and generic, so a vague prompt gets an average answer.
- This week's skill, **Description**, is about closing that gap.

---

## The New-Coworker Test

Hand your prompt to a capable coworker who:

- has never met you
- hasn't seen your course or your job
- can't ask a follow-up question

Would they have to guess? Then so will the model.

Read your prompt as that coworker would. Each place you'd have to guess is a sentence to add.


---

## Anatomy of a Strong Prompt

- **Goal**: what you want, and why you want it
- **Context**: background the model doesn't have, such as your course, job, or situation
- **Constraints**: length, scope, and anything to avoid
- **Audience**: who will read the result, and what they already know
- **Output format**: bullets, a table, JSON, an email…

Not every prompt needs all five. A quick question may need only the goal.

## Say Why

> Shorten this email.

> Shorten this email, because my manager reads on her phone between meetings.

The reason tells the model what kind of shortening you need: put the request first, cut background, and keep it skimmable on a small screen.

A reason also helps with cases you didn't think to mention, because the model can work out what you'd want.

---

## Before and After

**Weak**
> Summarize this article.

**Strong**
> Summarize this article for a busy department chair deciding whether to fund a pilot. 5 bullets, under 120 words. Lead with the cost and the evidence. Flag anything the article claims without support.

## Techniques That Help

- **Be clear and direct**: say exactly what you want, in plain sentences
- **Structure**: use headings or tags to separate the parts of a long prompt
- **Examples**: show one or two good outputs
- **Role and audience**: "You are reviewing this as…"
- **Ask for uncertainty**: "Say what you're unsure about"
- **Ask for questions first**: let the model interview you before it starts

## Structure: Separate Request From Material

```
Draft three bullets for my cover letter.
Use only experience from the resume.

<job_posting>
...
</job_posting>

<resume>
...
</resume>
```

The tag names aren't special. Any clear label works.

---

## Why Examples Work

- The model continues patterns. Given a few examples, it produces more of the same kind.
- One or two examples show format, tone, and level of detail better than a description of them.
- This is called **in-context learning**: the model learns from the prompt itself, with no retraining.
- It works for anything with a consistent shape: flashcards, product descriptions, data labels, replies to common questions.

---

## Describe It or Show It

**Describe**
> Keep the flashcards short. Make the back an example, not a definition.

**Show**
> Front: Osmosis
> Back: A raisin swells when you soak it in water.

Cautions: it copies your examples' flaws, and one example can be copied too closely. Give two or three that differ.

---

## Advice That No Longer Helps

- Magic phrases, such as "take a deep breath" or offering a tip
- SHOUTING or repeating an instruction to make it stick
- Keyword strings in place of sentences, as if it were a search engine
- Trying to write the perfect first prompt instead of revising

Most of these came from tests on older models. Clear content beats clever wording.
