## A Working Definition of AI

**Artificial intelligence**: a subfield of computer science that is interested in building computer systems that do tasks we would call intelligent if a person did them.

- Typical tasks include understanding language, recognizing images, predicting, and planning.
- The boundary keeps moving: once a task becomes routine, people stop calling it AI.
- So "AI" describes a goal more than a single technology.

## Rules, Then Data

- AI is a broad field that includes expert systems, search algorithms, genetic algorithms, and learned models.
- For its first decades, most AI ran on **rules written by people**. Those systems were brittle and slow to build, because every case had to be anticipated.
- Since about 2010, the dominant approach has been **learning from data**. Learned systems handle messy, unexpected input more robustly, and on many tasks they perform better.
- The tools in this course are all learned systems.

## Nested Terms

- **AI**: the whole field
  - **Machine learning**: systems that learn patterns from data
    - **Deep learning**: machine learning with large neural networks
      - **Generative AI**: creates new text, images, audio, or code
        - **Large language models**: generate text from language

This course covers one narrow slice of AI: the part that matters most today.

In this course, "AI tools" means applications built on large language models.

## AI You Already Use

- Recommendations in social feeds, streaming services, and online stores
- Maps that predict traffic and estimate arrival times
- Phone cameras that sharpen photos, and photo search that finds "dog" or "beach"
- Speech recognition, voice assistants, and automatic captions
- Fraud alerts from your bank, spam filters, and autocomplete

Most of it is narrow: one job each. A spam filter *classifies*. A chat assistant *creates*, across a wide range of tasks.

---

## What "AI" Doesn't Tell You

"Powered by AI" on a product label says very little. Ask instead:

1. What does it actually do? Does it classify, predict, recommend, or generate?
2. What was it built on? Whose data was it trained on, and how current is that data?
3. What happens when it's wrong? Who notices, and who bears the cost?
