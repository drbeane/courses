What to notice

  1. After 10 trials the bars rarely match the probabilities. That is normal. Probability describes the long run and says little about any short run.
  2. After 10,000 trials each proportion sits close to its probability. Estimating a probability by running many trials is the idea behind Monte Carlo sampling.
  3. The sample mean wanders at first, then closes in on the expected value. The expected value is the long-run average, even when it is a number you can never roll, such as 3.5.
  4. For two dice, 7 is the most likely sum because six of the 36 equally likely pairs add up to it. Only one pair gives 2, and only one gives 12.
  5. In the game of chance, the rare 50 and 100 payouts move the sample mean a lot when they land. It takes many more trials to settle than a single die does.
  6. Turn on the seed, clear, and run again. The "random" results repeat exactly, because the computer builds them from the seed with a fixed recipe.
