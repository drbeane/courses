## Calculus and optimization

**Calculus** is a field of mathematics that studies continuous change. It provides a framework for understanding and calculating rates of change, as well as how small quantities accumulate. It has applications in many scientific fields, engineering, and economics.

Some of the most valuable applications of calculus are in the field of **optimization theory**. Suppose you have some quantity that depends on various inputs, and you wish to vary the inputs in an attempt to either maximize or minimize the value of the dependent quantity. This is an example of an **optimization problem**. Calculus provides important tools for addressing certain types of optimization problems.

Optimization problems arise frequently in data science, machine learning, and artificial intelligence. The training processes for most machine learning models and artificial intelligence algorithms are described as optimization problems. In order to have a good grasp of how these algorithms learn, it is important to have at least an intuitive understanding of calculus.

## History of calculus

Calculus has a rich history marked by independent discoveries and rivalries. Mathematicians in ancient Greece applied concepts closely related to calculus to develop an understanding of geometric concepts such as areas, volumes, and tangent lines. However, these ideas were limited in their application and were not developed into a systematic framework.

The true foundations for calculus were developed in the 17th century, primarily by Isaac Newton and Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz. Newton and Leibniz separately and independently developed versions of calculus, leading to a bitter dispute over who had first invented calculus, as well as many accusations of plagiarism. Today, both mathematicians are recognized as independent developers of the field.

The 18th century saw further development by Euler and Lagrange, who expanded calculus to address specific problems in science and engineering. By the 19th century, Cauchy and Weierstrass introduced rigorous definitions based on limits, strengthening the theoretical foundations of the field. In the 20th century, calculus evolved into a highly sophisticated field of mathematics with connections to other areas like analysis, geometry, and topology. Today, calculus is a cornerstone of applied mathematics.

## Branches of calculus

There are two main branches of calculus: **differential calculus** and **integral calculus**. We will discuss the two branches below.

- **Differential calculus** is primarily concerned with studying rates of change using a mathematical tool called the **derivative**. The derivative of a function measures how the function's output changes as its input changes. It provides information about the rate of change of a function at a particular point. Derivatives are used to find the slopes of curves, determine where functions are increasing or decreasing, and solve optimization problems.
- **Integral calculus** is concerned with how small quantities of things accumulate. It studies accumulation through the use of a tool called the **integral**. Integrals have applications in calculating areas, volumes, work done, and various other quantities that involve accumulation.

The two branches of calculus are closely related, and in some sense, derivatives and integrals can be thought of as inverse operations. We will focus our attention on differential calculus in this course. Integral calculus does have applications in data science, machine learning, and artificial intelligence, but they are somewhat niche. Differential calculus, on the other hand, plays a critical role in these fields, primarily because of its role in optimization theory.

## The limit

In some sense, calculus can be thought of as the study of infinity. Calculus provides us with tools for formally working with quantities that are infinitesimally small or infinitely large. The tool that calculus introduces for this purpose is the **limit**. Limits provide a way of discussing the behavior of a function as the inputs get arbitrarily close to, but not equal to, certain values. Limits serve as the foundation for the definitions of both derivatives and integrals, and are an essential component of calculus. Although an understanding of limits is required for a formal study of calculus, an intuitive understanding of calculus concepts can be obtained without the need to study limits. For that reason, we will omit a discussion of limits in this course.
